# Editorial References: Chang'e Flies to the Moon

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- The ancient core of the ten-sun and moon-flight traditions, the popular Peng Meng retelling used for this children's story, the Mid-Autumn connection, and the Jade Rabbit imagery.

## 1. The ancient stories are much shorter

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Huainanzi* passages collected under “Heng'e”
URL: https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?char=%E5%A7%AE%E5%A8%A5&if=gb

- Preserves the *Huainanzi* line that Yi requested the medicine of immortality from the Queen Mother of the West and Heng'e took it and fled to the moon.
- The early passage does not include Peng Meng, a sword at the house, or Chang'e swallowing the medicine to protect it from a thief.

Source: Wikisource, Lu Xun, *A Brief History of Chinese Fiction*, Chapter 2
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E4%B8%AD%E5%9C%8B%E5%B0%8F%E8%AA%AA%E5%8F%B2%E7%95%A5/%E7%AC%AC%E4%BA%8C%E7%AF%87

- Quotes the two relevant *Huainanzi* traditions: ten suns scorching crops and plants before Yi shoots at them, and Yi receiving the immortality medicine from the Queen Mother of the West before Heng'e goes to the moon.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing opening says that the tale has many versions rather than presenting one modern narrative as the only ancient form.
- The story uses the familiar name Chang'e. Older texts also write the name as Heng'e.
- The falling suns, split gift, and Peng Meng episode are treated as parts of the selected popular retelling, not as details proved by the earliest passage.

## 2. The popular Peng Meng version

Source: Hong Kong Education Bureau, “Mid-Autumn Festival: The Legend of Hou Yi and Chang'e”
URL: https://www.edb.gov.hk/attachment/en/curriculum-development/kla/eng-edu/Chi_Fest/Mid-AutumnFestival/PartA.html

- Presents a widely taught modern sequence: ten suns appear together; Hou Yi shoots nine; he receives immortality medicine and asks Chang'e to guard it; his student Pang Meng tries to steal it while Hou Yi is away; Chang'e swallows it first and flies to the moon.
- Connects the retelling with fruit and cakes set out in Chang'e's memory, family gathering, moon viewing, and mooncakes on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month.

Editorial boundary:

- This is the main narrative selected for the children's article because its danger, choice, consequence, and separation form one clear story.
- The English spells the student's name “Peng Meng,” while the Education Bureau page uses “Pang Meng.” These are romanization variants of the same name 蓬蒙.
- The article does not claim that this popular version is the single original form of the legend or the documented historical origin of every Mid-Autumn custom.

## 3. Chang'e and the Jade Rabbit in moon imagery

Source: National Museum of China, “Moon Palace Bronze Mirror”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/csp/202008/t20200826_247444.shtml

- Describes the Chang'e moon-flight legend as widely known and identifies common Tang-dynasty moon-palace imagery: Chang'e, a cassia tree, the white rabbit pounding medicine, and a toad.

Editorial boundary:

- The Jade Rabbit is introduced as a companion from later moon tales, not as a character who took part in Chang'e's decision to swallow the medicine.
- The article mentions the cassia tree and medicine-pounding rabbit because both appear in the final illustration and in established moon-palace imagery.

## 4. Mid-Autumn moon and reunion

Source: Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage, “Hou Yi and the Ten Suns: A Chinese Folktale”
URL: https://www.si.edu/object/yt_5JEUEOmyduA

- Describes Mid-Autumn Festival on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, the round full moon, family gathering, and the moon as an image of reunion and completeness.

Editorial boundary:

- The story shows the connection through a concrete scene of fruit, cakes, moon viewing, lanterns, and mooncakes rather than ending with an abstract lesson about symbolism.

## Image note

The four pictures are modern children's-book illustrations. They are not historical reconstructions. The second and fourth images include a white rabbit as visual foreshadowing of later Jade Rabbit traditions; the rabbit does not take part in the medicine theft scene.
