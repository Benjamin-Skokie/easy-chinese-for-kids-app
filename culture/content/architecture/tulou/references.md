# Tulou — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## The UNESCO property, building forms, and broad functions

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Fujian Tulou”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1113/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The World Heritage property contains 46 buildings constructed between the 15th and 20th centuries across roughly 120 kilometers in southwestern Fujian.
- The selected buildings include circular and square plans, rise several stories around central courtyards, and were organized as housing for large family communities.
- Defensive features in the UNESCO examples include a limited entrance and exterior windows above lower levels; families received vertically divided sets of rooms.
- Tulou combined defensive needs with long-term family residence and responded to the region’s economic and social history.

Editorial boundary:

- “Fujian Tulou” on the World Heritage List refers to 46 selected buildings and clusters, not every earthen communal building in Fujian or neighboring provinces.
- Do not treat the most spectacular round buildings as the only authentic form; UNESCO explicitly includes round and square plans.
- “Up to 800 people” is a capacity reported for particular large examples, not a normal population for every tulou, so it is omitted from the child-facing copy.
- Defense is one documented function, but the article must not reduce centuries of family, agricultural, ceremonial, and domestic life to wartime fortress use.

## Hakka and Minnan traditions, construction, and layout types

Source: Fujian Provincial People’s Government, “老土楼新‘活’法：方圆之间找‘方圆’”
URL: https://fj.gov.cn/zwgk/ztzl/sxzygwzxsgzx/sdjj/wvjj/202502/t20250227_6768939.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Fujian tulou include Hakka, Minnan, and related regional traditions; they are not exclusively Hakka buildings.
- Rammed-earth walls and timber beams and columns work together, with residential space arranged around the perimeter.
- A common Hakka inner-gallery arrangement places kitchens below, grain storage on the second level, and bedrooms above, linked by shared stairs and galleries.
- A Minnan unit arrangement gives each household an independent section entered from the courtyard with its own stair.
- Current adaptive reuse faces unresolved questions about building standards, fire safety, resident participation, and appropriate new uses.

Editorial boundary:

- Present these as two important patterns, not universal floor plans followed by every building.
- Do not describe all residents as Hakka or collapse Minnan and Hakka social histories into one culture.
- Do not claim that every household was spatially identical or socially equal simply because perimeter rooms could be allocated in repeating units.

## Rammed-earth and timber construction

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Earthen Architecture Programme, *Earthen Architecture in Today’s World*
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/document/126549
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Most Fujian tulou combine an outer rammed-earth wall, an interior timber framework, and a base or foundation of stone, blocks, or cobbles.
- Builders used local materials and constructed walls in compacted layers; exact earth mixtures and reinforcement practices varied, and special additives were sometimes rather than always used.

Editorial boundary:

- Explain the load-bearing system as rammed earth working with timber, not as a building made from one solid mass of mud.
- Stone bases are a common moisture-protecting construction feature, not a promise that every building uses the same height or stonework.
- Stories about sticky rice, egg whites, sugar, bamboo, or branches may fit some recorded practices but must not be presented as one universal tulou recipe.

## Architectural variation and household units

Source: Zhiwen Huang et al., “The Geo-Distribution and Spatial Characteristics of Tulou Dwellings in Chaozhou, Guangdong, China,” *Buildings*
URL: https://www.mdpi.com/2075-5309/13/9/2131
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tulou occur in circular, oval, square, octagonal, half-moon, and other external forms.
- Scholarship distinguishes Hakka inner-corridor plans from Minnan unit plans with separate household entrances and stairs.
- Allocation and ownership of household space can make maintaining shared parts complicated rather than automatically producing permanent unity.

Editorial boundary:

- This study includes Chaozhou examples outside the UNESCO Fujian property and is used for comparative typology, not to claim that every form it lists occurs among the 46 UNESCO buildings.
- The article may compare some unit-plan tulou to attached multistory townhouses as a modern explanatory analogy, not as a historical identity.

## Communal life, lineage, privacy, and conflict

Source: Lijun Zhang, *Living in Heritage: Tulou as Vernacular Architecture, Global Asset, and Tourist Destination in Contemporary China*, Chapter 3, Indiana University Press
URL: https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/living-in-heritage-tulou-as-vernacular-architecture-global-asset-and-tourist-destination-in-contemporary-china/section/98d7c305-fd7d-436a-999e-7717c37d188c
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shared central halls could serve ancestor rites, life-cycle ceremonies, festivals, and public events within a lineage community.
- Tulou spaces helped transmit family history and belonging across generations.
- Sharing space also reduced privacy and increased everyday interaction, which could lead to disputes and conflict.
- Lineage organization contained unequal family branches and hierarchical relationships; “community” did not erase social difference.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not invent a perfectly harmonious courtyard or imply that architecture made all residents emotionally close.
- Sensory details in the child-facing imagined visit are general illustrations of plausible domestic activity, not a reconstructed scene assigned to named historical residents.
- The book’s case study centers on Hakka communities in Hongkeng; its findings should not automatically be generalized to every Minnan tulou.

## Rammed-earth durability and maintenance risk

Source: Fujian Provincial Department of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, “关于加强对有价值的非世遗土楼的维护和保护的建议”
URL: https://zjt.fj.gov.cn/xxgk/rdjyhzxtabl/rdjyblqk/202110/t20211009_5701743.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Thousands of valuable non-World-Heritage tulou also require documentation and conservation.
- Resident departure, unsafe electricity use, fire, roof leaks, decay, and lack of maintenance threaten some buildings and can lead to collapse.

Editorial boundary:

- Long survival does not make a rammed-earth building indestructible or guarantee resistance to every earthquake.
- Do not repeat a single universal recipe of earth, sticky rice, and egg whites. Materials and construction methods vary; the central documented method is repeated compaction of prepared earth with timber construction inside.
- Avoid claiming that every tulou had the same wells, grain stores, animal pens, window levels, or ability to remain sealed for months.

## Living heritage, tourism, and fire protection

Source: Fujian Provincial People’s Government, “南靖土楼：要保下来更要活起来”
URL: https://www.fujian.gov.cn/zwgk/ztzl/sxzygwzxsgzx/sdjj/wvjj/202308/t20230817_6231276.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Residents’ traditional indoor cooking and later electrical systems create practical fire and conservation concerns.
- Recent work has altered wiring and cooking systems and reused spaces to improve safety, resident living conditions, and tourism income.

Editorial boundary:

- Preservation is not only repair of exterior walls; it must address residents’ daily needs and the risks of a heavily timbered interior.
- Tourism and adaptive reuse may support livelihoods and conservation, but should not be described as purely beneficial or as preserving daily life unchanged.

Source: Indiana University Press, *Living in Heritage*, Chapter 4
URL: https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/living-in-heritage-tulou-as-vernacular-architecture-global-asset-and-tourist-destination-in-contemporary-china/section/ccb5a124-a753-498f-b3eb-ad4212eac721
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- World Heritage designation and tourism changed how residents, officials, experts, and visitors understood and used tulou spaces.
- Local residents had experienced the buildings as ordinary homes before outsiders framed them as exceptional heritage and “living museums.”
- Tourism management can blur boundaries between residents’ private lives and spaces presented to visitors.

Editorial boundary:

- “Living heritage” should not romanticize residents as museum performers or pretend that remaining in an old building is always their preferred choice.
- The article presents preservation as negotiation among multiple groups, not a simple victory over change.
