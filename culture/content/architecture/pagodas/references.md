# Pagodas — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## From South Asian stupas to Chinese pagodas

Source: Tracy Miller, “Performing Center in a Vertical Rise: Multilevel Pagodas in China's Middle Period,” *Ars Orientalis*
URL: https://quod.lib.umich.edu/a/ars/13441566.0046.005/--performing-center-in-a-vertical-rise-multilevel-pagodas?rgn=main&view=text
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese pagodas developed from the Indian Buddhist stupa/reliquary tradition and retained important relic-enshrining functions.
- In China, stupa forms and meanings changed through a long architectural process that produced taller multilevel structures, varied interiors, and new ritual programs.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not describe the change as one simple moment when an Indian bowl was combined with a Chinese tower.
- Relics remained central, but pagoda contents, ritual use, internal space, and accessibility differed by time and place.

## Yingxian Wooden Pagoda: date, height, and structure

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Wooden Structures of Liao Dynasty—Wooden Pagoda of Yingxian County, Main Hall of Fengguo Monastery of Yixian County” (Tentative List)
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5803/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: China Cultural Heritage Research Institute author, “960岁的应县木塔如何保护”
URL: https://www.lhlzw.gov.cn/sitesources/nysjwjw/page_pc/xcjd/s/article97f14dcf046340b59edfb8349905d693.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Liao pagoda was completed in 1056 and is octagonal, with five visible stories, six eaves, four hidden levels, and nine internal levels altogether.
- Published total height is about 66 to 67 meters: the nomination gives 67.31 meters, while a 2011 heritage-institute measurement gives 65.84 meters.
- The structure has inner and outer column rings, beams, bracket sets, and hidden structural levels.
- It is identified as China's oldest surviving wooden pagoda and one of the tallest surviving ancient wooden pagodas.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a Tentative List nomination, not proof that the site is inscribed as World Heritage.
- Published height varies slightly by measurement method; use a range rather than pretending one decimal is universally settled.
- “Five stories,” “six eaves,” and “nine levels” describe different features and are not contradictory.

## Yingxian conservation and earthquake claims

Source: Li Yuhang et al., “Improving seismic performance for inclined historical building by using external protection structure: Case study of Yingxian wooden pagoda, China,” *Journal of Building Engineering* 96 (2024)
URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352710224019491
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Yingxian has accumulated inclination and damage and remains the subject of monitoring and conservation research.
- Researchers model the pagoda as a whole system and are testing minimum-intervention approaches to limiting further movement and earthquake response.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not credit survival to mortise-and-tenon joints alone or claim that joints simply shift and snap perfectly back after an earthquake.
- Do not describe the structure as undamaged, perfectly preserved, or guaranteed to survive future earthquakes.

## Giant Wild Goose Pagoda and Xuanzang

Source: Shaanxi Provincial Local Records Office, “大雁塔”
URL: https://dfz.shaanxi.gov.cn/zslm/sxsq/msgj/201405/t20140513_2621734.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Xuanzang requested or supervised construction of the first five-story pagoda in 652 to preserve Buddhist scriptures, images, and relics brought from India.
- The first brick-faced earthen-core tower deteriorated; rebuilding under Empress Wu and later repairs changed the structure, and a major restoration took place in 1604.

Editorial boundary:

- Today's seven-story brick tower is not the untouched 652 building.
- Avoid the oversimplified claim that a complete Tang core survives inside a purely Ming shell.
- Xuanzang is a historical monk, traveler, and translator; the Monkey King adventures are later fiction inspired in part by memories of his journey.

## Odd-number interpretations

Source: Hugh D. R. Baker, “Pagoda,” in *Ancestral Images: A Hong Kong Collection*, Oxford University Press
URL: https://academic.oup.com/hong-kong-scholarship-online/book/13496/chapter/166998444
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Commentators commonly connect odd-numbered stories with yang and auspiciousness.
- Some pagodas have staircases while others have no obvious means of climbing.
- Closely-eaved and pavilion-style pagodas can organize exterior eaves and interior space differently; Tracy Miller's study above also documents varied access and interior programs.

Editorial boundary:

- Treat the yin-yang explanation as a cultural interpretation, not a universal building code proven for every pagoda.
- Do not confuse the number of exterior eaves with the number of usable interior floors.
- Do not imply that every pagoda is hollow, climbable, a library, or an observation tower.
