# The Forbidden City: One of the World's Largest Palaces

## Nine Thousand Nine Hundred Ninety-Nine and a Half Rooms

The Forbidden City in Beijing is one of the largest palace groups still standing in the world.

People often say it has 9,999 and a half rooms.

Why not 10,000?

The story says Heaven's palace had 10,000 rooms. The emperor on earth did not dare stand equal with Heaven, so he built half a room fewer.

Where is that "half room"?

People point to Wenyuan Pavilion, the imperial library inside the Forbidden City. On the far west side of the pavilion, there is a very narrow little room, only about half as wide as a normal room.

It was not made that way because the builders ran out of space. It was done on purpose.

The library held books, and books are easy to lose to fire. In old Chinese five-phase thinking, water can control fire. Six rooms was an even number, tied to yin and water. But in the Forbidden City, odd numbers were usually honored more. So the builders squeezed in one tiny "half room." In name, the building could count as an odd number, while still keeping the idea of six water-linked rooms for fire protection.

One little room hides a clever ancient way to balance rules, symbols, and practical worry.

If you slept in a different room of the Forbidden City every night from the day you were born, you would have to sleep for many, many years before you used them all.

## The Journey of the Nanmu Wood

The Forbidden City was ordered by the Yongle Emperor of the Ming dynasty. Work began in 1406 and finished in 1420.

In the 1400s, there were no cranes, no trucks, and no steel-and-concrete building methods. Materials had to be moved by human strength and by water.

The huge golden nanmu wood pillars in the Hall of Supreme Harmony came from deep forests in Sichuan and Yunnan. After a tree was cut down in the mountains, it had to be dragged to a river, tied into a raft, and floated for thousands of kilometers down the Yangtze River. Then it entered the Grand Canal and traveled north toward Tongzhou near Beijing.

From forest to palace worksite, one pillar could easily take two or three years to arrive.

The carved white marble slab in front of the great hall was even harder to move. The largest stone weighed more than 200 tons.

How could workers move it?

They waited for winter. Along the road, they dug wells about every half kilometer. They poured well water onto the road and let it freeze into ice. Then they placed the giant stone on a special sled and had thousands of workers pull it across the ice.

The stone traveled from Fangshan to the Forbidden City, a trip of many kilometers, and took almost a month.

When you walk across the carved stone ramp before the Hall of Supreme Harmony, remember this: under that stone is the memory of thousands of workers breathing white clouds into winter air as they pulled it forward.

## The Treeless Outer Court

The southern half of the Forbidden City is the Outer Court. The Hall of Supreme Harmony, the Hall of Central Harmony, and the Hall of Preserving Harmony stand in a straight line.

When you walk into the great square before the Hall of Supreme Harmony, one thing may surprise you:

there are no trees.

Not because they died.

Because they were not planted.

This was the place where the emperor worked and held great state ceremonies. The view had to be wide open. No tree was allowed to block the powerful line of sight toward the Son of Heaven.

There was another worry too: a tree could hide a person. What if an attacker stood behind it?

So the Outer Court is bare and bright. You see white marble railings, golden paving bricks, yellow glazed roof tiles, and red walls.

Everything is larger than human size. The terrace in front of the Hall of Supreme Harmony rises in three levels. In the middle of each stairway lies a huge carved stone with clouds and dragons.

Stand anywhere in that square and look up. The roof of the hall seems to press down from above. You may feel very small.

That was not a mistake.

The design was meant to make visitors feel the emperor's power.

## The Imperial Garden: The Emperor's Small Patch of Green

The northern half was the Inner Court, where the emperor and his family lived. Next to it was the Imperial Garden.

The garden is not very large. A few ancient cypress trees, rockeries, and pavilions stand close together.

For the emperor, a normal walk might go only from the Palace of Heavenly Purity to a pavilion in the Imperial Garden. He could not simply leave the palace. He could not go out for noodles on the street. He could not ride freely wherever he wanted.

The whole Forbidden City was a golden cage.

The dragon throne in the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the warm rooms of the Palace of Heavenly Purity, the golden bricks underfoot, and the bright roof tiles overhead all look grand.

But seen another way, they were also part of one of the most beautiful cages in the world, a place where it could be hard to breathe freely.

The Forbidden City is no longer private. After 1925, it became the Palace Museum.

Every year, many visitors pass through the Meridian Gate and stand in a square where even officials once could not casually lift their eyes.

A place that was once very hard to enter became a museum open to everyone.

Late in life, Puyi, the last emperor, returned to the Forbidden City. He stood in the Hall of Supreme Harmony and looked at the dragon throne for a long time without saying anything.

Later, he bought a ticket and walked into the home where he had grown up.

It was the first time he had ever needed a ticket to enter the Forbidden City.

## Summary

The Forbidden City has more than 9,000 rooms. The pillars of the Hall of Supreme Harmony floated all the way from the southwest to Beijing. The great Outer Court has no trees at all.

One purpose of its design was to make people feel small the moment they entered. From the Meridian Gate to the Hall of Supreme Harmony, each level rises higher and feels more powerful.

But the palace that was once almost impossible to enter is now open to everyone.
