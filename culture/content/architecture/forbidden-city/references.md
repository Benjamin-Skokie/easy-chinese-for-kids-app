# The Forbidden City — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Current Child-Facing Draft (2026-09-03)

- The story now explains the name *Zǐjìnchéng*, then follows a visitor through gates, work spaces, an ice-road stone haul, the Imperial Garden, and the palace's change into a museum.

## Construction, Scale, and the Meaning of the Site

Source: Palace Museum, “The Forbidden City Is a Museum” (《故宫是座博物馆》)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/journal_detail/246142.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Construction of the Beijing palace began in 1406 and was completed in 1420.
- “Forbidden City” was the name of the Ming-Qing imperial palace, while the Palace Museum was founded in 1925 on the basis of the palace and imperial collections.
- The complex has more than 980 surviving buildings and more than 8,700 rooms or architectural bays, covering roughly 780,000 square meters.
- It is described as the largest and best-preserved surviving ancient wooden palace complex in China.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Imperial Palaces of the Ming and Qing Dynasties in Beijing and Shenyang”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/439/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Beijing Forbidden City was constructed from 1406 to 1420 under the Ming emperor Zhu Di.
- Fourteen Ming and ten Qing emperors used it as the center of imperial power over roughly five centuries.
- Its major spatial features include a central axis, symmetry, an Outer Court in front, an Inner Court behind, and garden areas.
- The site reflects changes and cultural exchanges during both dynasties rather than one frozen building moment.

Editorial boundary:

- The article distinguishes the historic Forbidden City, the former imperial palace commonly called Gugong, and the museum institution founded in 1925.
- “Room” is explained as an architectural bay rather than treated as a bedroom count.
- The popular “9,999 and a half rooms” claim is labeled as legend. It is not substituted for the museum's modern count.
- “Largest” is qualified as a wooden palace complex. The article does not claim the site wins every possible worldwide palace comparison.

## Why It Was Called Zǐjìnchéng

Source: Palace Museum, “Modeling the Palace after the Heavens” (《象天立宫》)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/243823.html
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Source: Palace Museum, “Walking toward the Hall of Supreme Harmony” (《角度——走向太和》)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/forum_detail/100138.html
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Supports:

- In ancient Chinese astronomy, the Purple Forbidden Enclosure, or *Zǐwēiyuán*, was associated with the heavenly ruler and became a model for imagining the earthly emperor's palace.
- The character 紫 (*zǐ*) literally means “purple,” but in this name it carries that older celestial and imperial association.
- 禁 (*jìn*) refers to a restricted or closely guarded place, while 城 (*chéng*) refers to the walled palace city.

Editorial boundary:

- The children's text separates the three characters and explains that 城 (*chéng*) carried a stronger sense of an enclosed, walled place in older Chinese than “city” usually does today.
- The explanation uses “a group of stars” instead of requiring young readers to learn the full structure of traditional Chinese constellations.

## A Palace Rebuilt Across Six Centuries

Source: Palace Museum, “Forbidden City at 600” timeline (故宫六百年)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/subject_600/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Yongle emperor ordered construction in 1406, and the palace was declared complete in 1420.
- The palace continued to be altered: changes were made around Qin'an Hall in 1535; the Palace of Earthly Tranquility was rebuilt in 1655 with features connected to the Qingning Palace in Shenyang and Manchu-Han cultural interaction.
- The Hall of Supreme Harmony burned after lightning in 1679; after more than a decade of material preparation, rebuilding occurred from 1695 to 1697.
- The Palace Museum opened on October 10, 1925; collections were moved south for protection beginning in 1933.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text uses selected examples to show change over time, not a complete construction chronology.
- It does not imply that all existing structures date unchanged to 1420 or that Ming and Qing palace life was identical.

## Ice-Road Transport of Heavy Stones

Source: Jiang Li, Haosheng Chen, and Howard A. Stone, “Ice lubrication for moving heavy stones to the Forbidden City in 15th- and 16th-century China,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 110.50 (2013), 20023–20027
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3864338/
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1309319110
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Erland Schulson, “Sliding heavy stones to the Forbidden City on ice,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 110.50 (2013), 19978–19979
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3864354/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- A historical record describes a roughly 123-metric-ton stone moved about 70 kilometers from the Dashiwo quarry area to the Forbidden City in winter 1557, a journey of 28 days.
- A wooden sledge traveled on an artificial ice road; wells along the route, roughly every half kilometer, supplied water for the surface.
- Engineering analysis finds that a lubricating water film greatly reduced friction and explains why sledging could be more reliable than carts for extremely heavy, valuable stone.
- The research studies stone transport in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and distinguishes archival evidence from modern calculations.

Editorial boundary:

- The detailed 1557 record is more than a century later than the palace's initial completion in 1420. It is not presented as an eyewitness description of the original construction campaign.
- Calculated worker requirements are not reported as a historical headcount. The artwork does not prove crew size, clothing, expressions, shouted commands, weather, or an exact destination scene.
- The retained illustration is labeled as modern imaginative art, not documentary evidence.

## Spatial Order, Court Functions, and Movement

Source: Palace Museum, “Architecture”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/explore/buildings/p/8.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Palace Museum, “Meridian Gate”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/explore/building/236454.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Palace Museum, “West Prosperity Gate”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/explore/building/236501.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Outer Court centers on the three great halls and served as an important area for state ceremonies; the Inner Court centers on the three rear palaces and includes residences and other functions.
- Repeated gates and courtyards follow older ceremonial planning and help create a solemn, hierarchical sequence.
- The central Meridian Gate opening was generally reserved for the emperor, with limited ceremonial exceptions.
- The West Prosperity Gate linked palace circulation to the western imperial parks; Qing emperors and royal family members traveled out through it.

Editorial boundary:

- “Outer Court” and “Inner Court” are useful broad divisions, not a claim that every front building had one function and every rear building another.
- Court access varied by identity, office, time, ceremony, and period. The article gives the spatial principle rather than inventing one universal daily schedule.
- Emperors were constrained by ritual and security but also traveled outside the Forbidden City. The old “golden cage” passage and the claim that an emperor's farthest normal walk ended in the garden were removed.

## Treeless Ceremonial Courtyards and Fire

Source: Palace Museum, “Fire Safety Work at the Palace Museum” (故宫消防工作)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/classify_detail/225371.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Palace Museum, “Meridian Gate”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/explore/building/236454.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Fire is a serious and recurring danger in the predominantly wooden palace complex, with many fires recorded since 1420.
- The gate-courtyard sequence and axial arrangement support formal ceremony and a solemn sense of approach.

Editorial boundary:

- The main ceremonial courtyards are visibly almost treeless, but no reliable Palace Museum or scholarly source located in this review established “preventing assassins from hiding” as the documented design reason.
- Real fire risk does not by itself prove that fire prevention was the sole reason for treeless courts.
- The article discusses open space, symmetry, circulation, and ceremonial order as observable or documented functions. It rejects claims about an architect's unrecorded wish to frighten every visitor.

## From Imperial Palace to Public Museum

Source: Palace Museum, “Hong Kong's Future, the Forbidden City's Mission” lecture record
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/classify_detail/257958.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Palace Museum, “Leave the Palace Immediately” (即日出宫)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240537.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- After Puyi's abdication, the Antiquities Exhibition Office opened in part of the Outer Court in 1914, an early step in transforming palace space into a museum.
- Puyi left the palace in November 1924, after revision of the Articles of Favorable Treatment.
- The Palace Museum was formally founded on October 10, 1925.

Editorial boundary:

- The transition was gradual: imperial rule ended in 1912, an early museum used the Outer Court from 1914, Puyi left in 1924, and the Palace Museum opened in 1925.
- The old story that Puyi later bought his first ticket, stood silently before the throne, or followed a particular emotional routine was omitted because it was not needed and was not verified here from a strong source.
- “Open to the public” does not mean every room is always accessible. Museum access remains shaped by conservation, safety, repair, and visitor management.

## Image Audit

The three retained images are modern illustrations supplied with the article, not historical documents.

- `no-trees-outer-court.webp`: retained as a general image of an open ceremonial axis. Its hall, figures, and proportions must not be treated as an exact reconstruction.
- `giant-stone-on-ice.webp`: retained to explain the documented ice-road method. The pictured crew size, clothing, stone shape, gate, and moment are imaginative.
- `imperial-garden.webp`: retained as a symbolic contrast between garden and wall. It is not an accurate plan of the Imperial Garden and does not document an emperor's routine or solitude.
