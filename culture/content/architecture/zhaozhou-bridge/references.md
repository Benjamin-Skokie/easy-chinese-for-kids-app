# Zhaozhou Bridge — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Date, dimensions, stone ribs, and the precise “oldest” claim

Source: American Society of Civil Engineers, “Zhaozhou Bridge”
URL: https://www.asce.org/about-civil-engineering/history-and-heritage/historic-landmarks/zhaozhou-bridge
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The bridge was completed around 605, has a main span of about 37 meters, and uses a low segmental arch.
- The main arch is formed from twenty-eight parallel stone ribs joined with iron connectors.
- ASCE recognizes it as the oldest surviving open-spandrel arch bridge and an International Historic Civil Engineering Landmark.

Editorial boundary:

- Use the precise combined description “oldest known surviving open-spandrel segmental stone arch bridge,” not “the world's first arch bridge.”
- Earlier segmental arches and open structures existed in other materials and settings; survival is not the same as first invention.
- Do not reduce the structural system to friction and one keystone; the whole arch, iron connections, abutments, and foundations participate.

## Open spandrels and repeated repairs

Source: China Daily government portal, “Zhaozhou Bridge”
URL: https://govt.chinadaily.com.cn/s/201908/16/WS5d561547498ebcb1905787b3/zhaozhou-bridge.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The segmental main arch creates a gentler roadway than a comparable semicircular arch.
- Four small arches remove fill weight, add high-water flow area, and transmit load toward the main arch.
- The bridge was repaired repeatedly; the earliest recorded renovation came about two centuries after completion.
- The major mid-twentieth-century overhaul used cement pressure grouting, and ancient pieces recovered or removed were preserved in museums.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not say the small arches make the bridge flood-proof or that one feature alone explains its long survival.
- Do not say the bridge stood intact and unchanged for fourteen centuries.

## Li Chun and the limits of attribution

Source: Hebei Provincial Administration for Market Regulation, “长虹卧波”
URL: https://scjg.hebei.gov.cn/info/55876
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A Tang inscription by Zhang Jiazhen identifies the bridge as the work or trace of the Sui craftsman Li Chun.
- Li Chun's name survives, but biographical information about him is otherwise missing.

Editorial boundary:

- Credit the bridge to Li Chun on the basis of this later early inscription; do not imply that a Sui blueprint or first-person account survives.
- Do not invent Li Chun's childhood, motives, dialogue, or exact working process.

## What survived and what was rebuilt

Source: *Sanlian Lifeweek*, “赵州桥：千年石拱桥的‘旧’与‘新’”
URL: https://mdev.lifeweek.com.cn/h5/article/detail?artId=87069
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Liang Sicheng's 1930s investigation documented three collapsed eastern arch ribs, rebuilt western ribs, cracks, and outward movement.
- The bridge uses a segmental, open-spandrel form; the smaller arches reduce fill weight and provide extra space during floods.
- Historical repairs and the large modern intervention mean that the visible bridge contains work from different periods.

Editorial boundary:

- Today's monument retains its Sui design and substantial historic masonry but also includes replacement stone, reconstructed portions, cement grout, and later deck and railing work.
- Conservation accounts differ in their evaluation of the 1950s work; present it as both a rescue from serious damage and a source of later debate.

## Legends of Lu Ban, Zhang Guolao, and Lord Chai

Source: Zhao County People's Government, “省级非物质文化遗产——赵州桥的传说”
URL: https://www.zhaoxian.gov.cn/columns/1d7018c5-9c6d-4833-a55d-e7bd402b1d92/202306/25/5352361f-d69b-4775-bd9e-475c03b3ce7f.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Stories of Lu Ban, Zhang Guolao, Lord Chai, the donkey, cart, and bridge marks form a long-lived local folklore tradition celebrated in *Little Cowherd*.
- The legends use imagination and exaggeration to express admiration for the bridge and its builders.

Editorial boundary:

- Treat the supernatural crossing and Lu Ban's construction as folklore, not bridge history.
- The cultural value and age of a story do not prove that a physical mark is ancient.

## The modern hoofprint and wheel rut

Source: *Liaoning Daily*, “讲解文博古迹切莫信口雌黄,” 28 July 2023
URL: https://epaper.lnd.com.cn/lnrbepaper/pc/att/202307/28/4415dab6-2261-4752-babf-6a9953583e8b.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- During the 1950s restoration, old deck stones were replaced and a rut and hoofprint were deliberately carved into the new surface to echo the legend.
- The restoration choice is now discussed as an example of the tension between popular storytelling, visitor interpretation, and material authenticity.

Editorial boundary:

- The modern marks do not prove an ancient event or traffic-control device; remove the unsupported “ancient speed bump” theory.
- The image used by this story is also a modern illustration, not a photograph of an old surface.
