## City Plan, Population, Zhuque Avenue, and Ward Counts

Source: Mitsuo Inoue, “Ancient Chinese capital models—Measurement system in urban planning,” *Journal of Asian Architecture and Building Engineering* 16.3 (2017)
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5743849/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Victor Cunrui Xiong, *Sui-Tang Chang'an: A Study in the Urban History of Medieval China*, University of Michigan Center for Chinese Studies, 2000; city and market evidence summarized in Yōhei Kakinuma, “Money, Markets, and Merchants,” *The Cambridge Economic History of China*
URL: https://yohey.w.waseda.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/shizhao202211.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Heng Chye Kiang, “Visualizing Everyday Life in the City: A Categorization System for Residential Wards in Tang Chang’an,” *Journal of the Society of Architectural Historians* 73.1 (2014)
URL: https://online.ucpress.edu/jsah/article-abstract/73/1/91/60443/Visualizing-Everyday-Life-in-the-CityA
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Archaeological survey and reconstruction place the outer city at roughly 9.7 by 8.65 kilometers, an enclosed area of about 84 square kilometers.
- Common reconstructions estimate Zhuque Avenue at roughly 150–155 meters wide.
- A frequently cited peak population is roughly one million, but modern estimates and the boundaries they count differ substantially.
- Reconstruction plans commonly show 108 residential wards plus the two major markets; sources that count market or other blocks can give 110 or describe the units differently.
- Residential-ward reconstructions depend on archaeological remains, textual records, social status, and modeled population density rather than a complete surviving plan of daily life.

Editorial boundary:

- Area, avenue width, ward totals, and population are labeled archaeological or scholarly estimates, not exact observations from one year.
- “One million” is not presented as a census count of people living within the walls, and Chang'an is not given an unsupported fixed rank as the world's single largest city.
- The old invented morning walk, named merchant, camel cargo, Japanese copyist, monk, weather, bells, and universal multilingual soundscape were removed.
- The illustration is not treated as an archaeological reconstruction of the road surface, buildings, traffic, or social interaction.

## Wards, Markets, and Government Control

Source: Yōhei Kakinuma, “Money, Markets, and Merchants,” *The Cambridge Economic History of China*, 2022 author manuscript
URL: https://yohey.w.waseda.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/shizhao202211.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Kim Young Hwan, “Markets and Everyday Life in Tang Chang'an,” *Journal of Asian Historical Studies* 130 (2015)
URL: https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch.kci?sere_id=001261&arti_id=ART002022505
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: *Tang Huiyao* 唐會要, juan 86, market regulations, Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=489197&if=gb&remap=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chang'an's East and West Markets were large enclosed official markets with market administrations, regulated hours, and oversight of trade and measures.
- Archaeology at the West Market has identified road surfaces, drainage, bridge remains, wells, pottery, and coins.
- The West Market's western location and nearby Central Asian merchant communities make it especially relevant to overland exchange, but both markets sold varied goods and served diplomatic as well as economic functions.
- Commerce also occurred in residential wards, and shops and night markets increasingly challenged older spatial and time controls in the middle and late Tang.
- Ward gates, market gates, curfews, and official signals made Chang'an a managed and policed capital, not a modern always-open shopping district.

Editorial boundary:

- The East Market is not called purely domestic, and the West Market is not called an eighth-century global mall.
- No invented list of goods is placed on one fictional vendor's stall. Imported objects can show exchange without identifying the ethnicity of a seller.
- Market regulations changed and were not necessarily enforced uniformly throughout all 289 years of the dynasty.

## Sogdians, Persians, Other Communities, and Tang Categories

URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/tang-cosmopolitanism-towards-a-critical-and-holistic-approach/16309DB09C12BB94DDEB656A0A8F7540
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0026749X23000318
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Internationalism in the Tang Dynasty (618–907)”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/internationalism-in-the-tang-dynasty-618-906
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Sogdians were an Iranian-speaking Central Asian people whose trade networks connected many Silk Road cities; a settled community lived and worked in Chang'an.
- Persians and other Central Asians appeared as merchants, artisans, religious specialists, soldiers, officials, performers, and residents; Korean, Japanese, South Asian, and other people also entered the capital in varied roles.
- Some migrants became registered Tang subjects, while envoys and people from outside Tang jurisdiction occupied different legal positions.
- Tang government used household registration, passports/travel documents, naturalization, surveillance, trade rules, and legal distinctions to manage mobile populations.
- Restrictions applied to some marriages, contact with envoys, and private foreign trade. Foreign or non-Han people could also arrive through captivity, slavery, or human tribute rather than voluntary migration.
- Modern “cosmopolitanism” is a scholarly framework that can illuminate exchange while hiding hierarchy, coercion, and Tang categories such as *hu* or *huawairen*.

Editorial boundary:

- The story names communities only where texts, archaeology, epitaphs, or institutional history support their presence.
- It avoids using hair, eye, or skin color as a shortcut for Sogdian, Persian, Central Asian, Korean, Japanese, or other identity.
- “Foreign” is not treated as one stable identity: a person could be a visitor, envoy, immigrant, captive, enslaved person, or registered Tang subject.
- Tang Chang'an is neither dismissed as closed nor celebrated as uniformly tolerant and equal.

## Japanese Missions to Tang China

Source: Douglas Fuqua, “The Japanese Missions to Tang China, 7th–9th Centuries,” Japan Society, November 6, 2023
URL: https://japansociety.org/news/the-japanese-missions-to-tang-china-7th-9th-centuries/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Japan Tourism Agency, “Japanese Missions to Tang China”
URL: https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R4-00662.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Saeki Arikiyo, translated by Joshua A. Fogel, *The Last Japanese Embassy to Tang China, 836–839*, Chinese University of Hong Kong Press, 2026
URL: https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-last-japanese-embassy-to-tang-china-836-839/9789882373839/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Japanese court missions to Tang China began in 630 and continued into the ninth century; counts differ according to whether appointments, canceled missions, departures, arrivals, or successful returns are included.
- Delegations could include envoys, officials, interpreters, students, monks, doctors, musicians, craftspeople, sailors, and attendants, with diplomatic and learning goals.
- Voyages could involve several ships and were genuinely dangerous, but outcomes varied greatly by mission and by vessel.
- The 777 and 836–839 missions document major losses and complicated returns; other ships and missions arrived safely.
- Tang institutions and culture influenced Japan, while Nara and Heian-kyō adapted Sui-Tang planning rather than exactly copying Chang'an.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not repeat a universal “one-third died” statistic. A cross-mission aggregate, even if calculated, would not describe the risk of every voyage.
- “Japan was the most serious student” is removed as an unsupported ranking.
- Envoys are not depicted kneeling in a Chang'an street copying laws, architecture, or city plans; the artwork is labeled imaginative.
- Transmission is described as selection and adaptation by varied people, not one-way copying of a complete Chinese package.

## The Jingjiao / Xi'an Stele

Source: British Museum, *Silk Roads* exhibition large-print guide, section “The first Christian missionaries”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/silk-roads/large-print-guide
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: British Museum, collection record, “Rubbing of the stele commemorating Christianity in China”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1989-1004-0-1-4
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Michael Keevak, *The Story of a Stele: China's Nestorian Monument and Its Reception in the West, 1625–1916*, University of Hong Kong Press, 2008
URL: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1xwfv5
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Asian Civilisations Museum, Singapore, “Rubbing of the stele commemorating Christianity in China”
URL: https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1388517
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The limestone monument was erected in Chang'an in 781 and is now housed at the Xi'an Beilin Museum.
- It carries a long Chinese inscription and Syriac names and records the early history of the Church of the East tradition in Tang China, called Jingjiao in the inscription.
- The text says Aluoben led the first recorded mission in 635 and describes imperial patronage.
- The stele was rediscovered in or around 1625 after centuries out of view.
- Its headpiece includes a cross and decorative imagery; the actual monument does not have a central Buddha figure like the retained illustration.

Editorial boundary:

- “Nestorian” is retained only as a common historical label; “Church of the East” is preferred because the older label can flatten theology and community identity.
- “Daqin” is not translated simply as modern Rome, Syria, or Persia. Its geographic meaning shifted in Chinese sources, and Aluoben's precise birthplace and route are not invented.
- The stele is a community's commemorative account, not proof of mass conversion, equal numbers of religions, permanent tolerance, or a harmonious everyday meeting among all faiths.
- The story does not state exactly when or why the stele was buried, because that remains less certain than its 1625 rediscovery.

## Policy Change and Religious Suppression

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Internationalism in the Tang Dynasty (618–907)”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/internationalism-in-the-tang-dynasty-618-906
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: British Museum, *Silk Roads* exhibition large-print guide
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/silk-roads/large-print-guide
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang policies toward religious institutions changed over time rather than following one continuous tolerant program.
- From 842 to 846, especially in the Huichang suppression centered on 845, the state attacked Buddhism on a vast scale and also suppressed Church of the East Christianity, Manichaeism, and Zoroastrianism.
- Cross-cultural exchange coexisted with conquest, tribute, political crisis, regulation, and persecution.

Editorial boundary:

- An emperor's patronage of one mission or monastery is not generalized into religious freedom for all people and all Tang reigns.
- The account introduces persecution without graphic detail and does not claim that every community disappeared immediately or permanently in 845.

## Image Audit

All four retained images are modern imagined scenes. None is a photograph, excavation record, or evidence for a particular encounter.

- `4-1-many-worlds-avenue.webp`: combines a camel caravan, vaguely western/Central Asian clothing, an official, and a Buddhist monk on a paved commercial-looking street. Architecture, paving, traffic, identities, cargo, and friendly proximity are unverified; it is not a reconstruction of Zhuque Avenue.
- `4-2-west-market.webp`: combines glass, carpets, spices, nuts, invented merchants and shoppers, and mosque-like domes/minaret forms. The single stall and skyline are unsupported and could wrongly turn West Market into a modern multicultural bazaar.
- `4-3-japanese-envoys-study.webp`: the figures' identities cannot be read from clothing, and no source supports this exact map-copying lesson. The image is kept only to introduce the varied work of embassy members and the difference between influence and copying.
- `4-4-nestorian-stele-scene.webp`: materially inaccurate. The illustrated stone shows a large Buddhist figure and tree-like decoration; the real 781 stele is text-dense, with Chinese inscription, Syriac names, and a cross in its headpiece. The caption and story explicitly turn the error into a source-checking exercise.
