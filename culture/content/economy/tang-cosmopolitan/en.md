# Tang Chang'an: Distant Worlds, City Rules

## Before We Walk Down That Avenue

Suppose someone hands you a reconstructed plan of Tang Chang'an. A huge rectangle of walls surrounds a grid of north-south and east-west roads. Walled residential wards fill many of the blocks. Down the middle, Zhuque Avenue runs from the southern gate toward the government and palace districts like a thick line drawn on the ground. Can the plan tell you exactly who walked there each morning, what languages they spoke, or whether they got along? No. A city plan shows how space was arranged. It cannot create a busy street scene by itself.

Archaeological surveys and modern reconstructions usually give the walled city an area of about 32 square miles, or 84 square kilometers, and estimate Zhuque Avenue at roughly 490 feet, or around 150 meters, across. Scholars have suggested populations ranging from several hundred thousand to around one million at the city's height, depending on the date, the area counted, and how incomplete records are interpreted. Many plans show 108 residential wards plus the East and West Markets; other counting systems include market or administrative blocks and reach a different total. These numbers help us picture the scale, but they are estimates—not a perfect snapshot of one day.

Broad streets did not mean unlimited freedom. Wards and official markets had walls, gates, and schedules, while movement at night was restricted. The positions of the palace, government offices, and ordinary neighborhoods also displayed rank and power. Chang'an did gather people from far away, but it was first of all a carefully governed imperial capital.

## Two Markets, Not Two Separate Worlds

Chang'an had two large official marketplaces, the East Market and the West Market. A simple story sometimes turns the East Market into a place for “Chinese goods only” and the West Market into an ancient international mall, as if an invisible national border separated them. The evidence is messier. Both markets had government offices that supervised trade, checked measures, settled market matters, and controlled opening hours. Business did not stay forever behind those two sets of walls either. By the middle and late Tang, shops inside residential wards and night markets increasingly pushed against the old rules.

The West Market stood near gates and routes leading west. Archaeologists have uncovered roads, drains, a bridge, wells, pottery, and coins there, so it is an especially useful place for studying long-distance exchange. [[Sogdians]], an Iranian-speaking people from Central Asia, were famous for trade networks linking many cities. Persians and other Central Asians also lived in Chang'an as merchants, artisans, officials, and residents. Yet a glass vessel or foreign coin might have passed through many hands before reaching the capital. It cannot tell us who owned one imagined stall. The East Market also sold many kinds of goods and received foreign objects and envoys. The two markets were not simply “domestic” and “foreign.”

## Who Counted as a Visitor?

Epitaphs, laws, religious inscriptions, documents, and works of art preserve traces of many different people: Sogdian and Persian merchants and families; Central Asian artisans, soldiers, musicians, and dancers; envoys, students, and migrants from kingdoms on the Korean Peninsula; Japanese missions; and Buddhist monks from South and Central Asia. Some stayed briefly. Others raised families in Chang'an or became registered Tang subjects. Still others did not arrive freely at all. They were prisoners of war, people sent as human tribute, or enslaved people who had been bought and sold. Calling everyone a “foreign tourist” would erase these very different lives.

Tang writers often grouped varied peoples under broad labels such as *hu*. Those labels did not work like clear modern nationalities. When scholars today call Chang'an “cosmopolitan,” they are offering a modern interpretation of its wide connections. The word helps us notice exchange, but it can falsely suggest equality. Tang authorities registered households, checked travel documents, supervised embassies and trade, and treated people differently according to status, official rank, gender, and whether the state counted them as Tang subjects. Some marriages across political borders and some private contacts were restricted. The city's splendor also depended on labor, tribute, warfare, and movements of people who were not free.

## The Japanese Missions Did Not Pack Up One Box of Answers

From 630 into the ninth century, Japan's court appointed a series of official missions to Tang China. Modern totals differ because scholars may count missions that were planned, launched, reached China, or completed the round trip. A mission did not consist of one kind of “student.” It could include ambassadors, deputy envoys, officials, interpreters, long-term students, monks, doctors, musicians, craftspeople, sailors, and attendants. They performed diplomacy and studied government, Buddhism, writing, calendars, medicine, and the arts. Some returned after a few years; others stayed much longer. Abe no Nakamaro entered Tang government service, while the monk Kūkai traveled with the 804 mission, studied Buddhism, and returned to Japan.

The sea voyage was truly dangerous. Storms, navigation errors, groundings, and wrecks caused deaths and long drifts on different missions. But one disaster cannot become a rule that “one-third always died.” Some ships arrived safely. Some disappeared. Some survivors washed up far from their planned destination and eventually found a way home. Risk changed with the route, season, ship, and particular voyage.

The Japanese capitals of Nara and Heian-kyō—later Kyoto—were influenced by the axes and grids of Sui-Tang capital planning. Yet Japanese planners adjusted those ideas to local land, politics, and scale. Exchange did not mean photocopying a complete plan of Chang'an and carrying home a box of answers. Travelers selected, understood, and reshaped what they encountered; then people at home helped turn it into something new.

## A Picture That Gets the Stele Wrong

Look at this illustration and you might think the Jingjiao Stele had a large Buddha carved in its center. The real monument is nothing like that. Erected in Chang'an in 781, it is covered mainly with a long Chinese inscription and includes names written in Syriac. A cross appears among the decorations at the top. The text looks back on the Christian tradition that Tang writers called Jingjiao, part of the Church of the East. It says that a religious leader named Aluoben reached Chang'an in 635, and it praises support from several emperors. The stele is precious evidence, but it is also a community's monument to its own history—not a census of the entire city.

The stone disappeared from view for centuries and was unearthed near Xi'an in 1625. It proves that Church of the East communities were active in Tang China and shows how religious ideas and images were expressed in a Chinese setting. It does not prove that most residents converted, that every religion had equal standing, or that harmony lasted forever. Government policy changed. Around 845, during the Huichang suppression, the court attacked Buddhist institutions on a huge scale and also suppressed Jingjiao, Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and other foreign religions. Religious buildings, people, and property suffered.

That makes the inaccurate picture useful in one unexpected way: it reminds us to compare a beautiful story with the surviving evidence. Chang'an held translation, trade, and learning—but also gates, ranks, bans, and persecution. We need both sides to see the city more clearly.

## “Worlds Met” Is the Beginning, Not the Ending

Return to the city plan one last time. Roads carried people from far away into Chang'an. Ward walls and government systems shaped where they could stay, trade, and move. Objects, music, religions, and knowledge crossed boundaries, but people could not always cross boundaries of law and status just as freely.

Calling Chang'an “cosmopolitan” should not be the end of the story or a prize for being an “open city.” It should begin a better set of questions: Who came? Why? Who welcomed them, who supervised them, and whose voice is missing from the record? Those questions turn the squares on a map into a real, complicated city worth knowing.
