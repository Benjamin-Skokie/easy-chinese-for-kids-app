# Ancient Markets — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Rural periodic markets and market schedules

Source: G. William Skinner, “Marketing and Social Structure in Rural China, Part I,” *The Journal of Asian Studies* 24, no. 1 (1964)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abs/marketing-and-social-structure-in-rural-china-part-i/20071250B7BC9B70B1C47DF11B608FCA
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Rural markets formed networks connecting villages with market towns and higher levels of trade.
- Market schedules varied by region and could follow ten-day or twelve-day cycles; traveling traders benefited from non-conflicting schedules.
- Small producers, including household producers, could combine production with selling on periodic market days.

Editorial boundary:

- Skinner's core evidence is strongest for late imperial and twentieth-century rural China. It supports explaining how periodic market systems worked, but not assigning one schedule to all periods called “ancient China.”
- Do not universalize the old claim that markets met every five or ten days. Use “selected days according to local custom” and identify the later evidence base.

## Tang Chang'an's plan and commercial change

Source: Victor Cunrui Xiong, *Sui-Tang Chang'an (A.D. 582–904)*, Australian National University doctoral thesis (1988)
URL: https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/5f37976b-3855-461d-a83f-f6908a991c67
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chang'an contained a highly developed ward system, official marketplaces, market administration, and a substantial foreign population.
- Commercial activity gradually spilled from designated markets into residential areas as the ward-and-market system loosened over time.
- The city should be treated as changing across the Tang period, not as a static plan that worked identically for three centuries.

Editorial boundary:

- The planned city and later commercial expansion are a sequence, not a simple Tang-versus-Song switch.
- Avoid exact population superlatives in the child-facing story because estimates and comparison standards vary.

## East and West Markets and social geography

Source: Seo Tatsuhiko, “The Official Residences of Chang'an in the Tang Dynasty,” *Tōyōshi Kenkyū* 55, no. 2 (1996), abstract indexed by CiNii
URL: https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1390572174787716224?lang=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Official and elite residences became concentrated in parts of eastern Chang'an, while popular commerce was especially important in the western city.
- The two markets helped anchor different urban zones, but both belonged to a more complicated commercial and social geography.

Editorial boundary:

- The evidence does not justify a strict “East Market for the rich, West Market for ordinary people” rule.
- Describe tendencies in surrounding residence and commerce, not exclusive admission or an exact department-store division of goods.

## Market measures and transaction rules

Source: Shaanxi Provincial Local Records Office, provincial commercial-history volume (section on Tang market administration and weights and measures)
URL: https://dfz.shaanxi.gov.cn/zslm/fzzlk/xbsxsz/szdylpdf/201404/P020240923630937181754.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang rules required merchants in Chang'an's two markets to have capacity measures, scales, and rulers checked by the responsible government office.
- Market administration addressed prices, fair measurement, and the conduct of sellers.

Editorial boundary:

- This supports a general explanation of supervised measures. It does not prove that every transaction was fair or that enforcement was identical across the empire.
- The article avoids unexplained unit names and exact penalties because those would add difficulty without advancing the story.

Source: Tsinghua China Law Review, “Tang Dynasty's Regulation of Contracts”
URL: https://www.tsinghuachinalawreview.law.tsinghua.edu.cn/UploadFiles/2022-11-18/vpnmy1k8vbb9ezft.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang law recognized and regulated contracts, with particular certification requirements for some sales such as livestock.
- Excavated documents from Turfan give evidence that written agreements and credit existed in medieval China.

Editorial boundary:

- Contracts and credit were tools available in particular transactions, not proof that every market purchase used paperwork or borrowed money.
- The child-facing article therefore says these devices “could” support trust and does not assign one procedure to all dynasties.

## Sogdian and other long-distance merchants

Source: University of Washington, Silk Road Seattle, “Xi'an/Chang'an” and “Foreign Traders and Caravan Animals”
URL: https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/cities/china/xian/xian.html
URL: https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/exhibit/tang/camel_trader.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang Chang'an was a major center for the circulation of western products and fashions.
- Central and West Asian merchants, especially Sogdians, were important participants in overland trade.
- Tomb figurines of merchants and caravan animals are archaeological evidence for how Tang patrons represented long-distance trade.

Editorial boundary:

- Tomb figurines are funerary objects, not portraits of a documented market encounter.
- Do not reduce foreign merchants to blond hair, blue eyes, broken Chinese, or one costume. Do not assign an imported object to an exact stall without a source for that stall.

## Northern Song Kaifeng night markets

Source: Meng Yuanlao, *Dongjing Menghua Lu* (Dreams of Splendor of the Eastern Capital), volume 3, modern English translation hosted by Deltoi
URL: https://deltoi.com/zh/meng-yuanlao/dongjing-menghua-lu/chapter-3
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The memoir describes Kaifeng night markets lasting until the third watch and reopening at the fifth watch.
- It distinguishes especially busy districts, where activity might continue through the night, from the city as a whole.
- The account records dense mixtures of shops, food stalls, services, brokers, and people working late on public or private business.

Editorial boundary:

- Meng wrote a retrospective literary account of a particular capital. Do not turn it into a claim that every Song market was open all night or that hours never varied.
- The long lists of foods are evidence of variety, not permission to build a fictional stall-by-stall scene.

## Southern Song Hangzhou street trade and calls

Source: Wu Zimu, *Mengliang Lu*, volume 13, Chinese text hosted by Wikisource
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E5%A4%A2%E7%B2%B1%E9%8C%84/%E5%8D%B713
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The text records early markets, some all-night food shops, mobile repair workers, door-to-door services, and sellers calling out goods in Hangzhou's streets.
- Named shops and detailed lists show that fixed shops and mobile street trade coexisted.

Editorial boundary:

- It supports the existence of advertising calls, not the exact modern-sounding cries invented in the old draft.
- It describes thirteenth-century Hangzhou, not all Song cities and not all imperial periods.

## Commercial taxes and official involvement in Song trade

Source: *History of the Song Dynasty*, volume 139, “Commercial Taxes, Market Exchange, Equal Transport, Mutual Trade and Shipping Regulations,” Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=606585&if=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Song commercial life involved tax offices, transport rules, official depots, exemptions, quotas, and controls over particular goods and routes.
- Market expansion did not mean the disappearance of government regulation.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not present the Song night market as a rule-free modern mall.
- The institutional details changed by reign, region, and commodity; the child-facing article therefore uses general language rather than one universal tax rate or procedure.

## *Along the River During the Qingming Festival* as visual evidence

Source: National Palace Museum, “A Special Exhibition of Paintings on ‘Up the River During Qingming’ in the Museum Collection”
URL: https://www.npm.gov.tw/Exhibition-Content.aspx?l=2&sno=04007005
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zhang Zeduan's early-twelfth-century composition depicts activity along the Bian River and in Northern Song Kaifeng.
- Many later versions and reinterpretations of the subject exist and include features from their own periods.

Editorial boundary:

- The scroll is a composed work of genre painting, not a photograph or complete inventory of every occupation in Kaifeng.
- Never use details from a later Ming or Qing version as automatic evidence for the Northern Song original.

Source: Palace Museum journal article, “张择端《清明上河图》卷新探”
URL: https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2018/06/05/u5b167df5352f9.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Scholarly analysis compares painted shop names and urban details with textual records such as *Dongjing Menghua Lu*.
- Some apparently precise shop details may be artistic construction rather than direct matches to surviving city records.

Editorial boundary:

- Use the painting to ask how movement, commerce, and urban space are represented; do not tell children they can “look directly into” an unfiltered historical reality.

## Image policy for this article

The four existing images are retained to avoid unnecessary art replacement. Each is a modern composite illustration rather than documentary evidence.

- `1-1-market-day.webp` combines generic rural goods, clothing, buildings, and animals without identifying a period or region.
- `1-3-east-west-market.webp` exaggerates a clean division between elaborately dressed Chinese shoppers and visually stereotyped foreign traders; it must not be treated as a reconstruction of either Tang market.
- `1-2-night-market.webp` offers an orderly lantern-lit scene but does not document one recorded street or date.
- `1-4-street-sellers.webp` invents the seller, goods, and pictorial shop sign; the caption explicitly separates the documented practice of street calling from these designed details.
