# Tea — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Early physical evidence and the limits of “first tea” claims

Source: Houyuan Lu et al., “Earliest tea as evidence for one branch of the Silk Road across the Tibetan Plateau,” *Scientific Reports* 6 (2016)
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4704058/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Biomolecular and botanical analysis identified tea remains from the Han Yangling complex near Xi'an, dated to about 2,100 years ago.
- Tea residue from a tomb in western Tibet dated to roughly the second or third century CE.
- These finds show early elite consumption and movement of tea toward the Tibetan Plateau.

Editorial boundary:

- These are among the earliest surviving physical traces identified as tea, not proof of the first tea ever grown or drunk.
- The Han evidence comes from an imperial burial setting and cannot by itself describe everyday consumption across society.

## Shennong and early-tea tradition

Source: James A. Benn, “The Early History of Tea: Myth and Reality,” in *Tea in China: A Religious and Cultural History*, University of Hawai'i Press (2015)
URL: https://academic.oup.com/hawaii-scholarship-online/book/29980/chapter-abstract/255043105
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shennong belongs to the mythology and later cultural history surrounding tea.
- Early-tea history requires separating retrospective origin stories from datable textual and material evidence.

Editorial boundary:

- Preserve the Shennong story as cultural memory, not a biography of a verified discoverer or an event dated to 2737 BCE.
- Do not claim that tea medically cured poisoning.

## Southwest tea forests and community knowledge

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Cultural Landscape of Old Tea Forests of the Jingmai Mountain in Pu'er”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1665/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Blang and Dai communities have developed and maintained Jingmai Mountain's understorey tea-forest system for more than a thousand years.
- Villages, forest ecology, farming knowledge, belief, and community governance work together in this cultural landscape.

Editorial boundary:

- Jingmai is strong evidence for a living southwest Chinese tea tradition beginning around the tenth century; it is not proof that one Jingmai community invented all tea.
- Tea-plant domestication and origins are scientifically complex. Avoid a single-tree, single-person, or tightly bounded national-origin claim.

Source: Xia et al., “Domestication Origin and Breeding History of the Tea Plant (*Camellia sinensis*) in China and India,” *Frontiers in Plant Science* 8 (2017)
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5788969/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tea includes genetically varied plant groups and a long, complex history of domestication and breeding.
- China and India contain distinct tea landraces, cultivars, and maternal lineages relevant to domestication history.

Editorial boundary:

- “All true tea is *Camellia sinensis*” does not mean every tea plant is genetically identical.
- Avoid turning a debated domestication history into a simple claim that one modern country alone contains a proven single point of origin.

## Lu Yu, Tang preparation, and diverse tastes

Source: Lu Yu, *The Classic of Tea*, English translation by Brother Anthony of Taizé
URL: https://anthony.sogang.ac.kr/ClassicofTeaLuYu.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The text describes tea plants, production, equipment, utensils, preparation, drinking, historical notices, and producing regions.
- Lu Yu includes a salt container and directs the preparer to season the water at the first boil.
- He criticizes tea repeatedly boiled with scallion, ginger, dates, orange peel, cornel, mint, and other additions.

Editorial boundary:

- Lu Yu records and argues for one refined method; his complaints are evidence that other Tang preparations existed.
- Do not turn his instructions into the recipe followed by every Tang person or claim that salt, scallion, and ginger were one universal mixture.
- “The first book devoted to tea” is commonly said, but the article avoids an absolute first claim and instead identifies the work, date, scope, and influence.

## Song powdered tea and Japanese transformation

Source: Kyoto National Museum, “Special Exhibition Chanoyu: Tea in the Cultural Life of Kyoto”
URL: https://www.kyohaku.go.jp/eng/exhibitions/special/chanoyu_2022/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tea plants and tea-drinking customs reached Japan through contact with Tang China.
- In the late Heian period, monks who traveled to Song China carried back methods of grinding tea and mixing or whisking it with hot water.
- Japanese temple, warrior, court, merchant, and craft cultures transformed tea practice across later centuries.

Editorial boundary:

- Song powdered-tea practice contributed to Japanese tea history, but Japanese *chanoyu* was not a complete Song invention transferred unchanged.
- Do not describe the tool in the current illustration as a verified Song tea whisk or call it by the Japanese word *chasen* in a Song scene.

Source: Tokyo National Museum, “Chanoyu — The Arts of Tea Ceremony, The Essence of Japan”
URL: https://www.tnm.jp/modules/r_free_page/index.php?id=1828&lang=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Monks traveling between Song China and Japan were important carriers of powdered-tea methods.
- The practice later spread among warriors, aristocrats, and townspeople and developed within Japan.

Editorial boundary:

- Present this as exchange followed by local development, not a one-directional “China invented the Japanese tea ceremony” claim.

## Six categories, plant diversity, oxidation, and fermentation

Source: Chuan Chen, “Tea classification in theory and practice,” *Journal d'agriculture traditionnelle et de botanique appliquée* 28 (1981), translated from a 1979 Chinese article
URL: https://www.persee.fr/doc/jatba_0183-5173_1981_num_28_3_3850
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The influential six-category system was formulated as a modern theoretical classification based on processing and tea quality.
- Green, yellow, dark, white, black/red, and oolong categories reached their modern six-part organization over historical time.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not imply that Tang or Song consumers used today's six-category shelf system.
- The six classes are useful organizing tools, not timeless natural boxes that erase variety, cultivar, place, and craft.

Source: Zhou et al., “Tea Harvesting and Processing Techniques and Its Effect on Phytochemical Profile and Final Quality of Black Tea: A Review,” *Foods* 12 (2023)
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10743253/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- During the processing stage conventionally called fermentation, black tea consumes oxygen and leaf enzymes oxidize catechins, producing compounds important to color and flavor.
- Time, temperature, humidity, oxygen, and handling affect the finished tea.

Editorial boundary:

- Explain that tea-industry Chinese often calls the stage *fa jiao* or fermentation, while its central chemistry in black/red tea is enzymatic oxidation.
- Do not confuse this with microbial fermentation in bread or yogurt; post-fermented dark teas involve microbes more directly.

## Wuyi black tea and the accident story

Source: Kunbing Xiao, “Shifting Wind, Eternal Earth: Lapsang Souchong, a Foreign *Fengtu* Comes Home,” book chapter based on fieldwork in Tongmu village
URL: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403170715_Chapter_9_Shifting_Wind_Eternal_Earth_Lapsang_Souchong_a_Foreign_Fengtu_Comes_Home
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tongmu oral tradition attributes Lapsang Souchong to soldiers delaying tea work and villagers rapidly drying leaves with local pinewood.
- The author explicitly notes that no historical record confirms the story and treats it as community lore.
- Production in Tongmu developed in relation to changing domestic and overseas markets.

Editorial boundary:

- Label the soldier story as local oral tradition, not a documented Ming battle or the proven single invention of all black tea.
- Do not invent named farmers, dialogue, exact troop identity, or a first tasting scene.

## Tea-horse trade as a changing borderland network

Source: Yudru Tsomu, “The Sino-Tibetan Borderlands,” *Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Asian History* (2023)
URL: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61799/chapter/546340776
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tea-horse commerce moved through several routes and trading centers, including Dartsedo, Dechen, Gyaltang, Zungchu, and Tangkar.
- Governments, merchants, monasteries, local communities, innkeepers, wholesalers, smugglers, and power holders all participated.
- Horses and wool moved alongside tea, while later commerce increasingly included musk, deer antlers, hides, yak tails, medicinal plants, and other goods.
- The importance of the horse trade declined, but borderland trade continued and changed form.

Editorial boundary:

- “Tea Horse Road” is a modern umbrella name for a network, not one fixed road used unchanged for a thousand years.
- Official tea-for-warhorse exchange was one major policy and trade pattern, not the only activity and not a simple exchange between two uniform ethnic groups.
- Do not justify tea demand with the medical stereotype that Tibetans needed it to digest an exclusively meat-and-dairy diet.

## European tea, opium, plantations, and labor

Source: British Museum, “The tea-rific history of Victorian afternoon tea”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/tea-rific-history-victorian-afternoon-tea
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- European tea was initially expensive and heavily taxed, while the East India Company controlled British trade.
- Tea plantations in Assam and Ceylon were part of British colonial expansion and reduced dependence on Chinese imports.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not tell a cheerful “Europe loved tea, so other countries planted it” story that hides colonial control of land and commerce.
- Afternoon tea is one part of British tea culture, not the reason the entire global tea economy developed.

Source: British Museum, “Opium and the British Empire,” collection context for an opium pipe
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1945-1017-243
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Britain's large purchases of Chinese tea and other goods created a trade imbalance.
- British interests cultivated opium in India and shipped it for illegal sale in China.

Editorial boundary:

- Tea demand was an important part of the trade background to the Opium Wars, but not their only cause.
- Keep the child-facing explanation direct without suggesting that ordinary British tea drinkers personally planned the war.

Source: Rana P. Behal, “Labour Resistance in Indenture Plantations in the Assam Valley,” in *Beyond Indenture*, Cambridge University Press (2023)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/beyond-indenture/labour-resistance-in-indenture-plantations-in-the-assam-valley/859E887CB54EF15079FBEBD21349E59B
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Colonial Assam tea estates depended on a large migrant workforce recruited and transported under indenture contracts.
- British capital, plantation control, global markets, and restricted labor mobility were central to the industry.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not describe plantation expansion only as successful farming or a solution to insufficient Chinese supply.
- Explain indenture as contracts that sharply limited workers' freedom; avoid graphic detail not needed for this age group.
