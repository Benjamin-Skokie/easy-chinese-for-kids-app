# Editorial References: Silk Roads

Accessed: 2026-08-21

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Editorial boundary:

- These notes support fact-checking, chronology, terminology, and image decisions; they are not learner-facing copy.

## 1. UNESCO Silk Roads Programme — “About the Silk Roads”

URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-roads/about-silk-roads?hub=196704

Supports:

- The Silk Roads were changing networks of land and maritime routes, not one continuous road.
- For most of their history, the ancient routes did not share one name.
- Ferdinand von Richthofen introduced the German term *Die Seidenstrasse* in the nineteenth century; the plural now helps describe a broader network.
- Goods, languages, religions, arts, crafts, technologies, and knowledge moved through cities and communities along the routes.

Editorial boundary:

- “Silk Road” is presented as a useful modern organizing label, not the name printed on an ancient map.
- The article does not turn “East” and “West” into two sealed civilizations exchanging packages across an empty middle.

## 2. UNESCO Silk Roads Programme — “Dunhuang”

URL: https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/dunhuang

Supports:

- Dunhuang was an inhabited oasis, garrison, market, religious center, and logistical crossroads near routes around the Taklamakan Desert.
- Documents from the Mogao library cave record locally produced and traded goods, many languages, and communities following several religions.
- The list of Dunhuang goods belongs mainly to the first millennium CE and changed over time.

Editorial boundary:

- Oasis residents are presented as producers, translators, hosts, officials, believers, and market participants—not scenery between China and Europe.
- Tea, ceramics, printing, and the surviving Dunhuang documents are not projected backward into Zhang Qian's second-century-BCE mission.

## 3. Sima Qian, *Shiji*, chapter 123 — Zhang Qian account, academic translation assembled by the University of Washington Silk Road project

URL: https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/hantxt1.html

Supports:

- The Han emperor sent Zhang Qian to seek the Yuezhi as allies against the Xiongnu.
- Zhang Qian left with more than one hundred people, was held by the Xiongnu for about ten years, escaped and continued west, failed to persuade the Yuezhi to return and fight, was detained again, and returned after roughly thirteen years with very few of the original party.
- His report included places he visited and farther places known through information from others.

Editorial boundary:

- Zhang Qian's journey is framed first as diplomacy and intelligence gathering, not a trade expedition.
- The account is an early written historical source, not a modern travel diary. It does not verify the scroll map, clothing, or exact group arrangement shown in the illustration.
- The article omits the old wording that he was “forced to marry” because the early account's family detail does not support that simple modern claim.

## 4. Høisæter — “Polities and nomads: the emergence of the Silk Road exchange in the Tarim Basin region during late prehistory (2000–400 BCE)” (*Bulletin of SOAS*)

URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/polities-and-nomads-the-emergence-of-the-silk-road-exchange-in-the-tarim-basin-region-during-late-prehistory-2000400-bce/6311521432E4F0AFA783860A3C51C0BA

Supports:

- Archaeology shows exchange and increasing interregional connections in the Tarim Basin before Zhang Qian's mission.
- Local oasis communities and mobile pastoral groups shaped networks and depended on exchange; ancient empires did not create all routes from nothing.

Editorial boundary:

- Zhang Qian's report and later Han policies could intensify official contact without making him the inventor or “opener” of all Silk Roads.
- Pre-Han exchange is not described as the same network, volume, or political system found in later centuries.

## 5. British Museum — *Silk Roads* large-print guide and Sogdian background

URLs:

- https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/silk-roads/silk-roads-large-print-guide
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/who-were-sogdians

Supports:

- During the Tang period, Sogdians were important long-distance traders and immigrant residents linking China with Central Asia and the Iranian world.
- Archaeological objects and texts show movement of gold and silver vessels, glass, aromatics, grape wine, textiles, religious objects, and artistic designs.
- Local craftspeople adopted and transformed foreign forms rather than merely copying them.

Editorial boundary:

- Tang-period goods and fashions are not inserted into Han-period scenes.
- The market illustration is a modern composite and does not prove that every pictured person, garment, vessel, textile, and grape appeared together at one place and time.
- Some merchants crossed great distances; the article avoids both “everyone went end to end” and “no merchant ever traveled the whole route.”

## 6. Jason Neelis / University of Washington Silk Road project — Buddhism on the Silk Routes

URLs:

- https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1163/j.ctt1w8h16r.12
- https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/exhibit/religion/buddhism/buddhism_silkroad.html

Supports:

- Buddhism spread through interconnected overland and maritime networks involving South Asia, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, and China.
- Parthian, Sogdian, Indian, Kuchean, Khotanese, and Chinese monks, translators, and communities took part across many centuries.
- Central Asian Buddhist centers developed distinctive traditions and were not passive stepping-stones.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not say Buddhism entered China once, on one road, in one shipment.
- Translation is treated as cooperative intellectual work, not as mechanically replacing each foreign word with one Chinese word.

## 7. Huili and Yancong, *A Biography of the Tripitaka Master of the Great Ci'en Monastery* (Li Rongxi translation)

URL: https://kinhsach.chuakhainguyen.com/Books/T50n2053/A%20Biography%20of%20the%20Tripitaka%20Master%20of%20the%20Great%20Cien%20Monastery%20of%20the%20Great%20Tang%20Dynasty.pdf

Supports:

- Xuanzang traveled from 629 to 645 to study Buddhist teachings and collect Sanskrit texts.
- The traditional biography counts 657 Sanskrit texts brought back; it also records assistance from rulers, religious communities, guides, escorts, attendants, and others during different stages.
- After returning, Xuanzang translated with monastic and lay collaborators; the biography's modern introduction counts 74 works in 1,335 fascicles.

Editorial boundary:

- “657” is explicitly attributed to the traditional biographical count, not presented as a modern physical inventory.
- The article says sixteen or seventeen years because inclusive traditional counting and simple year subtraction differ.
- Xuanzang may have been alone on some stretches, but the full journey and translation project depended on many people. He studied and translated; he did not merely “fetch books.”

## 8. Encyclopaedia Iranica — “Paper and Papermaking”

URL: https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/paper-and-papermaking/

Supports:

- A later tradition links captured Chinese papermakers after the 751 Battle of Talas with papermaking in Samarkand.
- Scholars have challenged the one-battle story because paper and possibly papermaking were present in Central Asia before the battle.
- Papermaking spread through Islamic lands and changed materials and methods, including greater use of rag fibers.

Editorial boundary:

- The Battle of Talas is treated as one debated episode, not a switch that released a guarded secret to the world.
- Technology transfer is described as gradual work by artisans, markets, states, and religious communities adapting methods to local materials.

## 9. Liu et al. — “Exchanges of economic plants along the land Silk Road” (*BMC Plant Biology*)

URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12870-022-04022-9

Supports:

- Archaeology, genetics, and historical evidence can be combined to study crop movement across Eurasia.
- Several grains moved before the conventionally defined Silk Roads, while fruits, vegetables, forage crops, and other plants moved in different periods and directions.
- Evidence and dates remain incomplete for some proposed crop histories.

Editorial boundary:

- Grapes and alfalfa are described as part of larger crop movements, not as two objects proven to have been carried personally by Zhang Qian.
- Recipes cannot be traced in the same way as plant remains. The unsupported claim that China learned noodles from the Western Regions is removed, as is any one-way China-to-Europe noodle origin story.
