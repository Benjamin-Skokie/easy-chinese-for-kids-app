# Ancient Money — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Cowrie shells in early China

Source: Mikael Fauvelle, *Shell Money*, Cambridge University Press, 2024
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/shell-money/59F5D361C6CA7C596A8C168C84A960D0
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Archaeological and written evidence connects cowrie shells with value, payments, and rewards in early China.
- Cowries were portable and durable, and imitation cowries were made from materials including bone, stone, and bronze.

Editorial boundary:

- Cowries also served ornamental and prestige purposes; the story does not claim that every excavated shell was used as money.

## The changing forms of ancient Chinese currency

Source: National Museum of China, “Ancient Chinese Money”
URL: https://en.chnmuseum.cn/exhibition/exhibition_series/special_exhibitions/202102/t20210210_248989.html
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Chinese currency developed from early cowries through knife-, spade-, and ring-shaped money to round coins with square holes and later paper money.
- Round square-holed coins remained a characteristic form for about two thousand years.

Editorial boundary:

- The sequence is used to explain changing forms, not to imply that every older form vanished as soon as a new one appeared.

## Qin coinage standardization

Source: National Museum of China, “‘Ban Liang’ Bronze Coin and Stone Coin Mold” (《半两青铜钱及石钱范》)
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/hb/202010/t20201013_247850.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- After unifying the states in 221 BCE, Qin Shihuang brought coinage under a more unified system.
- The round, square-holed *bànliǎng* became the empire's principal bronze coin.

Editorial boundary:

- Standardization did not make every older coin or non-coin form of payment disappear immediately from every locality.

## Warring States money and the square-holed coin tradition

Source: British Museum, *Money Gallery Guide*, “The Beginnings of Coinage—China”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/Money_Gallery_LPG_2020_Room_68.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Warring States money included knife-shaped, spade-shaped, and round forms.
- After Qin unification, the *bànliǎng* became an imperial standard, and the round coin with a square hole became a characteristic East Asian form for about two thousand years.

Editorial boundary:

- The tool-shaped pieces were purpose-made money rather than ordinary working knives or spades.

## Song dynasty jiaozi

Source: National Museum of China, “Song Dynasty Jiaozi and the Issuance of Paper Currency” (《宋代交子与纸币发行》)
URL: https://en.chnmuseum.cn/Portals/0/web/exhibition/exhibitions/161120Chines-Epic/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Private Sichuan *jiāozǐ* began as certificates for deposited metal money and gradually circulated as currency.
- The Northern Song government established the Yizhou Jiaozi Office in 1023 and subsequently issued official *jiāozǐ*.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing account calls *jiāozǐ* the earliest known government-issued circulating paper currency, rather than the first paper receipt or financial document of any kind.

## Paper currency after jiaozi

Source: National Museum of China, “Paper Money in Marco Polo's Eyes” (《马可·波罗眼中的点金术》)
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/yj/xscg/xslw/201812/t20181224_33235.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Early Sichuan *jiāozǐ* developed from merchant-issued certificates into paper currency.
- Government management helped turn paper money into an official currency used by later dynasties.

Editorial boundary:

- This source provides the longer history of Chinese paper money; the story itself stops with the early development of *jiāozǐ*.
