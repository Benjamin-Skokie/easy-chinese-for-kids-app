# Ancient Money — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Cowries, Imitations, and the Limits of the Evidence

Source: Mikael Fauvelle, *Shell Money*, Cambridge University Press, 2024, section 4.2 “China”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/shell-money/59F5D361C6CA7C596A8C168C84A960D0
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: National Museum of China, “Bronze shell coins”
URL: https://en.chnmuseum.cn/collections_577/collection_highlights_608/ancient_currencies_613/202008/t20200817_247008.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Natural cowries and imitations made from bone, stone, clay, shell, lead, and bronze occur in early Chinese archaeological contexts.
- Oracle-bone and bronze inscriptions record cowries as royal rewards and, especially in Zhou inscriptions, in transactions involving land, goods, and services.
- Cowries were counted in units called *peng*; excavated paired strings support, but do not make universal, an association of one *peng* with ten shells.
- Uninscribed bronze shell imitations are known from Shang and Western Zhou contexts.

Editorial boundary:

- The story does not call every excavated cowrie a coin. Cowries could also be ornaments, prestige goods, offerings, or burial objects, and the extent and character of monetary use remain debated.
- “The first money in China” is avoided. The evidence supports importance in early systems, not one clean starting date or one uniform national use.
- Imitation cowries are not presented as proof that natural shells ran out or as one automatic evolutionary step toward later coins.

## The Shell Component in Chinese Characters

Source: Ministry of Education, Taiwan, *Stroke Order of Standard Chinese Characters*, entry and radical note for 買
URL: https://stroke-order.learningweb.moe.edu.tw/dictMean.jsp?ID=36023&la=0
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: MIT OpenCourseWare, Julian K. Wheatley, *Learning Chinese: A Foundation Course in Mandarin*, Character Lesson 2
URL: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-21g-003-learning-chinese-a-foundation-course-in-mandarin-spring-2011/97915adbcca432319d8793d2530e5768_MITRES_21G_003S11_char02.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- 貝/贝 is an originally pictorial shell graph and serves as the dictionary radical or semantic component in many characters connected with goods, wealth, and transactions.
- 買/买, 賣/卖, 貴/贵, and other modern forms preserve that visible component in traditional or simplified form to different degrees.

Editorial boundary:

- The article treats the shared component as linguistic evidence of a broad old association, not as a complete etymology of every whole character.
- It does not invent a literal market scene to explain the creation of each graph. Character formation, borrowing, sound components, and later graphic change require character-by-character study.

## Regional Coin Systems and Simultaneous Forms of Payment

Source: British Museum, “Money Gallery,” Room 68 large-print guide, section “The beginnings of coinage—China”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/Money_Gallery_LPG_2020_Room_68.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: British Museum, collection record, “coin; spade-money,” Warring States period
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1913-1011-17
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Armin Selbitschka, “Managing a Multicurrency System in Tang China: The View from the Centre,” *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* 24.2 (2014)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/managing-a-multicurrency-system-in-tang-china-the-view-from-the-centre/2FF20E5E8D46BAD698FE2C545C78DEDC
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Academy of Chinese Studies, “Currency in Ancient China”
URL: https://chiculture.org.hk/en/china-five-thousand-years/2041
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Knife money was associated especially with northern and northeastern Warring States, while spade and round coin systems occurred in other regions; round coins existed before Qin imperial unification.
- Tool-shaped coins retained visual features of tools but were purpose-made cast money with inscriptions, not ordinary working knives and spades.
- Coins, textiles, grain, labor obligations, and other payments could coexist; even later governments managed multicurrency systems rather than one medium replacing every other one.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text gives broad regional patterns rather than a rigid coin map. Coin finds, political borders, and circulation zones do not line up perfectly.
- “Bronze” and “copper alloy” are used carefully. “Copper money” is retained only as a familiar category name, not a promise of chemically pure copper.
- The story is organized by discoveries, not a shells-to-tools-to-round-coins evolutionary ladder.

## Qin and Han Standardization

Source: National Museum of China, “半两青铜钱及石钱范” (“Banliang bronze coins and stone coin mold”)
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/hb/202010/t20201013_247850.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: National Museum of China, “中国古代钱币展” (“Ancient Chinese Currency Exhibition”)
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/202102gdqianbi/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qin-state *banliang* coinage predates the empire; after the 221 BCE conquest, Qin promoted the round, square-holed form and weight-denominated system across the empire and regulated production.
- Surviving Qin *banliang* coins vary in actual weight, despite the standardized name and legal system.
- Han first issued *wuzhu* coins in 118 BCE; in 113 BCE, the central government prohibited commanderies and kingdoms from casting and centralized production.
- The long history of later changes prevents “Qin standardized it once and nothing changed for two thousand years” from being accurate.

Editorial boundary:

- Standardization is described as a state project that made common accounting easier, not an instantaneous erasure of every older coin or commodity payment.
- The later “round Heaven, square Earth” interpretation is labeled as a cultural explanation, not established proof of the original design intention.
- The practical uses of the hole—production handling, stringing, and counting—do not imply that every historical string always contained exactly 1,000 coins.

## From Iron Coins to Private Jiaozi

Source: National Office for Philosophy and Social Sciences, He Ping, “成都‘交子’产生的信任机制与‘官方交子’发行的历史意义” (“The Trust Mechanism Behind Chengdu Jiaozi and the Historical Significance of Official Jiaozi”), September 11, 2023
URL: https://www.nopss.gov.cn/n1/2023/0911/c219544-40075019.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Peking University Institute of Financial Law, “论‘交子’作为纸币的法律特征” (“On the Legal Characteristics of Jiaozi as Paper Currency”)
URL: https://www.finlaw.pku.edu.cn/jrfy/gk/2006_jrfy/2006nzd71j/37finlaw240178.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Jón Steinsson, *Money and Banking*, University of California, Berkeley, historical discussion of Song money
URL: https://eml.berkeley.edu/~jsteinsson/teaching/moneybanking.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Sichuan's shortage of bronze coin and reliance on low-value, cumbersome iron coins formed the setting for private exchange bills around the turn of the eleventh century.
- Customers deposited coin with issuing shops and received marked notes; the notes were initially redeemable merchant liabilities and began circulating because trusted issuers promised payment.
- A group of wealthy issuing houses played an important role, but failures to redeem, fraud, and disputes weakened private issues.
- In 1023 the government established the Yizhou Jiaozi Office and took official control of issuance, with coin reserves, redemption, printing controls, records, and issue rules supporting credibility.

Editorial boundary:

- Private *jiaozi* are first explained as deposit receipts or promissory instruments that acquired monetary use, not as fully formed state fiat currency from the beginning.
- Tang “flying cash” is distinguished as a remittance/withdrawal mechanism, not casually labeled the first circulating banknote.
- The artwork's blank paper is not described as a real *jiaozi*, and the burden of iron coins is explained without repeating a dramatic unsourced exact load.

## Government Paper Money, Regional Limits, and Inflation

Source: Columbia University, Asia for Educators, “The Song Dynasty in China: The Commercial Revolution—Paper Money”
URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/songdynasty-module/econ-rev-money.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Xueying Guan and Charles K. S. Ma, “The rise and fall of paper money in Yuan China, 1260–1368,” *Economic History Review* 77.3 (2024)
URL: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ehr.13305
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13305
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Hans-Ulrich Vogel, “‘Mothers and children’: discourses on paper money during the Song period,” in *Money as God?*, Cambridge University Press, 2014
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/money-as-god/mothers-and-children-discourses-on-paper-money-during-the-song-period/2DEB0B622DC9531ACBEDDF146280690A
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107337695.013
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Niv Horesh, “‘Cannot Be Fed on When Starving’: An Analysis of the Economic Thought Surrounding China’s Earlier Use of Paper Money,” *Journal of the History of Economic Thought* 35.3 (2013)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-history-of-economic-thought/article/abs/cannot-be-fed-on-when-starving-an-analysis-of-the-economic-thought-surrounding-chinas-earlier-use-of-paper-money/BE2E64B5E9C8C341D1318683151AB5DD
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S1053837213000239
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Northern Song authorities took over the Sichuan certificate system in the 1020s, producing what is commonly identified as the earliest known government-issued circulating paper currency.
- Early *jiaozi* had defined issue periods and regional reach and were initially convertible; later paper instruments and policies differed.
- Paper money coexisted with metal coin and commodities rather than replacing them everywhere.
- State backing did not eliminate monetary risk: excessive issue, weak redemption, war, and fiscal pressure could produce depreciation and inflation in later Song, Yuan, and Ming experiments.

Editorial boundary:

- “World's first” is restricted to the historically specific category of known government-issued circulating paper currency. It is not applied to all paper claims, receipts, remittance orders, or credit documents.
- Deposit certificate, merchant promissory note, convertible government note, and unconvertible state fiat are treated as related but distinct arrangements.
- The story gives no false promise that paper was always lighter, safer, more modern, or more successful than coin; its usefulness depended on institutional trust and rules.

## Image Audit

All four retained images are modern teaching illustrations, not photographs, archaeological records, or reconstructions of documented events.

- `2-1-shell-money.webp`: retains a useful exchange question, but the child, merchant, grain payment, clothing, buildings, and direct handful-of-shells transaction are invented. The caption does not claim that any cowrie always bought a bowl of rice.
- `2-3-many-coin-shapes.webp`: retains the comparison among shells and coin shapes. Scale is inconsistent; the forms and inscriptions are stylized; the “early round coins” resemble later square-holed cash and must not be treated as accurate Warring States specimens.
- `2-2-coins-and-paper-note.webp`: retains the contrast between metal and paper, but the coins resemble much later cash and the printed note is a generic fantasy. They do not form one excavated group and the paper is not a verified Song *jiaozi*.
- `2-4-heavy-iron-coins.webp`: retains the physical-burden idea, but exaggerates a traveler covered in strings of coins. Its people, dress, shop, blank paper, and coin arrangement do not reconstruct a historical transaction or prove the exact weight of a payment.
