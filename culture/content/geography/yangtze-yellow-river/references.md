# Editorial References: Yangtze and Yellow River

Accessed: 2026-08-21

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Editorial boundary:

- These notes support fact-checking and image decisions; they are not learner-facing copy.

## 1. National Development and Reform Commission — “Where Is Qinghai?”

URL: https://www.ndrc.gov.cn/wsdwhfz/202106/t20210609_1282865.html

Supports:

- The Yangtze headwater region is on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, its main stem crosses eleven provincial-level regions, and it enters the East China Sea east of Chongming Island.
- This source gives a measured length of 6,387 kilometers; the child text rounds this to “about 6,400 kilometers.”
- It also lists the Yellow River as approximately 5,464 kilometers long.

Editorial boundaries:

- Other authoritative and educational sources often round the Yangtze to “more than 6,300 kilometers.” The article does not present one measurement as eternally exact.
- River length is not used as a ranking game; the learning point is how source, mouth, map scale, and changing channels affect a measurement.

## 2. U.S. National Park Service — “Measuring the Mississippi”

URL: https://www.nps.gov/miss/learn/nature/measmiss.htm

Supports:

- River measurements are snapshots because channels, bends, banks, and deltas change.
- A river may shorten by cutting across a bend or lengthen when sediment builds new land at its mouth.

Editorial boundaries:

- This source explains general measurement principles; it is not used for Chinese river-length figures.
- The child text also makes explicit that surveyors must choose a headwater and mouth and measure along the main channel rather than using straight-line distance.

## 3. Yellow River Conservancy Commission — “Analysis of Yellow River Flood Control”

URL: https://www.yrcc.gov.cn/zwzc/zcjd/202403/t20240312_426969.html

Supports:

- The Yellow River rises on the northern side of the Bayan Har Mountains, flows through nine provincial-level regions, reaches the Bohai Sea in Shandong, and has a main-stem length of 5,464 kilometers.
- Much of the floodwater and sediment comes from different parts of the basin; the Loess Plateau tributary region is the main source of floodwater and sediment in the middle reaches.
- The present lower channel is generally four to six meters above the land behind the levees and locally more than ten meters above it.
- High sediment loads, mobile alluvial channels, levee breaches, and course changes are linked processes. Modern management combines reservoirs, levees, flood-storage areas, water-and-sediment regulation, watershed treatment, and monitoring.

Editorial boundaries:

- The traditional saying about “half a bowl of sediment” is omitted because it is not a measurement.
- Floods are not blamed on an “angry” river. The article separates physical processes from choices about levees, land use, floodplains, settlement, and reservoir operation.
- The image is labeled as an exaggerated cutaway and cannot be used to estimate an actual levee or riverbed height.

## 4. U.S. Geological Survey — *Geographical Overview of the Three Gorges Dam and Reservoir, China: Geologic Hazards and Environmental Impacts*

URL: https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2008/1241/

Supports:

- The Three Gorges project provides hydropower, assists flood management, and improves navigation.
- The project displaced human and animal populations, altered bank stability, affected archaeological investigation, and had other environmental and social consequences.

Editorial boundaries:

- The source predates some later monitoring, so the article uses it for the broad benefit-and-cost framework, not for a claim that every predicted impact occurred exactly as forecast.
- The story does not reduce the dam to either a heroic monument or a single environmental disaster.
- Li Bai's “a thousand *li* in one day” is treated as literary experience, not a measured speed or a description of today's reservoir conditions.

## 5. Liu et al. — “Impacts of Land-Use Changes on the Lakes across the Yangtze Floodplain in China” (*Environmental Science & Technology*)

URL: https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.est.6b04260

Supports:

- The middle and lower Yangtze floodplain contains dense lake clusters and has experienced long-term lake and wetland loss.
- Conversion to cropland, aquaculture ponds, and built-up land, along with climate variation, sediment, and hydrological regulation, contributed to changing lake area.

Editorial boundaries:

- Flood risk and habitat change are presented as the result of interacting river processes and human land use, not as one dam's work alone.
- The article avoids exact modern lake-area numbers because they vary strongly by season and study definition.

## 6. UNESCO World Heritage Centre — Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City

URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1592/

Supports:

- Liangzhu in the lower Yangtze basin was an early regional state based on rice cultivation, with urban planning, social hierarchy, and a complex water-management system.
- The site demonstrates a major contribution from the Yangtze basin to the origins of Chinese civilization.

Editorial boundaries:

- A “major contribution” is not rewritten as “the one birthplace.” Liangzhu is one strong piece of evidence within a multi-regional history.

## 7. UNESCO — “Archaeological discovery of the Hongshan culture jade dragons”

URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/archaeological-discovery-hongshan-culture-jade-dragons

Supports:

- Hongshan culture in the Liao River region developed distinctive ritual and jade traditions.
- Archaeological evidence shows contact among Hongshan, Yangshao, Lingjiatan, Liangzhu, and neighboring cultures, supporting a history of regional interaction rather than one isolated origin point.

Editorial boundaries:

- The article uses the Liao basin only as one example of important development beyond the Yellow and Yangtze main valleys.
- Modern national identity is not projected backward as if all Neolithic communities called themselves one people.

## 8. Deng et al. — “From Early Domesticated Rice of the Middle Yangtze Basin to Millet, Rice and Wheat Agriculture” (*PLOS ONE*)

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4604147/

Supports:

- Early northern traditions often emphasized millets, while middle and lower Yangtze traditions often emphasized rice.
- Rice spread north, millet moved into the middle Yangtze, and mixed farming existed in transition zones long before modern north-south slogans.
- Crop histories reflect both environmental suitability and cultural contact.

Editorial boundaries:

- “Wheat north, rice south” is presented as a rough memory aid, not a border or deterministic explanation of food, personality, architecture, transport, or dialect.
- Modern farming choices are explained through interacting climate, soil, water, irrigation, technology, markets, transport, history, and food preference.
