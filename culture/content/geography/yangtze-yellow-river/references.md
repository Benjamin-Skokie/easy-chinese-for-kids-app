# Editorial References: Yangtze and Yellow Rivers

Final child-facing draft: 2026-09-03
Accessed: 2026-09-04

This file supports fact-checking and image decisions. It does not render on the website.

## Current Story and Editorial Boundaries

- The final story follows two drops of snowmelt, explains the Yellow River's color and raised riverbed, rides through the Three Gorges with Li Bai, visits the Three Gorges power station and ship locks, and compares concrete life beside the two rivers.
- The U.S.-facing English text uses rounded miles rather than kilometers.
- Specialist river-measurement questions, detailed dam policy, archaeological surveys, and abstract lessons about “giving rivers space” are intentionally omitted.
- The Three Gorges claim is limited to *installed capacity*: the station can make more electricity at one time than any other operating hydropower station. The story does not claim that it produces the greatest amount every year.

## 1. River Lengths, Sources, and Destinations

National Development and Reform Commission, “Where Is Qinghai?”

URL: https://www.ndrc.gov.cn/wsdwhfz/202106/t20210609_1282865.html

Supports:

- The Yangtze begins in the highlands of western China and reaches the East China Sea.
- Its stated length of 6,387 kilometers is rounded to about 4,000 miles for children in the United States.
- The Yellow River's stated length of 5,464 kilometers is rounded to about 3,400 miles; it reaches the Bohai Sea.

## 2. Yellow River Mud and the Raised Lower Riverbed

Yellow River Conservancy Commission, “Analysis of Yellow River Flood Control”

URL: https://www.yrcc.gov.cn/zwzc/zcjd/202403/t20240312_426969.html

Supports:

- Much of the Yellow River's sediment comes from the loose-soil region crossed by tributaries in its middle reaches.
- When the river slows downstream, sediment settles in the channel.
- Parts of the existing lower riverbed stand above the nearby land after long sediment buildup between raised riverbanks.
- River management includes maintaining flood barriers, protecting soil with vegetation, and using reservoirs.

Editorial boundary:

- The learner text describes “soft, yellow-brown soil” and “high walls of packed earth.” It does not ask young readers to learn *Loess Plateau* or *levee*.

## 3. The Three Gorges Power Station and Ship Locks

U.S. Geological Survey, “Three Gorges Dam: The World's Largest Hydroelectric Plant”

URL: https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/three-gorges-dam-worlds-largest-hydroelectric-plant

U.S. Energy Information Administration, “The World's Nine Largest Operating Power Plants Are Hydroelectric Facilities”

URL: https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=28392

China Three Gorges Corporation, “Three Gorges Dam: A Model of China's Infrastructure Construction”

URL: https://www.ctg.com.cn/ctgenglish/news_media/media56/2024082017122577429/index.html

China Three Gorges Corporation, “Three Gorges Project”

URL: https://www.ctg.com.cn/cypc/zxgg99/sxgc3/index.html

Supports:

- Independent English-language sources identify Three Gorges as the world's largest operating hydroelectric station by installed capacity.
- Water drives large machines that turn its force into electricity.
- Annual production changes with seasonal water supply, so installed capacity is not the same as winning every year's electricity total.
- The project assists flood control and navigation.
- Its two-line, five-stage ship locks move large vessels between the different water heights above and below the dam.

Editorial boundary:

- The children's story explains a ship lock step by step as gated pools that fill or empty. It omits megawatts, generator counts, and engineering vocabulary.

## 4. Li Bai in the Three Gorges

Li Bai, “Leaving Baidi City in the Morning” (*早发白帝城*)

Text: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/早發白帝城

Supports:

- The story quotes “轻舟已过万重山” and connects the short line with the feeling of a boat moving swiftly past peak after peak.

Editorial boundary:

- The English line “My light boat has passed ten thousand mountains” is a brief child-facing rendering, not a word-by-word language lesson.
- The removed “a thousand *li* in one day” wording is not used, so children do not need an explanation of the historical distance unit.

## 5. Broad Crop Patterns

Deng et al., “From Early Domesticated Rice of the Middle Yangtze Basin to Millet, Rice and Wheat Agriculture,” *PLOS ONE*

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4604147/

Supports:

- Northern farming traditions often emphasized dry-land grains, while many middle and lower Yangtze communities developed rice farming.
- Crops moved between regions, so the comparison is a broad pattern rather than a hard food border.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text uses familiar wheat fields, noodles, rice fields, and boats to show two kinds of river life, while noting that people throughout China now eat both rice and noodles.
