# Mount Everest — References and Editorial Notes

This file supports fact-checking and does not render on the website.

## Current Child-Facing Draft (2026-09-03)

- Story path: find Everest on the map, see how surveyors measure a mountain, compare three meanings of “highest,” discover sea-floor rock near the summit, and meet the team behind the 1953 climb.
- The measurement section deliberately stops at the child-size idea: choose sea level as zero, find the height of the survey point, use a known distance and a measured angle to calculate the summit's extra height, then add the two heights.
- Advanced details about satellite receivers, gravity models, snow depth, and surveying corrections are outside the scope of this story.

Supports:

- Mount Everest, also called Qomolangma, stands in the Himalayas on the China-Nepal border.
- China and Nepal jointly announced a snow-surface elevation of 8,848.86 meters in 2020.
- The story rounds the conversion to about 29,032 feet for young U.S. readers.

Editorial boundary:

- The story gives one current official height and does not compare older rock-height and snow-height figures.
- It introduces both “Everest” and “Qomolangma” without adding a long history of the mountain's names.

## 2. How Surveyors Measure Height

Source: U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Geodetic Survey, “Geodetic Leveling”
URL: https://geodesy.noaa.gov/heightmod/Leveling/
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Source: U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Geodetic Survey, “Elevation Above Sea Level”
URL: https://geodesy.noaa.gov/PUBS_LIB/basgeo.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Supports:

- Elevation is measured from a shared reference based on mean sea level.
- Surveyors can carry known heights inland through a network of measured points.
- Trigonometric leveling uses a measured vertical angle and a known distance to calculate the height of another point; it is especially useful in mountains.

Editorial boundary:

- The flagpole is an explanatory analogy, not a description of the full 2020 Everest survey.
- The story says “a small telescope-like tool” instead of requiring a six-year-old to learn *theodolite*.
- The final explanation uses simple addition: the survey point's elevation plus the summit's extra height. It does not describe modern geodesy as only one triangle drawn from sea level to the summit.

## 3. Three Meanings of “Highest”

Source: U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Ocean Service, “What is the highest point on Earth as measured from Earth's center?”
URL: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/highestpoint.html
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Supports:

- Everest is the highest mountain above mean sea level.
- Mauna Kea is taller when measured from its base on the ocean floor to its summit.
- Chimborazo's summit is farther from Earth's center because Earth bulges around the equator.

Editorial boundary:

- These are answers to three different measurement questions. The comparison does not claim that Everest is “not really” the highest mountain.

## 4. Ancient Sea Rock and the Making of the Himalayas

Source: Montana State University Everest Education Expedition, “Sea Floor to Summit”
URL: https://www.montana.edu/everest/resources/documents/lesson03/Lesson03_Sea_Floor_to_Summit.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, *This Dynamic Earth: The Story of Plate Tectonics*
URL: https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/understanding.html
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Supports:

- Limestone near Everest's summit formed from sediment in an ancient ocean and contains fragments of marine fossils.
- The long collision of India and Asia folded, faulted, and lifted rock to form the Himalayas.
- That movement is still continuing, while erosion and other processes also reshape the mountains.

Editorial boundary:

- The story uses the simple image of land pressing into land, but does not present the Himalayas as the result of one sudden crash.
- It does not give one fixed yearly “growth rate” for Everest.

## 5. Climate with Height and the 1953 Climb

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Sagarmatha National Park”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/120/
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Source: Royal Geographical Society, “Mount Everest”
URL: https://www.rgs.org/schools/resources-for-schools/mountains-volcanoes-and-earthquakes/mount-everest
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Source: Royal Geographical Society, “Everest 1953 Limited Edition Platinum Prints”
URL: https://www.rgs.org/our-collections/buy-and-license-images/platinum-prints/everest-1953
Accessed: 2026-09-03

Supports:

- The Everest region changes from inhabited valleys and forests to glaciers, bare rock, intense cold, and very thin air as elevation rises.
- Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay reached the summit together on May 29, 1953, as members of a much larger expedition.
- A successful ascent includes returning safely; the story therefore treats the descent and the work of the wider team as part of the achievement.

Editorial boundary:

- “Sherpa” names a people, not a job. The story distinguishes Sherpa guides and high-altitude crew from the wider Sherpa community.
- Earlier disputed attempts and later climbing records are omitted because they interrupt this short story.
