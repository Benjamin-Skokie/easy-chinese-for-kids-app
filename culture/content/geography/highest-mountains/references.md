# Highest Mountains — References and Editorial Notes

## The 2020 Joint Height and What It Measures

Source: Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People's Republic of China, “President Xi Jinping and Nepalese President Bidya Devi Bhandari Exchange Letters to Jointly Announce New Height of Mount Qomolangma,” December 8, 2020
URL: https://www.mfa.gov.cn/zyxw/202012/t20201208_348872.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Nepal, “Press Release on the Joint Announcement of the Height of Mount Sagarmatha/Zhumulangma,” December 8, 2020
URL: https://old.mofa.gov.np/news-and-activities/page/91/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- China and Nepal jointly announced a new snow-surface elevation of 8,848.86 meters on December 8, 2020.
- The result was based on work by the two countries' professional surveying teams and connected to a global/common height reference.
- The summit is a boundary point between China and Nepal, and the two governments connect measurement with cooperation in research and environmental protection.

Editorial boundary:

- 8,848.86 meters is identified as a snow-surface elevation above a reference sea level, not a base-to-summit body length.
- The date and measurement context remain attached to the number. The article does not imply that a natural snow summit is an eternally fixed object measurable without uncertainty or convention.
- Older rock-height and snow-height values are not mixed into the child-facing story.

## Different Meanings of “Highest”

Source: U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, National Ocean Service, “What is the highest point on Earth as measured from Earth's center?”
URL: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/highestpoint.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Everest has the highest elevation above mean sea level.
- Mauna Kea rises more than 10,000 meters from its base on the ocean floor to its summit.
- Chimborazo's summit is farther from Earth's center than Everest's because Earth bulges around the equator.

Editorial boundary:

- These mountains answer different measurement questions; the article does not claim that Everest is “not really highest.”
- The comparison is about reference points, not a competition among cultures or countries.

## Location and Names

Source: National Geographic Education, “Mount Everest”
URL: https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/mount-everest/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Montana State University Everest Education Expedition, “The Naming of Mount Everest”
URL: https://www.montana.edu/everest/facts/naming.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The mountain stands in the Himalayas on the Nepal-China boundary, with Nepal to the south and the Tibet Autonomous Region of China to the north.
- Qomolangma/Chomolungma is a Tibetan name; Sagarmatha is the official Nepali name.
- The Royal Geographical Society adopted “Mount Everest” in 1865 after Andrew Waugh proposed naming Peak XV for former Surveyor General George Everest.
- Sherpa is the name of an ethnic community and should not be used merely as a synonym for guide or porter.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses multiple names respectfully and does not present the English colonial-era name as the only neutral or original name.
- It avoids locking the Tibetan name into one uncontested English translation, because published translations vary.
- “Shared summit” is clarified: the international boundary crosses the summit, and each country administers its side.

## Himalayan Geology and Summit Limestone

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, *This Dynamic Earth: The Story of Plate Tectonics*
URL: https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/understanding.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, “Geology of the Southern Himalaya,” Professional Paper 716-C
URL: https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0716c/report.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Montana State University Everest Education Expedition, “Sea Floor to Summit”
URL: https://www.montana.edu/everest/resources/lessons.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Xu Han et al., “Recent uplift of Chomolungma enhanced by river drainage piracy,” *Nature Geoscience* 17 (2024) 1031–1037
URL: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10198003/
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-024-01535-w
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Himalaya formed through the long, continuing convergence of India and Asia, including crustal shortening, thickening, faulting, thrusting, uplift, and erosion.
- Limestone near Everest's summit formed as sediment in the ancient Tethys marine environment and contains marine fossils.
- Mountain elevation reflects competing and interacting processes, including tectonic deformation, earthquakes, erosion, landslides, glaciers, and isostatic adjustment.
- A 2024 study proposed that river capture and incision contributed roughly 15–50 meters to Everest's unusual elevation through isostatic response; this remains a scientific model and has received subsequent scholarly scrutiny.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not describe two plates as a simple car crash that directly pushes one intact seabed upward.
- It does not state one universal annual “growth rate” for Everest or the Himalaya. Rates depend on location, time span, reference frame, and process.
- The river-uplift work is phrased as active scientific research, not the final single cause of Everest's height.

## Vertical Climate, Weather, and Communities

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Sagarmatha National Park”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/120/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Tom Matthews et al., “Weather Observations Reach the Summit of Mount Everest,” *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society* 103.12 (2022)
URL: https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/103/12/BAMS-D-22-0120.1.xml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Sagarmatha National Park spans roughly 6,000 meters of elevation, contains deep valleys, glaciers, high peaks, and altitude-related changes in vegetation and habitat, and is home to Sherpa communities.
- Near Everest's upper slopes, air pressure is around one-third of sea-level pressure; weather, wind, temperature, sunlight, and oxygen availability vary substantially.
- Scientific knowledge of exact summit weather has historically been limited by sparse observations; automatic stations improve monitoring but do not justify one timeless exact temperature claim.

Editorial boundary:

- “From the equator to the Arctic” is labeled an exaggerated metaphor, not a climate classification. Latitude, monsoon, slope aspect, terrain, and elevation interact.
- The retained vertical-climate illustration is a modern composite and is not treated as a photograph or accurate transect.
- The article avoids one exact year-round summit temperature and does not say winds are literally nonstop.

## The 1924, 1953, and 1960 Ascents

Source: British Mountaineering Council, “Everest: Facts and Figures”
URL: https://www.thebmc.co.uk/en/everest-facts-and-figures
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Associated Press, “George Mallory's last letter from Everest said odds of reaching the top were ‘50 to 1 against us,’” April 22, 2024
URL: https://apnews.com/article/c204f7f3348a413d39a84f1a1b93c8cd
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Associated Press, “Partial remains of British climber believed found 100 years after Everest ascent,” October 11, 2024
URL: https://apnews.com/article/2081dc55cb79316a1c90bce0eb1e5cba
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Mallory and Irvine disappeared during their 1924 attempt; later discoveries have not established whether they reached the summit.
- Hillary and Tenzing reached the summit on May 29, 1953 and returned with documentation; their ascent is the first confirmed/well-documented success.

Source: General Administration of Sport of China, “Commemorating the 60th Anniversary of Humanity's First Ascent of Everest”
URL: https://www.sport.gov.cn/n20001280/n20745751/n20767635/c21071101/content.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Himalayan Database, “Everest 1960, N Col–NE Ridge”
URL: https://himalayan-database.climbing-history.org/expedition/EVER60102/1960
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Walt Unsworth, “Problems of Accuracy in Reporting Mountaineering,” *Himalayan Journal* 44 (1988)
URL: https://himalayanclub.org/hj/44/24/problems-of-accuracy-in-reporting-mountaineering/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Wang Fuzhou, Qu Yinhua, and Gonpo are recorded as reaching the summit from the north on May 25, 1960, the first accepted ascent by that side.
- The absence of a clear summit photograph and the political/information environment led some foreign mountaineers to doubt the report at first.
- Later contact, testimony, route detail, and China's documented 1975 ascent reduced those doubts; major databases and standard histories accept the 1960 ascent today.

Editorial boundary:

- The 1924 attempt remains uncertain and is not promoted to a summit.
- The 1953 success belongs equally to Hillary and Tenzing and is placed within the labor of the wider expedition.
- The 1960 controversy is neither hidden nor treated as permanently unresolved. The article distinguishes initial skepticism from current broad acceptance.
- The retained climber artwork depicts none of these expeditions and cannot support route, equipment, weather, identity, or summit claims.

## The Fourteen Eight-Thousanders and Their Countries

Source: NASA Earth Observatory, “The Eight-Thousanders”
URL: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/the-eight-thousanders/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Nepal Tourism Board, “Eight-Thousanders”
URL: https://ntb.gov.np/en/eight-thousanders
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Source: Elizabeth Hawley and Richard Salisbury, *The Himalayan Database*, peak and expedition records
URL: https://www.himalayandatabase.com/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The traditional list contains fourteen main summits above 8,000 meters, all in the Himalaya and Karakoram.
- Nepal has eight wholly or partly within its borders: Everest, Kangchenjunga, Lhotse, Makalu, Cho Oyu, Dhaulagiri I, Manaslu, and Annapurna I.
- China has nine wholly or partly within its borders: Everest, Lhotse, Makalu, Cho Oyu, K2, Gasherbrum I, Broad Peak, Gasherbrum II, and Shishapangma.
- Pakistan has five wholly or partly within its borders: K2, Nanga Parbat, Gasherbrum I, Broad Peak, and Gasherbrum II. India shares Kangchenjunga with Nepal.

Editorial boundary:

- Border peaks are counted for both countries, so national totals overlap.
- The list is not described as “all on the Tibetan Plateau” or as mostly within China.
- The 8,000-meter threshold is a human unit convention, not a natural geological boundary or measure of cultural importance.

Source: Mariusz Potocki et al., “Mt. Everest's highest glacier is a sentinel for accelerating ice loss,” *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science* 5, 7 (2022)
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41612-022-00230-0
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41612-022-00230-0

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Sagarmatha National Park”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/120/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Local organizations manage solid and human waste, route work, education, disaster preparation, and environmental protection in Nepal's Everest region.
- Microplastics have been detected in snow and stream samples on Everest.
- The South Col Glacier at roughly 8,020 meters shows rapid recent ice loss and sensitivity to warming and surface changes.
- Climate change affects glaciers, hazards, ecosystems, water, and Sherpa and other local communities, not only climbing conditions.

Editorial boundary:

- The story names major hazards but gives no instructions that encourage an untrained child to climb.
- It avoids unstable permit counts, current-season summit totals, death totals, or waste-deposit rules.
- Climbers are not the only people in the landscape; conservation includes resident communities and downstream effects.

## Image Audit

The four retained page images are modern illustrations or schematic graphics, not historical or scientific evidence.

- `qomolangma-location-map.webp`: retained only for broad Asian orientation. Borders, proportions, mountain placement, and relief are schematic.
- `everest-snowline-vertical-climate.webp`: retained to prompt discussion of change with elevation, but its vegetation, flowers, glacier, and peak are an exaggerated composite rather than a real Everest transect.
- `everest-climbers-shared-summit.webp`: retained as generic modern adventure art. It does not depict the 1924, 1953, or 1960 routes, people, equipment, safety systems, or summit.
- `himalayan-high-peaks.webp`: retained as an imaginative mountain panorama. It is not a map of the fourteen eight-thousanders, and the depicted children, village, and peaks should not be assigned a real ethnicity or location.

Two additional source images in the directory (`qomolangma-highest-mountain.webp` and `everest-shared-summit.webp`) remain unused; they were not needed to support the revised story and should not be treated as verified photographs of Everest.
