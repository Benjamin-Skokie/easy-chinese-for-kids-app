# Choosing Officials Shared Story and Facts Brief

## Core ideas and evidence

1. Chinese governments used changing combinations of family status, appointment, recommendation, education, examinations, and imperial authority to select officials.
2. Early officeholding often favored noble families, but no single rule explains every office or period. Education and access were extremely unequal.
3. Han `chaju` asked local officials to nominate men with reputations for character or talent; nominees could face later evaluation. It was not an open test.
4. The Wei nine-rank system tried to organize recruitment after disorder. Over time, powerful families strongly influenced ratings, although formal evaluation also considered character and ability.
5. Sui and Tang examinations grew gradually from earlier practices. Tang access expanded, but nomination, reputation, family resources, and other paths to office remained important.
6. Song exams expanded and used anonymity, numbering, and recopying to reduce favoritism, while hereditary privilege and unequal resources continued.
7. Ming-Qing exams formed a more regular ladder but remained limited by education, money, region, status, and gender. The exams ended in 1905 amid broader reforms.

## Boundaries

- Do not tell a straight story from unfair birth to perfectly fair exams.
- Keep all four gate, recommendation, ranking, and paper images symbolic.
- Do not imply that one kind act earned a Han office or that Tang testing was fully anonymous and open.
- Equal papers do not mean equal starting points.

## Level assignments

- Early Reader: use doors and keys to compare birth, recommendation, ranking, and written answers.
- Story: follow the major systems chronologically and keep their purposes, improvements, and limits visible.
- Advanced: preserve institutional nuance, competing routes, safeguards, continued privilege, and the 1905 ending.
