# Imperial Exam Questions Shared Story and Facts Brief

## Core ideas and evidence

1. Imperial examinations changed by dynasty, level, and subject. They were not one unchanged “ancient college entrance exam.”
2. Tang exams included several categories. The *jinshi* exam changed over time; policy questions mattered, while poetry and *fu* grew prominent later without erasing all other content.
3. In 1071, Wang Anshi's Northern Song reforms removed poetry and *fu* from the *jinshi* exam and emphasized classical interpretation, essays, and policy responses. The reform was debated and later partly reversed.
4. Ming and Qing exams used tightly regulated classical essays, but three-session exams also included other writing, legal judgments, policy questions, and—after 1757 in the Qing—regulated poetry.
5. A typical large late-imperial session could keep a candidate in an examination cell for about three days. This did not mean the whole examination pathway lasted three days.
6. Common papers and anti-cheating rules did not erase unequal access to education, travel, health, money, gender, or legal eligibility.

## Boundaries

- All candidate scenes are modern imagination, not biographies.
- Do not invent specific exam questions or claim every candidate took the same subjects.
- Do not reduce poetry to beauty, policy writing to pure thinking, or eight-legged essays to memorization alone.
- Keep one session, one exam level, and the full examination system distinct.

## Level assignments

- Early Reader: show that exam questions changed and that each choice trained and selected different skills.
- Story: move through Tang, Song, Ming-Qing, the cells, and fairness with enough context for a middle-elementary reader.
- Advanced: preserve categories, dates, reform debates, writing forms, session structure, and access limits.
