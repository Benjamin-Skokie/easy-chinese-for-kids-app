# What Was on the Imperial Exam? — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Tang Examination Poetry

Source: Nathan Vedal, “Never Taking a Shortcut: Examination Poetry of the Tang Dynasty,” *Tang Studies*
URL: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/766627
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Shows that poetry composition became part of the Tang *jinshi* examination from the later seventh century.
- Explains that examination poetry tested a candidate’s command of literary tradition within a constrained setting.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim that every Tang examination used poetry or that candidates could write any poem they wished.

## Tang Examination Categories

Source: *Xin Tang Shu* (《新唐书·选举志》), Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=304555&if=en
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Records Tang *jinshi* examination combinations including poetry, *fu*, and policy questions, together with later changes to the requirements.

Editorial boundary:

- Tang examination subjects changed over time; the article does not treat one recorded combination as a permanent syllabus.

## Wang Anshi’s 1071 Examination Reform

Source: Kazunari Kondo, “Concerning the Examination Reforms of Wang An-shi,” Kyoto University Research Information Repository
URL: https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/handle/2433/154215
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Describes the 1071 reform that removed poetry and *fu* from the *jinshi* examination and emphasized classical interpretation, essays, and policy questions.

Editorial boundary:

- The reform did not settle examination content permanently; poetry later returned for a time and the rules continued to change.

## The Eight-Legged Essay

Source: Gong Yanming and Gao Mingyang, “Evaluative Standard for the Eight-Legged Essay in the Imperial Examinations of the Qing Dynasty,” *Frontiers of Literary Studies in China*
URL: https://journal.hep.com.cn/flsc/EN/10.1007/s11702-007-0029-z
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Discusses the strict literary and evaluative rules governing the eight-legged essay and the skills required to write it.

Editorial boundary:

- The form was restrictive, but the article does not reduce it to memorizing a model or imply that it made up every part of a Ming or Qing examination.

## The Ming Provincial Examination

Source: National Museum of China, “明万历四年顾宪成试卷稿册”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/201812/t20181218_24728.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Describes the three-session Ming provincial examination and the different forms of writing candidates faced.

Editorial boundary:

- This surviving example supports the broad Ming examination sequence; it is not presented as every candidate’s personal paper.

## The Ming Metropolitan Examination

Source: National Museum of China, “明万历三十二年余懋孳会试朱卷”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/gjwxbt/202008/t20200824_247146.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Provides evidence for the comparable three-session structure of the Ming metropolitan examination and its different forms of written work.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses this metropolitan paper alongside the provincial example without suggesting that all examination levels were identical.

## The Qing Provincial Examination

Source: Palace Museum, “乡试”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/court/system/236410.html
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Describes the Qing provincial examination’s three sessions, including Four Books and Five Classics essays, official writing, policy questions, and regulated poetry.

Editorial boundary:

- Qing subjects and rules changed during the dynasty; the article describes the broad three-session pattern rather than one unchanging paper.
