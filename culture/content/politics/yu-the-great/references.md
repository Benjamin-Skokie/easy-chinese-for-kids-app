# Yu the Great — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## An Early Inscribed Yu Tradition

Source: Toru Takahashi, “Suigongxu as Source Material,” *Shigaku Zasshi* 115.1 (2006)
URL: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/shigaku/115/1/115_KJ00004091863/_article/-char/en
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Sui/Bin Gong xu is generally dated stylistically to around the middle Western Zhou period and contains an inscription about Heaven ordering Yu to spread out the land and dredge rivers.
- This object is often discussed as important early evidence for the circulation of a Yu water-ordering tradition.

Editorial boundary:

- The vessel did not come from a documented archaeological excavation, and scholars have raised questions about its provenance, form, and inscription.
- Even if accepted as a Western Zhou object, it proves that a Yu tradition circulated by that time; it does not prove that Yu was a historical engineer or that the events occurred exactly as later texts describe.
- The child-facing article therefore refers only generally to later bronze inscriptions and books and does not use the vessel as proof of Yu's life.

## Water Control in the *Book of Documents*

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Book of Documents*, “Yi and Ji”
URL: https://ctext.org/shang-shu/yi-and-ji/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Book of Documents*, “Tribute of Yu”
URL: https://ctext.org/shang-shu/tribute-of-yu/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The received text attributes to Yu the opening of major waterways toward the seas and the dredging of smaller channels toward rivers: “予决九川，距四海，浚畎浍距川.”
- The Yu tradition includes mountains, rivers, marshes, fields, food supply, travel, and regional organization, not only one Yellow River engineering site.

Editorial boundary:

- These texts are not eyewitness engineering records from Yu's supposed lifetime. The composition and transmission of chapters in the *Book of Documents* are complex.
- Do not turn their broad sacred-political geography into a precise modern project map.
- Do not invent which mountain Yu inspected, the exact tools used, the order of work, or Yu's private reasoning.

## The *Mencius*: Waterways and Three Passes

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Mencius*, “Teng Wengong I”
URL: https://ctext.org/mengzi/teng-wen-gong-i/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The text describes Yu clearing multiple waterways and sending waters toward the sea.
- It says Yu spent eight years away and passed his gate three times without entering: “禹八年于外，三过其门而不入.”

Editorial boundary:

- The *Mencius* does not supply three separate scenes involving childbirth, a toddler, and an older child.
- Its eight-year figure must not be silently combined with the thirteen-year figure in the *Records of the Grand Historian*.
- The *Mencius* is evidence that this form of the tradition circulated in the Warring States period, not proof that the described scene happened exactly as told.

## The *Records of the Grand Historian*: Gun, Yu, and Thirteen Years

Source: Chinese Text Project, Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian*, “Basic Annals of Xia”
URL: https://ctext.org/shiji/xia-ben-ji/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Gun is assigned to control the waters and fails after nine years.
- Yu is described as surveying mountains and rivers, organizing labor, opening waterways, and spending thirteen years away.
- The text says Yu passed his home and did not dare enter: “居外十三年，过家门不敢入.”
- It presents Yu as Shun's successor and the first ruler in its Xia annals.

Editorial boundary:

- Sima Qian wrote many centuries after the era assigned to Yu. This is a powerful later historical synthesis, not a contemporary biography.
- The sentence about thirteen years does not itself say “three times.”
- It does not document the three modern family scenes formerly included in this article.
- Gun's failure and Yu's success may be described as part of the received tradition, but the text does not provide a modern engineering comparison proving that one used only dams and the other used only channels.

## The “Block Versus Guide” Simplification

Source: Wang Hui, “A Re-exploration of Yu the Great's Thinking of Taming the Waters,” *Journal of Shaanxi Normal University (Philosophy and Social Sciences Edition)*
URL: https://www.xuebao.snnu.edu.cn/info/1072/7587.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- A historian explicitly challenges the simple explanation that Gun failed solely because he blocked water while Yu succeeded solely because he dredged it.
- The familiar contrast is an interpretation of a varied textual tradition rather than a secure reconstruction of two engineering programs.

Editorial boundary:

- The article may discuss “block or guide” as a memorable later cultural contrast.
- It must not claim that modern research has confirmed the exact wall failures, routes, or decision process shown in the illustrations.

## Early Flood Tradition and the Later Xia Connection

Source: Sarah Allan, “The Jishi Outburst Flood of 1920 BCE and the Great Flood Legend in Ancient China: Preliminary Reflections,” *Journal of Chinese Humanities* 3.1 (2017)
URL: https://doi.org/10.1163/23521341-12340041
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Allan argues that early Yu traditions describe the ordering of waters across the world, not one specifically identified Yellow River flood.
- She argues that Yu's ordering of the waters and the foundation of the Xia were not linked in the earliest accounts but became connected in the developing tradition.
- The flood story can be read as a cosmogonic or world-ordering narrative that makes land habitable and fit for agriculture.

Editorial boundary:

- These are scholarly interpretations, not unanimous conclusions.
- The child-facing article uses “some scholars argue” and does not state that every early-China specialist agrees.

## Erlitou and the Xia Debate

Source: Xu Hong, “Introduction to the Xia Period: Definitions, Themes, and Debate,” in *The Oxford Handbook of Early China*
URL: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34296/chapter-abstract/290747467
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Robert L. Thorp, “Erlitou and the Search for the Xia,” *Early China* 16 (1991)
URL: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0362502800003825
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: British Museum, “Knife,” curator's comments on the Erlitou culture
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1945-1017-144
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Erlitou was a major early Bronze Age center and is central to the debate about the Xia period.
- Excavations at Erlitou revealed large architectural complexes, roads or urban organization, burials, and finely made bronze and jade objects.
- Researchers disagree about whether and how Erlitou should be identified with the Xia dynasty.
- The lack of contemporary textual evidence that can verify Xia keeps the identification debated.

Editorial boundary:

- Erlitou is evidence for a complex early society; it is not a written identification of Yu or Xia.
- Do not say archaeology has proved that Yu founded the Xia or ruled from Erlitou.

## The Proposed Jishi Gorge Outburst Flood

Source: Qinglong Wu et al., “Outburst flood at 1920 BCE supports historicity of China's Great Flood and the Xia dynasty,” *Science* 353.6299 (2016)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27493183/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The research team reconstructed an earthquake-triggered landslide-dam outburst flood in the upper Yellow River around 1920 BCE.
- The authors proposed that this event could account for the Great Flood tradition and might align with the emergence of Erlitou.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a published hypothesis, not proof that the flood was “Yu's flood.”
- The proposed date is later than some traditional chronologies for the beginning of Xia.
- A large regional flood cannot by itself verify a named hero, a multi-year control project, or the founding of a dynasty.

## Scientific and Historical Challenges to the Jishi Link

Source: Fengwen Liu et al., “Multiple evidences indicate no relationship between prehistoric disasters in Lajia site and outburst flood in upper Yellow River valley, China,” *Science China Earth Sciences* 61 (2018), 441–449
URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11430-017-9079-3
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- This study reports dates and geological interpretations inconsistent with the 2016 reconstruction.
- Its authors argue that the Lajia earthquake disaster, the dammed lake, and the proposed outburst were independent events at different times.
- The study demonstrates that the geological reconstruction itself remains disputed.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not present either paper as the final word.
- The article should say researchers reached different conclusions about dates, sediments, and connections among events.
