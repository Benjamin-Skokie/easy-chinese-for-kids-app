# Why Did Zhu Yuanzhang Become Such a Busy Emperor?

## Do Not Begin with a Timetable

It is tempting to begin an emperor's day with a tidy schedule: wake at three, hold court at five, and work until midnight. The trouble is that such schedules often mix emperors and dynasties. We have no reliable evidence that Zhu Yuanzhang lived by one exact clock every day.

History lets us ask a better question: What work reached him as emperor? Zhu, also known as the Hongwu emperor, founded the [[Ming dynasty]] and ruled from 1368 to 1398. His capital was in present-day Nanjing.

---

## What Did a New Dynasty Have to Do?

Zhu was born in 1328 near present-day Fengyang in Anhui. His family was extremely poor. Later biographies say that he herded animals and then entered a monastery. Famine, disease, rebellion, and war transformed his life. He joined a rebel army and eventually founded a new dynasty.

As emperor, he did not inherit a country that was already running smoothly. The government had to rebuild after war, send orders across the empire, and manage grain, taxes, rivers, laws, officials, and border defense.

---

## Follow One Report

Suppose a flood strikes a county. Officials describe the damage and ask for grain or lower taxes. Their formal document joins the flow of memorials—reports, requests, and advice sent toward the emperor.

A messenger does not burst into the emperor's private room. Offices receive, register, check, and pass along the document. River repairs, grain shipments, appointments, court cases, and border news may require several departments to study them.

Zhu gradually changed the route. Some important reports could now reach him more directly instead of first passing through the Central Secretariat, the highest administrative office.

---

## The Great Change of 1380

Early in the Ming, the Central Secretariat and its chancellor helped direct much of the government. Think of the institution as a major desk between the emperor and six important ministries.

In 1380, after the case against Chancellor Hu Weiyong, Zhu abolished the office and stopped appointing a chancellor. The Six Ministries—which managed officials, revenue and population records, ceremonies, the military, law, and public works—then answered more directly to him.

The change concentrated more authority in the emperor's hands and sent more business his way. It was not a personal preference for “doing his own homework.” It reshaped Ming government.

---

## Eight Days and 1,660 Documents

An official record compiled after Zhu's death preserves an astonishing count. During eight days in 1384, central and local offices submitted 1,660 memorials containing 3,391 separate matters. That averages more than two hundred documents and four hundred issues each day.

Read those numbers carefully. They count business submitted by the government. They do not say that Zhu read every character alone or wrote a long answer to every matter. Eight days cannot stand for every day of a thirty-year reign.

The tally still shows that, after the chancellor disappeared, more paths of decision ended with the emperor.

---

## Power, Help, and Danger

Zhu did not work alone. The Six Ministries handled their own areas. Other offices organized memorials. Scholars near the emperor helped review documents, record decisions, or draft texts following his instructions. The later Grand Secretariat developed gradually; it was not simply the old chancellorship with a new name.

Removing one powerful helper gave the emperor more control. It also created a traffic jam when more roads led to one person.

A high document count proves workload, not fairness. Zhu's rule grew harsher in its later years, and many officials were imprisoned or killed in political purges. When one emperor holds so much power, his mistakes and suspicions can cause greater harm.

---

## Look Again at the Lamplight

Moonlight, lamplight, and piles of paper make the pressure of concentrated government easy to see. They do not show one documented night.

We do not know Zhu's exact daily routine. We do know that government produced a huge flow of business, the 1380 reform made the ministries answer more directly to him, many officials still helped process the work, and concentrated authority also concentrated danger.

Instead of inventing a timetable, we can follow a document through the government and ask why so many roads ended at the same desk.
