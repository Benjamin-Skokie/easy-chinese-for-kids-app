# The Six Arts — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## The Six Arts and the *Guozi*

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Rites of Zhou*, “Baoshi” (《周礼·地官司徒·保氏》)
URL: https://ctext.org/rites-of-zhou/di-guan-si-tu/zh
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Lists ritual, music, archery, chariot driving, writing, and calculation as the Six Arts.
- Says the *guozi* were instructed in them and divides each art into smaller groups.

Editorial boundary:

- The article presents this as an educational ideal connected with young people of the ruling elite, not as proof that every child in ancient China attended the same school or learned all six arts.

## The Broader Educational Program

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Rites of Zhou*, “Ministry of Land and People” (《周礼·地官司徒》)
URL: https://ctext.org/rites-of-zhou/di-guan-si-tu/zh
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Places ritual, music, archery, driving, writing, and calculation within a broader program of education.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text does not turn this institutional ideal into a universal timetable or claim equal access to teachers, equipment, and training.

## Archery and Self-Examination

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Book of Rites*, “The Meaning of Archery” (《礼记·射义》)
URL: https://ctext.org/liji/she-yi/zh
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Connects ritual archery with self-examination and says that an archer who misses should look back at himself rather than blame the person who shot better.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses this later ritual interpretation to explain one way archery could become a lesson about conduct; it does not present the passage as a description of every archery practice.

## Chariots in the Shang and Zhou Worlds

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Plaque in the Shape of a Horse-Drawn Chariot”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/65256
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Describes the importance of horse-drawn chariots during the Shang and Zhou periods in warfare and as symbols of elite prestige.

Editorial boundary:

- The evidence explains why chariot driving could matter to elite education; it does not identify the illustrated people or reconstruct one exact training session.

## Brushes and Bamboo Manuscripts

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Chinese Arts of the Brush”
URL: https://archive.asia.si.edu/exhibitions/online/ChineseArts/default.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Provides background on the long history of brush writing in China before paper became common.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says only that brushes had already developed before paper became common; it does not assign one exact invention date.

Source: Dartmouth College, “Tsinghua Bamboo Manuscripts Offer Clues About Early China”
URL: https://global.dartmouth.edu/news/2013/05/tsinghua-bamboo-manuscripts-offer-clues-about-early-china
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Describes Warring States manuscripts written vertically with brush and ink on bamboo slips tied together with cords.

Editorial boundary:

- This archaeological evidence supports the general description of the writing material and method, not a claim that every early text was made in exactly the same way.

## Counting Rods in Chinese Mathematics

Source: Yiwen Zhu, “The Interplay between Textual Procedures and Material Operations from the Viewpoint of Chinese Mathematical Texts,” *Science in Context*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/science-in-context/article/interplay-between-textual-procedures-and-material-operations-from-the-viewpoint-of-chinese-mathematical-texts/7E654BC8863452F26F2E0C43892699F4
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Discusses the long use of counting rods as a major tool for representing numbers and carrying out operations in Chinese mathematical practice.

Editorial boundary:

- Counting rods provide later mathematical context for calculation; the article does not claim that every Six Arts student used them in one standardized classroom lesson.
