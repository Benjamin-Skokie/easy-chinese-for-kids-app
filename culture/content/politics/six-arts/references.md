# The Six Arts — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## The Six-Arts List in the *Rites of Zhou*

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Rites of Zhou*, “Local Offices of Education and Land”
URL: https://ctext.org/rites-of-zhou/di-guan-si-tu/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The *Baoshi* is charged with educating the *guozi* and teaching six arts: “一曰五礼，二曰六乐，三曰五射，四曰五驭，五曰六书，六曰九数.”
- Another passage places “礼、乐、射、御、书、数” among broad programs of instruction for “万民.”
- The numbered subdivisions show that each “art” was a broad field, not one narrow modern school subject.

Source: Chinese Text Project dictionary entry, “国子”
URL: https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?char=%E5%9C%8B%E5%AD%90&if=en
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- *Guozi* is conventionally understood as children, especially sons, of nobles or high officials in this institutional context.

Source: “Education and Social Selection in Ancient China: Semantics, Conceptual Transformation and Social Change,” *Paedagogica Historica* 51.3
URL: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00309230.2014.934383
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Modern scholarship reads the *Baoshi* passage as describing the education of nobles’ sons.
- The six fields combine ritual, musical, military, written, and mathematical learning.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim that every child in the Spring and Autumn period received the same Six-Arts education.
- The two *Rites of Zhou* passages show both a broad ideal of public instruction and a more specific elite setting. Neither supplies attendance numbers, daily schedules, lesson length, student age, or equal access.
- “Five rites,” “six kinds of music,” and the other numbered groupings are retained as evidence of breadth; the article does not present later commentators’ exact sublists as one uncontested early curriculum.

## Dating and Nature of the *Rites of Zhou*

Source: Michael Nylan, *The Five “Confucian” Classics*, Yale University Press, notes to Chapter 4
URL: https://drupal.yalebooks.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/Media/9780300081855/9780300081855_nylan4notes.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The attribution and dating of the *Zhouli* have long been debated.
- Significant scholarship associates the work with the Warring States era rather than treating it as a straightforward administrative record written by the Duke of Zhou.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text says the book’s history is complex and that many scholars connect its system with Warring States thought. It does not assign every line one exact date.
- The title *Rites of Zhou* does not turn every institutional description into direct proof of daily Western Zhou or Spring and Autumn practice.
- The opening boy, father, dawn, target shots, weather, dialogue, and one-day sequence from the old draft were invented and have been removed as history.

## Meanings of the Six Practical Arts

Source: National Central University research record, Yen Yi Lee, “Sojourning in the Arts: Considering the Implications of the Confucian ‘Six Arts’ in a Contemporary Educational Context”
URL: https://scholars.ncu.edu.tw/zh/publications/sojourning-in-the-arts-considering-the-implications-of-the-confuc/
DOI: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003301516-8
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The conventional practical list is ritual, music, archery, chariot driving or driving, writing or literacy, and arithmetic.
- Modern scholarship often treats the combination as an educational ideal joining character and capability.

Editorial boundary:

- “Ritual” is not reduced to politeness, and “music” is not reduced to solo qin playing.
- “御” is presented as chariot driving, not ordinary horseback riding.
- “书” is presented broadly as literacy, writing, and knowledge of written forms; “数” as calculation or reckoning.
- The article does not claim that the Six Arts map neatly onto today’s school subjects or that the system was egalitarian.

## Bamboo Writing, Ink, Brush, and Knife

Source: National Museum of China, “白首汉廷刀笔吏”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/yj/xscg/xslw/201812/t20181224_33082.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Bamboo and wooden slips were important writing surfaces before paper became common.
- Writers used a brush to write; when they made mistakes, a writing knife could shave away the old character before rewriting.

Source: Dartmouth College, “Tsinghua Bamboo Manuscripts Offer Clues About Early China”
URL: https://global.dartmouth.edu/news/2013/05/tsinghua-bamboo-manuscripts-offer-clues-about-early-china
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Warring States bamboo manuscripts were written vertically with brush and ink and tied together with cords.

Editorial boundary:

- The old claim that an ordinary student carved the *Book of Songs* onto bamboo with a knife was removed.
- The retained image shows a knife in the student’s hand, so its caption explains the knife as a correction or preparation tool and labels the whole scene as imaginative.
- The article does not claim that this student, room, teacher, or copied text is historically documented.

## Archery, Ritual, and Self-Examination

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Book of Rites*, “The Meaning of Archery”
URL: https://ctext.org/liji/she-yi/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The chapter connects ritual archery with correct posture, ritual and musical rhythm, judgment of conduct, and self-examination.
- It says “射者，仁之道也” and explains that when an archer misses, he should not resent the person who surpasses him but turn back and examine himself.
- The text directly describes archery as a male activity and situates it within elite selection and ritual hierarchy.

Editorial boundary:

- “刚才是手在射，后来是心在射” is not in this source or another identified ancient source. The invented father’s speech was deleted.
- The article labels the moral interpretation as part of a later ritual text and does not turn it into a universal account of all early archery practice.
- Readers may consider the value of self-examination without being asked to copy the text’s gender and status rules.

## Counting Rods and Later Mathematical Context

Source: Mathematical Association of America, “Illustrating the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art”
URL: https://old.maa.org/press/periodicals/convergence/illustrating-the-nine-chapters-on-the-mathematical-art-their-use-in-a-college-mathematics-history-1
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Counting rods were used to represent numbers and perform calculations in the later mathematical tradition documented by the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.
- That later work includes practical problems involving fields, grain, proportions, and equations.

Editorial boundary:

- The counting rods in the illustration are used as a visual clue for calculation, not as proof of one specific Six-Arts lesson.
- Examples from the *Nine Chapters* are later mathematical context. The article does not project that compiled text, its exact problems, or its classroom methods backward into the curriculum described in the *Rites of Zhou*.

## Chariots, Warfare, and Elite Status

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Plaque in the Shape of a Horse-Drawn Chariot”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/65256
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- During the Shang and Zhou dynasties, horse-drawn chariots served as military transport and symbols of elite prestige.
- The number of chariots possessed by a state could represent its military power.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses this evidence only to explain why chariot driving could matter to elite education. It does not claim that the illustrated vehicle is an exact reconstruction or that all students trained with chariots.

## “Six Arts” and the Six Classics

Source: *The Oxford Handbook of Classical Chinese Literature*, “Traditional Genre Spectrum”
URL: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28107/chapter/514166256
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Cambridge University Press, “Who Said There Was a Classic of Music?”, *Early China*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/abs/who-said-there-was-a-classic-of-music/A6D9D81606DBCF10101DC3E929E5DFB5
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- “Six Arts” later became a label for areas of classical textual learning and is closely connected with the grouping commonly called the Six Classics.
- The textual category is historically more complicated than a timeless list of exactly six fixed books.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses the familiar child-level orientation—Poetry, Documents, Rites, Music, Changes, and Spring and Autumn Annals—while warning that “Six Arts” has more than one historical meaning.
- It does not claim that the practical Six Arts and Six Classics were always the same curriculum.

## Images and Modern Closing Question

All four retained images are modern imaginative illustrations, not documentary reconstructions:

- `1-six-arts-archery-dawn.webp`: no evidence for the student’s age, identity, father, dawn schedule, two shots, or dialogue.
- `2-six-arts-bamboo-writing.webp`: the knife is explained as a correction/preparation tool; ordinary manuscript writing used brush and ink.
- `3-six-arts-chariot-driving.webp`: the two-horse vehicle, teacher, clothing, and setting are not treated as an exact Zhou training reconstruction.
- `4-six-arts-qin-and-math.webp`: qin, slips, and counting rods are symbolic windows into broad fields, not proof that music and calculation occurred together at dusk.

The “design a new Six Arts” ending is an openly modern reflection. Its suggestions are not attributed to an ancient source.
