# One Armor Plate, Many Answers — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Early Leather, Bronze, Iron, and the Problem of Preservation

Source: British Museum, “armour; 盔甲,” collection object MAS.610
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_MAS-610
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Mayke Wagner et al., “No Borders for Innovations: A ca. 2700-Year-Old Assyrian-Style Leather Scale Armour in Northwest China,” *Quaternary International* 623
URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1040618221005553
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Archaeological finds include painted leather armor plates from Shang contexts and more leather, bronze, and iron plates from later periods.
- Leather plates could be lacquered, pierced, and joined in varied arrangements.
- A remarkably preserved leather scale armor from Yanghai in northwestern China demonstrates long-distance connections and sophisticated leather construction around 2700 years ago.
- Organic armor survives unevenly, so the material record is strongly affected by decay and burial conditions.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not turn scattered early finds into one standard “Shang uniform.”
- The Yanghai suit is from a northwestern oasis context and has close western Eurasian parallels; it must not be presented as a typical uniform of a central Chinese dynasty.
- The first illustration is not based on a documented workshop, complete suit, or stitching pattern from the Shang or Zhou.
- The sequence leather → bronze → iron → steel is not supported as a clean replacement timeline.

## Lacquered Leather Armor in Eastern Zhou Archaeology

Source: Jiayi Zhang et al., “Identification of the Components of Lacquered Leather Armor from the Warring States Period,” *Coatings* 15.10
URL: https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6412/15/10/1127
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: “曾侯乙墓发掘亲历记,” Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference
URL: https://www.cppcc.gov.cn/zxww/2018/09/21/ARTI1537491271568103.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Scientific analysis of a Warring States fragment confirms a layered lacquer-and-leather construction.
- The tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng contained many decayed leather plates from which specialists reconstructed thirteen suits of human armor and two suits of horse armor.
- Careful numbering and conservation of plate groups are necessary because the original relationships can be lost during excavation.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not assume that every brown plate modeled on a statue must be leather merely because lacquered leather armor existed.
- Reconstructed suits depend on plate position, holes, and comparative analysis; missing cords and decayed backing introduce uncertainty.
- “Leather armor” does not mean an untreated animal pelt wrapped around the body.

## Lamellar and Scale Construction

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Lamellar Shoulder Defenses”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/788894
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: UCL Petrie Museum, “Hide of the Dragon: Achaemenid Scale Armour”
URL: https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/museums/2020/03/06/specimen-of-the-week-392-hide-of-the-dragon-achaemenid-scale-armour/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Lamellar construction uses many pierced plates connected to one another; the material can include leather, bronze, or iron.
- Scale construction commonly arranges overlapping plates on a softer backing.
- Plate size, holes, lacing, backing, and overlap pattern are separate questions from the raw material.

Editorial boundary:

- Modern English and Chinese armor classifications do not always map exactly onto vocabulary used in ancient texts.
- The child-facing article uses “札甲/lamellar” and “鳞甲/scale” as useful structural descriptions, not as claims that every excavated suit fits one uncontested category.
- Do not describe every small-plate armor as “fish-scale armor.”

## What the Terracotta Army Can Show

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/441/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: M. C. Bishop, “The Articulated Cuirass in Qin Dynasty China,” *Antiquity* 63.241
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4D63720D884E6C37D3BFB2852EA58B74/S0003598X00076833a.pdf/articulated_cuirass_in_qin_dynasty_china.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Making the Warrior: The Qin Terracotta Soldiers in *Age of Empires*”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/es/perspectives/terracotta-warriors-age-of-empires
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The terracotta figures provide unusually detailed visual evidence for Qin military organization, clothing, weapons, and modeled armor arrangements.
- Armor plates, cords, collars, belts, and other surface details were modeled in clay and originally painted.
- Figures display different kinds and amounts of armor rather than one uniform set.

Editorial boundary:

- The figures reproduce armor shapes in clay; they are not preserved suits whose plate material can be directly tested.
- Lacquered leather is a strong interpretation for many modeled suits, supported by comparative finds, but the figures do not prove that the whole Qin army's primary armor was leather.
- A funerary army is valuable evidence but not a complete census of battlefield equipment, distribution, or regional variation.
- The second illustration mixes a modern costumed figure with clay warriors and is not an archaeological photograph or reliable reconstruction.

## Qin Stone Armor as a Funerary Model

Source: Neville Agnew, ed., *Conservation of Ancient Sites on the Silk Road*, Getty Conservation Institute, paper on stone armor from the Qin burial complex
URL: https://www.getty.edu/conservation/publications_resources/pdf_publications/pdf/2nd_silkroad5.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Shaanxi local-history compilation, section on the Qin stone armor pit
URL: https://dfz.shaanxi.gov.cn/zslm/fzzlk/dqcs/xas/201803/P020250630548327427771.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Pit K9801 contained numerous limestone armor plates and restored suits connected with copper wire.
- The stone models reproduce plate forms, hole patterns, and articulated construction at full scale.
- Their weight and fragility make ordinary battlefield use implausible; they belong to the funerary complex.

Editorial boundary:

- Stone armor can inform reconstruction of structure, but it is not proof that Qin soldiers fought in stone suits.
- Do not silently transfer every feature of a funerary stone model to leather or iron battlefield armor.

## Han Iron Armor and Reconstruction from Corrosion

Source: Beijing Municipal Government, “大汉玉衣——满城汉墓发掘始末（下）”
URL: https://www.beijing.gov.cn/renwen/pjsx/201801/t20180109_1874801.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Gansu Provincial Department of Culture and Tourism, “一生致力古代甲胄修复（薪火相传）”
URL: https://www.gswbj.gov.cn/a/2022/04/18/13269.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The iron armor from Liu Sheng's Western Han tomb was found corroded and folded together; conservation identified nearly three thousand wrought-iron plates.
- More than ten thousand iron armor fragments from the King of Chu's tomb at Shizishan, Xuzhou, supported reconstructions of lamellar and several scale configurations.
- Holes and cord traces are key evidence when conservators number and reassemble plates.

Editorial boundary:

- These elite tomb finds demonstrate real Han iron armor and structural variety; they do not prove that every Han soldier wore iron armor.
- Do not infer a standard helmet, plume color, or whole-army uniform from the torso finds.
- The third illustration's face-covering helmet and red plume are unsupported modern additions, not a reconstruction of either tomb.

## Iron, Steel, and Cast Iron Coexisted

Source: Mengyi Zhang et al., “Iron Production and Trading in Lingnan during the Qin and Han Dynasties,” *Antiquity* 94.373
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/iron-production-and-trading-in-lingnan-during-the-qin-and-han-dynasties/9EB822B1E9CE3B15A1DB1B6AC54B7970
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Qin- and Han-period iron objects from Lingnan include wrought iron, steel, malleable cast iron, white cast iron, and mottled cast iron.
- Different metallurgical processes and materials already coexisted rather than appearing as one simple iron-then-steel transition.

Editorial boundary:

- The Lingnan study analyzes many categories of objects, not armor alone.
- It supports the correction that “steel came after iron” is too simple; it does not establish the composition of every armor plate or the rate of steel armor use.
- Do not claim that Tang or Song armies suddenly adopted a new, uniform “steel armor” category.

## A Late Leather Armor from Dali

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Armor for the Torso and Hips,” Dali Kingdom
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/658575
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- This rare twelfth- or thirteenth-century armor from Yunnan is made from rigid leather panels coated in red and black lacquer and joined by leather laces.
- It also uses copper-alloy and iron components.
- Its form and construction are distinctive to Yunnan and cannot represent all of China.

Editorial boundary:

- The object demonstrates late survival and regional value of lacquered leather, not technological backwardness.
- Do not turn a unique Dali Kingdom object into a national Song-period uniform.
- Its mixed materials directly contradict a simple model in which leather disappeared when iron or steel became available.

## Existing Images

All four retained illustrations are modern concept art. None is a photograph of an excavated suit or a controlled archaeological reconstruction.

- 3-1-hide-armor-shang-zhou.webp: Retained to show cutting, piercing, and joining leather. The complete vest, stitching, tools, clothing, and workshop setting are invented.
- 3-2-qin-armor-terracotta.webp: Retained as a prompt about reconstructing equipment from sculpture. It combines a living costumed figure with the Terracotta Army and is not an excavation scene.
- 3-3-han-iron-armor.webp: Retained to visualize laced iron plates. The full suit, helmet, face protection, uniform red accents, and plume are not established Han facts.
- 3-4-armor-materials-evolution.webp: Retained only as an example of a misleading linear “upgrade” image. Several cuirasses use European-style solid breastplate silhouettes and cannot stand for Chinese dynastic armor.
