# Hundreds of Little Plates — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. British Museum, “armour; 盔甲,” MAS.616

Source: British Museum, “armour; 盔甲,” MAS.616
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_MAS-616
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The surviving armor scale is made from thick leather reinforced with several coats of red lacquer.
- Ten holes were drilled into the plate so that it could be connected to other pieces.
- The museum discusses archaeological evidence for lacquered leather armor in earlier China as well as bronze and iron scales.

Editorial boundary:

- One surviving plate does not show the complete shape of its original suit or represent all ancient Chinese leather armor.
- Its eighth- to ninth-century date cannot be used to reconstruct a Qin or Han uniform.

## 2. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Armor for the Torso and Hips,” Dali Kingdom

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Armor for the Torso and Hips,” Dali Kingdom
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/658575
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- This rare twelfth- or thirteenth-century armor from Yunnan is made largely from rigid leather panels coated in red and black lacquer.
- Leather laces join its panels, while the suit also contains metal components.
- Lacquered leather continued to be useful long after iron armor had appeared.

Editorial boundary:

- This regional Dali Kingdom object does not represent one uniform worn throughout China.
- Its survival shows that materials could coexist; it does not establish how common this construction was in every army.

## 3. Smarthistory, “The Terracotta Warriors”

Source: Smarthistory, “The Terracotta Warriors”
URL: https://smarthistory.org/the-terracotta-warriors/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The Terracotta Army includes infantrymen, archers, charioteers, cavalrymen, and other military roles with individually modeled armor and clothing.
- The clay figures show different kinds and amounts of armor rather than one identical suit.
- Cavalrymen wear shorter armor over clothing designed to make riding easier.

Editorial boundary:

- The armor is modeled in clay and cannot by itself reveal the material of every real Qin suit.
- A funerary army is important visual evidence, not a complete inventory of the equipment issued to every Qin soldier.

## 4. American Journal of Archaeology, “The First Emperor: China’s Terracotta Army”

Source: Zhixin Jason Sun, “The First Emperor: China’s Terracotta Army,” American Journal of Archaeology
URL: https://ajaonline.org/online-museum-review/369/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The Terracotta Army provides unusually detailed archaeological evidence for Qin military organization, weapons, clothing, and military roles.
- Different figures and groups help distinguish infantry, cavalry, charioteers, archers, and officers.
- The figures belong to the First Emperor’s funerary complex and date to the Qin dynasty.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not use the clay figures to claim that every living soldier owned the same armor.
- Modeled details can show shape and arrangement more securely than they can identify the original armor material.

## 5. China Social Sciences Network, “探寻千年甲胄里的奥秘”

Source: China Social Sciences Network, “探寻千年甲胄里的奥秘”
URL: https://www.cssn.cn/skgz/bwyc/202604/t20260417_5980783.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The Western Han iron armor from Liú Shèng’s tomb was found rolled and rusted into a hard mass.
- Conservator Bái Róngjīn recorded, numbered, and separated the corroded plates layer by layer.
- The reconstructed suit contained 2,859 iron plates, with sleeves and lower sections able to expand and move.

Editorial boundary:

- This elite tomb find demonstrates one sophisticated Han suit, not the armor issued to every Han soldier.
- The complete helmet, plume, colors, and other details in the accompanying illustration are not established by this find.
