# Mingguang Armor — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Early Textual Evidence and Military Use

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Gezhi Jingyuan*, chapter 41, preserving quotations on armor
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=529361&if=en&remap=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Cao Zhi’s memorial lists “black light” and “bright light” armor among suits bestowed by his father.
- Later compiled sources preserve the *Tang Liudian* list of thirteen armor categories, with *mingguang* first.
- The same collected passage quotes a Northern Dynasties record of fine *mingguang* armor for both person and horse.

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Book of Zhou*, chapter 27, Biography of Cai You
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=196119&if=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Cai You is described wearing *mingguang* iron armor at the Battle of Mangshan; enemies called him an “iron beast.”

Source: Chinese Text Project dictionary concordance for 青丝, quoting the *Book of Sui*
URL: https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?char=%E9%9D%92%E7%B5%B2&if=en&remap=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- A Sui ceremonial-military description organizes large cavalry groups by colored silk lacing, *mingguang* human armor, and iron horse armor.

Editorial boundary:

- Texts prove that *mingguang* was an ancient equipment name and provide clues about use and construction. They do not supply a complete diagram or prove that every object now labeled *mingguang* had the same form.
- The article avoids turning Cai You’s literary nickname into a claim that armor gave him supernatural power.

## Museum Identifications of Round-Guard Armor

Source: Palace Museum, “Painted Pottery Warrior Figure”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/sculpture/233388.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: National Museum of China, “Celadon Warrior Figures”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252167.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: National Museum of China, “Sancai-Glazed Pottery Warrior Figure”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252265.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Major Chinese museums conventionally identify Northern Dynasties through Tang warrior figures with paired round or oval chest guards as wearing *mingguang* armor.
- These figures also show such features as shoulder guards, neck protection, waist belts, and hanging armor panels.
- Museum interpretation commonly explains the name through polished, reflective metal guards.

Editorial boundary:

- A pottery figure is evidence for an artistic representation, not a surviving suit of armor. Its modeled surface cannot by itself establish original metal composition, thickness, joining method, or battlefield performance.
- Tomb guardians and Buddhist protectors may combine observed military dress with artistic exaggeration and religious symbolism.

## Scholarly Debate over the Name and Shape

Source: Xia Chaolun and Zhang Jianguang, “素甲日耀：中古时期明光甲的形制和应用,” originally published in *Historical Review* (*Shilin*), 2020.4
URL: https://www.aisixiang.com/data/123674.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- No complete archaeological suit securely identified as *mingguang* armor has been found; most visual evidence consists of sculpture and paintings with their own interpretive limits.
- The authors challenge the standard equation of every round-guard armor with *mingguang* armor.
- Texts refer to *mingguang* armor for both humans and horses, although known horse-armor imagery lacks the paired round guards.
- Some pictured round guards contain scales, faces, or other designs rather than smooth reflective surfaces.
- The article proposes an alternative reconstruction emphasizing pale or bright appearance, fine small plates, silk lacing, lining, paint, and ornament.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a scholarly argument, not a universally accepted replacement for museum terminology. The child-facing article presents both the conventional museum identification and the revisionist interpretation.
- It does not claim that “bright,” “pale,” “fine,” or “decorated” has been proven as the single final meaning of *mingguang*.

## Quantity, Status, and Later Use

Source: Xia Chaolun and Zhang Jianguang, “素甲日耀：中古时期明光甲的形制和应用”
URL: https://www.aisixiang.com/data/123674.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Early *mingguang* armor appears in elite gifts, but later sources describe group issue, a Dunhuang unit inventory of more than twenty suits, and Tang war booty numbered in the thousands.
- Different Tang histories disagree on exact booty totals, showing why large numbers should not be repeated without qualification.
- The name and equipment continued into the Five Dynasties and Song; a Song order added silk lining to prevent finely linked armor from scraping soldiers.
- The category disappears from common records only later, rather than being instantly replaced at the Tang–Song transition.

Editorial boundary:

- “Only generals and elite guards wore it” was removed as an unsupported universal rule.
- The article does not repeat disputed ancient booty totals or treat an inventory as proof of empire-wide issue rates.
- It removes the old one-step story in which Ming cotton armor simply replaced *mingguang* armor. Armor change occurred across long periods, with overlapping forms, terminology, and materials.

## Evidence Survival and Image Limits

Source: “解读中国古代的铠甲,” *Journal of Donghua University (Social Science)* 20.3
URL: https://qkzx.dhu.edu.cn/_upload/article/files/60/0f/8126ba314fa59433f859e45f517a/53112926-3fb8-496e-a4f2-8b7da131dee2.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Complete early Chinese armor is extremely rare; before the Yuan, museum evidence is usually archaeological fragments, restored objects, or reconstructions rather than intact suits preserving their original appearance.
- Historians therefore combine texts, excavated remains, and visual evidence to propose armor forms.

Editorial boundary:

- The story explicitly explains preservation bias: iron corrodes and organic lacing, leather, and padding decay, while pottery, stone, and protected murals may survive.
- It asks whether an image represents equipment, ceremonial dress, or an idealized guardian instead of treating every figure as a troop photograph.

## Images and Unsupported Claims Removed

All four retained pictures are modern imaginative illustrations:

- `4-1-mingguang-armor-sunlight.webp`: the general, palace, cape, complete armor design, and mirror-like landscape reflections are not archaeological documentation.
- `4-2-heart-protecting-mirrors.webp`: the craftspeople, tools, polishing cloths, workshop, and three separate round plates are invented. No reliable support was found for a fixed multi-day polishing process.
- `4-3-cavalry-bright-charge.webp`: the matching riders and unarmored horses are not a reconstruction of the Sui text describing armor for both humans and horses. No reliable evidence was found that reflected light blinded enemy cavalry.
- `4-4-heavy-armor-transition.webp`: the modern stands, teacher, child, and two complete suits are imagined. The dark suit is not identified as the single successor to *mingguang* armor.
