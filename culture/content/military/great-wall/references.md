# The Great Wall Was Never Just One Wall — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “The Great Wall”

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “The Great Wall”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/438/

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The Great Wall was built and rebuilt over a long period, from early walls in the third century BCE through later imperial construction.
- It functioned as a broad military defense system rather than as one wall created in a single project.
- Fortresses, passes, shelters, and beacon-related structures belonged to the wider system.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not present the Great Wall as one continuous surviving structure.
- UNESCO’s page repeats the old claim that the Wall can be seen from the Moon; that claim is not used in this story.

## 2. China Great Wall Heritage, “长城的构成 / Components of the Great Wall”

Source: China Great Wall Heritage, “长城的构成 / Components of the Great Wall”
URL: https://greatwallheritage.cn/CCMCMS/html/1/54/652.html

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Great Wall heritage includes walls made from earth, stone, brick, wood, and other materials.
- The complete defensive system also included forts, passes, trenches, watch structures, living quarters, and beacon towers.
- Beacon towers generally transmitted information with smoke and fire and did not always stand directly on the wall.

Editorial boundary:

- The terms “watchtower,” “enemy tower,” and “beacon tower” are not treated as interchangeable in every place and period.
- The story simplifies many structure types into a child-readable overview without claiming that every section contained every feature.

## 3. China Great Wall Heritage, “Great Wall Resource Survey and Identification”

Source: China Great Wall Heritage, “Great Wall Resource Survey and Identification”
URL: https://www.greatwallheritage.cn/CCMCMS/html/1/54/index.html

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The national survey identified Great Wall remains from the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods through the Ming dynasty.
- Recognized remains are distributed across 15 provincial-level regions and include walls, trenches, individual structures, passes, forts, and beacon towers.
- The survey demonstrates that Great Wall heritage is a large collection of remains from many historical periods rather than one continuous wall.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing story does not use the survey’s total-length figure, because that total combines many different remains and can easily be mistaken for the length of one unbroken wall.
- Survey recognition does not mean that all identified remains survive in the same condition or appearance.

## 4. China Daily / Gansu, “The Great Wall of Man and Nature”

Source: China Daily / Gansu, “The Great Wall of Man and Nature”
URL: https://subsites.chinadaily.com.cn/gansu/2025-03/31/c_1082091.htm

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Western Great Wall remains in the Hexi Corridor can be built from rammed earth, sandstone, and local plants such as desert reeds and red willow.
- Some surviving walls blend into the surrounding Gobi landscape and look very different from Beijing’s brick-and-stone Ming walls.
- Western passes and walls also stood along routes used for travel and exchange.

Editorial boundary:

- The story uses these materials as examples from dry western regions, not as a recipe for every wall in Gansu or every early Great Wall section.
- Describing a wall as earthen does not mean that it was unfinished or less functional in its original setting.

## 5. China Daily, “Guardians of the Great Wall”

Source: China Daily, “Guardians of the Great Wall”
URL: https://epaper.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202308/03/WS64caf5faa310a478839f7686.html

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Han Great Wall remains near Dunhuang survive as alternating layers of fine sand, reeds, and red willow.
- The surviving western remains preserve evidence of military use as well as travel and cultural exchange along the Hexi Corridor.

Editorial boundary:

- The article is used for material and landscape context, not to claim that every western section used exactly the same layers.
- Modern conservation work is outside the scope of the child-facing story.

## 6. Gakusho Nakajima, “The Structure and Transformation of the Ming Tribute Trade System”

Source: Gakusho Nakajima, “The Structure and Transformation of the Ming Tribute Trade System”
URL: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-10-4053-5_7

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Ming trade along the northern and northwestern frontier changed over time and included regulated tribute and mutual-trade arrangements.
- The Ming opened horse markets during periods of agreement with Mongol leaders.
- After the Longqing peace agreement, mutual-trade markets operated with Mongols along the Great Wall.

Editorial boundary:

- The story does not describe frontier trade as permanently open, peaceful, or uncontrolled.
- One market or agreement cannot represent every Great Wall gate, community, or century.

## 7. Qi Meiqin, “The Temporal Characteristics of Border Trading along the Great Wall during the Qing Dynasty”

Source: Qi Meiqin, “The Temporal Characteristics of Border Trading along the Great Wall during the Qing Dynasty”
URL: https://journal.hep.com.cn/fhc/EN/10.1007/s11462-008-0013-4

Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Regulated markets and informal trade both operated along parts of the Great Wall frontier.
- Governments controlled the location, scale, and conditions of border markets.
- Border trade remained important even where the Wall also served military and political purposes.

Editorial boundary:

- The story’s list of horses, livestock, cloth, grain, tea, silk, and other goods is illustrative; no single market necessarily exchanged every item.
- The article avoids turning two millennia of frontier history into one permanent relationship between two fixed groups.
