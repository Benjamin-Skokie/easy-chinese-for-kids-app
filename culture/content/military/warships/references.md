# Ancient Warships — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 《三国志·吴书·周瑜传》 (*Records of the Three Kingdoms*, Biography of Zhōu Yú)

Source: Wikisource transcription of *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, fascicle 54, “Biography of Zhōu Yú”
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E4%B8%89%E5%9C%8B%E5%BF%97/%E5%8D%B754
Accessed: 2026-09-05
Supports: Records Zhōu Yú describing Cáo Cāo’s northern forces as unaccustomed to campaigning on rivers and lakes and suffering illness. It also records Huáng Gài observing that Cáo Cāo’s ships were joined bow to stern, preparing ships filled with dry grass and fuel, pretending to surrender, and launching the fire attack during a strong wind at Red Cliffs.
Editorial boundary: The historical account says the ships were joined bow to stern but does not explain why. The article therefore identifies the seasickness and iron-chain explanation as a later novel’s version rather than established history.

## 《三国演义》第四十七回 (*Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, Chapter 47)

Source: Wikisource transcription of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, Chapter 47
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E4%B8%89%E5%9C%8B%E6%BC%94%E7%BE%A9/%E7%AC%AC047%E5%9B%9E
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- The novel says northern troops were unaccustomed to boats and became ill from the motion.
- It presents Páng Tǒng proposing that large and small ships be linked with iron rings and covered with broad planks to make them steadier.

Editorial boundary:

- This is the novel’s explanation for the connected ships, not a reason stated in the earlier *Records of the Three Kingdoms*.
- The children’s article distinguishes the later literary version from the early historical account.

## 中国国家博物馆，“陶船”

Source: National Museum of China, “Eastern Han Clay Ship”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202110/t20211028_251952.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-05
Supports: Describes the Eastern Han clay ship excavated in Guangzhou, including its stern-mounted rudder, three cabins, anchor, walkways, and structural details.
Editorial boundary: The model provides evidence for the represented ship design; the article does not treat it as a full-size surviving vessel or claim that it proves how every ancient Chinese ship was built.

## 上海交通大学董浩云航运博物馆，“水密隔舱”

Source: C. Y. Tung Maritime Museum, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, “Watertight Bulkheads”
URL: https://maritimemuseum.sjtu.edu.cn/info/1171/5641.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-05
Supports: Explains how bulkheads divide a hull into compartments and help contain flooding, strengthen the vessel, and organize space; identifies the Tang-period wooden vessel excavated at Rugao, Jiangsu, as an early archaeological example.
Editorial boundary: Watertight bulkheads reduce the spread of flooding but do not guarantee that a damaged ship will remain afloat in every situation.

## UNESCO，“Watertight-bulkhead technology of Chinese junks”

Source: UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, “Watertight-bulkhead technology of Chinese junks”
URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/USL/watertight-bulkhead-technology-of-chinese-junks-00321
Accessed: 2026-09-05
Supports: Describes the traditional Chinese method of building wooden ships with independently watertight compartments so damage to one or two cabins does not immediately flood the others.
Editorial boundary: UNESCO documents the traditional craft and its safety principle; the article does not project every detail of the living Fujian craft onto all ancient warships.

## 蓬莱一号古船

Source: Peking University School of Archaeology and Museology, lecture summary on ancient Chinese shipwreck archaeology
URL: https://archaeology.pku.edu.cn/info/1031/5219.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-05
Supports: Describes the Pénglái wreck’s surviving hull, fourteen compartment positions, and finds associated with military use, including weapons and ammunition, supporting its identification as a coastal warship.
Editorial boundary: The wreck supplies direct evidence for this particular vessel and period; missing upper decks, sails, and masts are not reconstructed in the child-facing story.
