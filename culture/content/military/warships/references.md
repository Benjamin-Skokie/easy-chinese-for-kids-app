# Ancient Warships — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Tower ships and the limits of models

Source: Chongqing University, “综述｜汉代的‘楼船’与江海航运”
URL: https://lac.cqu.edu.cn/info/1257/9915.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Han texts and archaeological images can be read together when studying *louchuan* and water transport.
- Images on objects from the Nanyue King tomb and an Eastern Han tomb are relevant evidence for how ships were represented.

Editorial boundary:

- The evidence supports raised structures and tower-ship imagery, not an exact five-story, three-rowed-oar vessel in the fifth century BCE.
- Tomb objects and ancient images are evidence, but they are not scale construction drawings.

## Stern rudder and watertight bulkheads

Source: C. Y. Tung Maritime Museum, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, “舵” and “水密隔舱”
URL: https://maritimemuseum.sjtu.edu.cn/info/1171/5601.htm
URL: https://maritimemuseum.sjtu.edu.cn/info/1171/5641.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- An Eastern Han pottery boat from Guangzhou provides early Chinese visual evidence of a stern rudder.
- Watertight bulkheads limit the spread of flooding and can strengthen a hull; the museum identifies the Tang Rugao wreck as the earliest archaeological example currently known.

Editorial boundary:

- A pottery model is not a surviving full-size rudder, and it does not show that other societies lacked steering technologies.
- Bulkheads reduce danger but do not make a ship unsinkable, and later merchant-ship evidence should not be projected onto every earlier warship.

## A Yuan dynasty warship wreck

Source: Peking University School of Archaeology and Museology, lecture summary on ancient Chinese shipwreck archaeology
URL: https://archaeology.pku.edu.cn/info/1031/5219.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Penglai wreck retained a hull about 28 meters long, with fourteen identified compartment positions.
- Weapons and ammunition supported its interpretation as a maneuverable coastal warship.
- The Quanzhou Southern Song wreck preserves thirteen watertight bulkheads.

Editorial boundary:

- These wrecks provide direct evidence for their own dates and contexts, not a universal blueprint for ancient Chinese ships.
- Reconstructed original dimensions should remain clearly distinct from surviving dimensions.

## Red Cliffs in the early historical record

Source: Chen Shou, *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, fascicle 54, Zhou Yu biography, with Pei Songzhi's annotation quoting *Jiangbiao Zhuan*
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E4%B8%89%E5%9C%8B%E5%BF%97/%E5%8D%B754
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Cao Cao's army suffered illness, lost an early clash, and withdrew to the north bank.
- Huang Gai used vessels filled with dry grass and oil in a false surrender; a strong wind spread fire to ships and shore camps.
- Chen Shou's main text says the ships were “bow and stern connected”; the quoted *Jiangbiao Zhuan* specifies a strong southeast wind.

Editorial boundary:

- The main biography does not establish the later novel's complete iron-chain and seasickness plot.
- Zhuge Liang “borrowing the east wind,” Pang Tong's chain scheme, and detailed dialogue belong to later literary storytelling, not verified battlefield reporting.

## Zheng He's fleet roles and reach

Source: C. Y. Tung Maritime Museum, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, “郑和宝船”
URL: https://maritimemuseum.sjtu.edu.cn/info/1021/3201.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Zheng He's seven voyages used multiple ship categories for command, transport, supply, personnel, and combat functions.
- The fleet should be understood as a complex expedition rather than a group made entirely of one kind of treasure ship or warship.

Editorial boundary:

- Museum reconstructions do not settle the dimensions of the largest treasure ships.
- The voyages involved diplomacy and exchange but should not be described as entirely nonmilitary.

## Treasure-ship dimensions and military action

Source: Southwest University, report on Sally K. Church's lecture, “郑和宝船尺度新探”
URL: https://his.swu.edu.cn/info/1220/3474.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Cambridge University Press, “The Ming–Kotte War in Southeast Asia, 1410–11”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/making-of-the-modern-chinese-navy/mingkotte-war-in-southeast-asia-141011/22CC2980B942006F422B101BE962E6A6
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The very large treasure-ship measurements in later records remain disputed; earlier voyage-related sources do not supply the same dimensions, and engineering objections are part of the debate.
- Zheng He's expedition became involved in military conflict in Sri Lanka, so “purely peaceful” is too simple.

Editorial boundary:

- Sally Church's smaller estimate is a scholarly argument, not a new settled measurement.
- The article should state that size is unresolved rather than choose either the largest traditional figure or one modern reconstruction as fact.
