# Compass — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Shěn Kuò and the magnetized needle

Source: Shěn Kuò, *Mèng Xī Bǐ Tán* (《梦溪笔谈》), juan 24, Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=gb&remap=gb&res=125489
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Rubbing a steel needle with lodestone to magnetize it.
- Allowing the needle to turn by floating it on water, balancing it on a fingernail or bowl rim, or suspending it from a fine silk thread.
- Shěn Kuò's preference for the suspended needle and his observation that it pointed slightly away from exact south.

Editorial boundary:

- The article describes what Shěn Kuò observed but does not claim that he understood Earth's magnetic field in modern scientific terms.
- The illustration is labeled as a modern imagined scene, not a precise reconstruction of his apparatus.

## Early compass use at sea

Source: Zhu Yu, *Píngzhōu Kětán* (《萍洲可谈》), discussed in “Sailing the Waters of East and Southeast Asia: Chinese Navigators and their Material and Mental Tools,” Cambridge University Press
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/sailing-the-waters-of-east-and-southeast-asia-chinese-navigators-and-their-material-and-mental-tools/66D276749D2F189F292E52F29BD5DFEC
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Early twelfth-century Chinese writing records sailors using stars at night, the sun during the day, and the compass when skies were cloudy.
- Later Chinese sailors combined compass directions with route knowledge and written sailing guides.

Editorial boundary:

- The compass is presented as one navigational aid among celestial observations, coastlines, winds, currents, and learned routes.
- The article does not imply that a compass could identify a harbor, reveal underwater hazards, or choose a route by itself.

## Why a compass needle turns

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, “How Does a Compass Work?”
URL: https://www.usgs.gov/media/audio/how-does-compass-work
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A compass needle is a small magnet that can move freely.
- The needle turns to align with Earth's magnetic field.

Editorial boundary:

- The modern magnetic-field explanation is clearly separated from what historical observers could see and record.
- The article says the needle aligns roughly north and south, not that every compass reading is perfectly exact.

## Magnetic declination

Source: U.S. Geological Survey, “Introduction to Geomagnetism”
URL: https://www.usgs.gov/programs/geomagnetism/introduction-geomagnetism
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Magnetic north and geographic north do not usually coincide exactly.
- The difference, called magnetic declination, varies by location.

Editorial boundary:

- The children's article explains the small directional difference without turning the story into a technical navigation lesson.
- It does not treat the needle as pointing directly to the geographic North Pole.

## Compass and maritime navigation

Source: Smithsonian Institution, “Time and Navigation: Magnetic Compass”
URL: https://timeandnavigation.si.edu/timeline2/1000
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Use of the compass for navigation at sea may have begun in China sometime after 1000 CE.
- The magnetic compass became an important instrument for maintaining a ship's course.

Editorial boundary:

- The Smithsonian's cautious “may have begun” wording is preserved rather than converted into an absolute claim about one invention date.
- The article focuses on the well-attested practical value of a reliable directional check when the sky was hidden.
