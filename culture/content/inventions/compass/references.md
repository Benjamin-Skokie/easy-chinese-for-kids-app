# Compass — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Overall chronology and unresolved questions

Source: Huang Xing, “中国指南针史研究文献综述” (Review of Research on the History of the Compass in China), *Journal of Dialectics of Nature*, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences
URL: https://jdn.ucas.ac.cn/home/journal/view/id/3164
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Scholarship identifies two broad approaches to the compass's origin: arguments emphasizing early magnetic-stone evidence and arguments emphasizing the Tang-Song magnetic needle.
- Song sources describe multiple kinds of direction finder and show use on land and at sea.
- The construction and performance of early devices, their artificial magnetization, and the routes by which compass knowledge moved west remain debated.

Editorial boundary:

- The article presents a sequence of surviving evidence, not one unquestioned invention date or named inventor.
- “Developed in China” is not expanded into a fully documented one-way transmission route to the Islamic world and Europe.

## Han *sinan* text and the disputed spoon reconstruction

Source: Wenren Jun, Fudan University Center for Research on Chinese Excavated Classics and Paleography, “王充论‘投之于地’及磁石勺说献疑”
URL: https://www.fdgwz.org.cn/Web/Show/4351
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The received *Lunheng* sentence about the *sinan* is textually and philologically disputed, including readings of the character usually rendered as “spoon.”
- Scholars disagree about what “placing it on the ground” meant and whether the passage describes the familiar lodestone spoon on a diviner's board.
- The spoon model is an interpretation of a short text, not an archaeologically recovered Han compass.

Editorial boundary:

- The article may say that Wang Chong's Eastern Han text mentions a *sinan* whose handle or lower part points south.
- It must not claim that an excavated Han lodestone spoon proves the popular museum reconstruction.
- The image caption explicitly labels the spoon as a modern imagined reconstruction and records the scholarly dispute.

Source: Institute for the History of Natural Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences, publication record for “Experimental Research on the South-Pointing Loadstone Spoon (Sinan)”
URL: https://english.ihns.ac.cn/sourcedb/pbs/202311/t20231107_611549.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Wang Zhenduo produced a modern lodestone-spoon reconstruction in the 1940s based on ancient textual interpretation.
- Later experimental work treats the device as a reconstruction problem rather than an untouched surviving artifact.

Editorial boundary:

- A successful or unsuccessful modern experiment cannot by itself prove the exact form of the Han device.
- No scene, dimension, or plate design from the modern image is described as directly documented in the ancient text.

## The Northern Song south-pointing fish

Source: *Wujing zongyao* (Essential Military Methods), digitized text and scanned edition links at Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=en&res=32116
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Northern Song military manual was completed in 1044 and records a fish-shaped iron direction finder.
- The process involved heating the shaped iron, cooling it while oriented, and floating it on water.
- The device was discussed as an aid for finding direction when troops were lost, alongside the south-pointing carriage.

Editorial boundary:

- The south-pointing fish is not described as a modern pivoted compass or as a fully developed mariner's compass.
- The children's article explains the sequence but does not provide a classroom construction activity.
- Small magnets and sharp needles are not proposed as child experiments.

Source: National Central Library scan of *Wujing zongyao qianji*, hosted by Wikimedia Commons
URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:NCL-05717_%E6%AD%A6%E7%B6%93%E7%B8%BD%E8%A6%81%E5%89%8D%E9%9B%86.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A digitized historical edition preserves the source context for the military manual rather than relying only on modern summaries.

Editorial boundary:

- The surviving edition history is complex; typography and later printing date are not treated as the physical appearance of a 1044 manuscript.

## The magnetic needle and Shen Kuo's observation

Source: Shen Kuo, *Mengxi bitan* (*Dream Pool Essays*), magnetic-needle passage, digitized by Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=gb&remap=gb&res=125489
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shen Kuo records magnetizing a needle by rubbing its point with lodestone.
- He compares floating the needle on water, balancing it on a fingernail or bowl rim, and hanging it by a silk thread.
- He writes that the needle usually points slightly east of south rather than exactly south and notes that some prepared needles point north.

Editorial boundary:

- The passage supports close observation of an offset direction; it does not show that Shen possessed a modern global geomagnetic theory.
- “First discovery of declination” is avoided in the child's text because priority language depends on what counts as a sufficiently clear surviving record.

## Early evidence for use aboard seagoing ships

Source: Zhu Yu, *Pingzhou ketan* (*Pingzhou Table Talks*), digitized by Wikisource
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E8%90%8D%E6%B4%B2%E5%8F%AF%E8%AB%87
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zhu Yu describes Guangzhou maritime practice based on knowledge associated with the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries.
- The pilot watches stars at night, the sun during the day, and the compass in overcast weather.
- The same passage mentions using a long line and hook to retrieve and identify seabed mud.

Editorial boundary:

- The passage does not say sailors abandoned celestial, coastal, depth, wind, or experiential navigation after adopting the compass.
- It is evidence for established use, not a record of the first sailor ever to carry a magnetic needle aboard a ship.

Source: Cambridge University Press, *Journal of Chinese History*, “Sailing the Waters of East and Southeast Asia: Chinese Navigators and their Material and Mental Tools”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/sailing-the-waters-of-east-and-southeast-asia-chinese-navigators-and-their-material-and-mental-tools/66D276749D2F189F292E52F29BD5DFEC
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese navigators used compass directions together with route books, travel time, stars, landmarks, water depth, and accumulated knowledge.
- In later evidence, pilots often brought out the compass when weather removed other directional cues; its importance did not make every other navigational tool obsolete.

Editorial boundary:

- Later rutters and early modern voyage practice illustrate a larger navigational system but are not silently projected unchanged into the first decades of Song compass use.

## Magnetic north, true north, and changing declination

Source: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, “Magnetic Declination (Variation)”
URL: https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/declination.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Magnetic declination is the angle between magnetic north and true north.
- Declination changes with location and time, so compass bearings require local correction for precise navigation.
- A compass aligns with the horizontal component of the local magnetic field; it does not simply point at one fixed magnetic-pole location.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not say a compass “always points exactly north” or “always points exactly south.”
- Modern magnetic-field explanation is clearly separated from what Shen Kuo himself could observe in the eleventh century.

Source: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, “Geomagnetism Frequently Asked Questions”
URL: https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetism-frequently-asked-questions
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Magnetic field strength and direction vary with place and time.
- Nearby magnetic influences can affect readings, and compass reliability becomes poor where the horizontal magnetic-field component is weak.

Editorial boundary:

- The children's article uses these points to correct absolute claims, not to turn the history story into a technical navigation manual.

## The mechanical south-pointing carriage

Source: H. D. Garner, “The Mechanism of China's South-Pointing Carriage,” *NAVIGATION: Journal of the Institute of Navigation*
URL: https://www.ion.org/publications/abstract.cfm?articleID=100137
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ancient references to the carriage are sparse, confusing, and in part legendary.
- Modern reconstructions treat it as a mechanical heading-retention device, not as a magnetic compass.

Editorial boundary:

- The Yellow Emperor tradition is labeled as legend and is not used to date the invention of the magnetic compass.
- A mechanical reconstruction is a reasoned model, not proof of the exact mechanism in every historical carriage.

## Records outside China and transmission uncertainty

Source: Petra G. Schmidl, “Two Early Arabic Sources on the Magnetic Compass,” *Journal of Arabic and Islamic Studies*
URL: https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/jais/volume/docs/vol1/V1_4_Schmidl.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Arabic sources from the thirteenth century describe magnetic direction finders in maritime and astronomical or religious contexts.
- The article places those records beside earlier Chinese evidence and medieval European records.
- Instruments and uses differed across regions, so the history is not one unchanged device moving intact from place to place.

Editorial boundary:

- Chronological order can suggest transmission but cannot alone prove the exact people, ports, and routes involved.
- European and Islamic-world users are described as adapting and developing the technology, not merely copying a finished Chinese object.
