## Chinese *zhūsuàn* and oral formulas

Source: UNESCO, “Chinese Zhusuan, Knowledge and Practices of Mathematical Calculation through the Abacus”
URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chinese-zhusuan-knowledge-and-practices-of-mathematical-calculation-through-the-abacus-00853
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Chinese *zhūsuàn* uses defined formulas to perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and more complicated calculations.
- Its oral formulas use easy-to-learn rhymes to represent calculation rules.
- Chinese Zhusuan was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.

Editorial boundary:

- The article explains formulas as part of traditional instruction but does not reproduce a complete calculation system.
- Claims about educational benefits beyond the story's scope are not added to the children's article.

## Structure of the traditional Chinese abacus

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, “The Chinese Abacus”
URL: https://americanhistory.si.edu/ne/collections/object-groups/the-abacus-the-numeral-frame-and-counters/the-chinese-abacus
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A traditional Chinese *suànpán* has a crossbar, with two beads above it and five below on each rod.
- Upper beads have a value of five and lower beads have a value of one.

Editorial boundary:

- The article describes a traditional form rather than claiming that every Chinese abacus in every period had exactly the same construction.
- The first illustration is used to show the stated bead arrangement, not as evidence for a specific historical date.

## Rods and place value

Source: Science Museum Group, “Chinese Abacus”
URL: https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co59717/chinese-abacus-abacus
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Numbers are represented by moving beads toward the crossbar.
- With one rod chosen as the units rod, neighboring rods represent tens, hundreds, and other place values.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses whole-number examples suitable for children and does not expand into decimal or fractional calculations.
- The explanation distinguishes the value of a bead from the place value of the rod on which it sits.

## Ming-dynasty evidence and commercial use

Source: National Museum of China, “Ivory Abacus” (象牙算盘)
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/ysp/202012/t20201217_248554.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The exact date when the Chinese abacus emerged remains unsettled.
- The Ming-dynasty *Suanfa Tongzong* clearly illustrated the familiar abacus form and recorded calculation formulas.
- By the Ming period, the abacus was widely used in commerce and accounting or statistical work.

Editorial boundary:

- The children's article begins with clear Ming evidence rather than assigning the abacus an unsupported single invention date.
- The shop scene is a modern illustration of historical use, not a reconstruction of one documented shop or merchant.
