# Research Notes: Flying Rainbows: Chinese Kites

This file does not render on the website. It records the sources, supported claims, and editorial boundaries for the published story.

## 《韩非子·外储说左上》 (*Hán Fēizǐ*, “Wài Chǔ Shuō Zuǒ”)

URL: https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/wai-chu-shuo-zuo-shang/zh
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- The text says Mòzǐ spent three years making a wooden kite, which flew for one day before failing.
- Mòzǐ compares it unfavorably with a small wooden cart part that can carry a heavy load for years.

Editorial boundary:
- The article presents this as a story preserved in an ancient text, not as a complete technical description of the first kite.
- It does not claim that the text tells us exactly what the wooden kite looked like.

## National Air and Space Museum, “Conserving the Museum’s Chinese Kites”

URL: https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/conserving-chinese-kites
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Early Chinese kites are associated with wooden bird forms.
- Later traditional kites used bamboo frames with silk or paper coverings.
- Thick split bamboo supplied strength, while thinner flexible pieces formed curved details.

Editorial boundary:
- The article describes a broad change from heavier wood to lighter framed kites without presenting one exact invention date or a single continuous design lineage.
- Material descriptions are simplified for young readers and do not include conservation procedures.

## Smithsonian Folklife Festival, “Kites, Beijing and Jiangsu Province”

URL: https://festival.si.edu/2014/china/to-the-sky/kites/smithsonian
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Swallow-shaped kites are associated with Beijing.
- Nántōng bǎnyào kites can carry carved bamboo or gourd whistles that sound in the wind.
- By about 900 CE, kite flying had become a springtime recreation associated with Qīngmíng.

Editorial boundary:
- “About eleven hundred years ago” is used as a child-friendly approximation of the cited date.
- The story describes regional examples without implying that every Chinese kite follows one design tradition.

## Smithsonian Institution, “Chinese Kite” collection record

URL: https://www.si.edu/object/chinese-kite%3Anmnhanthropology_8468439
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Smithsonian collections preserve nineteenth-century Chinese kites made in representational forms, including a large painted butterfly.
- The collection record supports the article’s description of kites as shaped and painted artworks rather than only plain geometric toys.

Editorial boundary:
- This individual record directly documents a butterfly kite; the story’s wider list of birds, fish, people, and connected forms is treated as a general description of the broader tradition, not as objects all represented by this single record.
