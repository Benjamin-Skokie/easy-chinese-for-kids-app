# Editorial References: Gunpowder

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Safety note: The learner-facing article intentionally omits all usable ratios, preparation procedures, ignition methods, and construction instructions. These sources are for historical verification, not experimentation.

## 1. *Zhenyuan miaodao yaolüe* (真元妙道要略)

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%9C%9F%E5%85%83%E5%A6%99%E9%81%93%E8%A6%81%E7%95%A5
- https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E7%9C%9F%E5%85%83%E5%A6%99%E9%81%93%E8%A6%81%E7%95%A5

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The text warns that heating sulfur, realgar, saltpeter, and honey together produced flames that burned hands and faces and destroyed a building.
- It repeatedly warns of injury, death, poisoning, and ruined households caused by misguided alchemical practices.
- It mentions deaths and severe injuries associated with ingesting mercury-, sulfur-, lead-, and arsenic-related preparations.

Editorial boundary:

- The work is transmitted in the Daoist canon under Zheng Siyuan's name, but its authorship and date are uncertain; its contents are often placed between the late Tang and early Song rather than assigned one unquestioned year.
- The warning is treated as evidence of proto-gunpowder knowledge, not as a modern optimized gunpowder recipe or one identifiable inventor's notebook.
- The learner text paraphrases the hazard and never reproduces an operational procedure.

## 2. *Wujing zongyao* (武经总要), completed in 1044

URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&remap=gb&res=817018
- https://zh.wikisource.org/zh/File:%E6%AD%A6%E7%BB%8F%E6%80%BB%E8%A6%81_%E7%81%AB%E7%90%83%E5%9B%BE.jpg

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Northern Song military compendium preserves the earliest surviving set of explicit gunpowder formulas.
- Its gunpowder-related devices include fireballs, smoke-producing balls, and arrows carrying incendiary material.
- The recipes contain saltpeter, sulfur, carbon-rich substances, and additional materials, and represent organized military knowledge.

Editorial boundary:

- Exact quantities and production instructions are intentionally excluded from the children's article.
- The surviving textual tradition includes later editions; 1044 is the completion date of the original compilation, not the printing date of every extant page or image.
- Terms translated as “cannon,” “bomb,” or “fire arrow” are not assumed to mean modern metal cannon, standardized grenades, or self-propelled rockets.

## 3. Columbia University, Asia for Educators — “Gunpowder”

URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/songdynasty-module/tech-gunpowder.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- *Wujing zongyao* is dated to 1044 and records an early true gunpowder formula and military production.
- Early warfare used gunpowder chiefly as an incendiary.
- Small packets attached to arrows were launched by bows or crossbows.

Editorial boundary:

- Bow-launched fire arrows are kept separate from later self-propelled rockets.
- “First true formula” refers to the earliest surviving explicit formula in this source tradition, not proof that no one used related mixtures earlier.
- The article does not turn a general educational description into a child-usable experiment.

## 4. Academy of Chinese Studies — “Gunpowder and Firearms in Ancient China”

URL: https://chiculture.org.hk/en/china-five-thousand-years/2747

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Gunpowder emerged from collective work by alchemists, pharmacists, artisans, and other workers rather than one named genius.
- The 1044 manual contains three early military formulas and records fireballs, smoke weapons, and gunpowder arrows.
- Northern and Southern Song weapons developed from incendiaries toward tube-shaped fire weapons and explosive devices.
- Gunpowder knowledge later circulated beyond China.

Editorial boundary:

- Broad claims that the Mongols transmitted the technology west are qualified using other scholarship; no single proven route or traveler is asserted.
- National “invention” language is not converted into a story of one Chinese person acting alone.
- The article distinguishes evidence for ingredients, military application, firearm form, and later global adoption.

## 5. *Kaixi De'an shouyu lu* (开禧德安守御录)

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/%E9%96%8B%E7%A6%A7%E5%BE%B7%E5%AE%89%E5%AE%88%E5%9F%8E%E9%8C%84
- https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%AE%88%E5%9F%8E%E9%8C%84

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The record of the 1132 defense of De'an describes more than twenty long-bamboo-shaft fire lances.
- Two people handled each device, and they were prepared for use against siege bridges and structures approaching the city.
- The work survives through a later compilation and manuscript tradition.

Editorial boundary:

- The device is described as a pole-mounted, tube-like flame weapon, not a modern rifle.
- The text does not support the modern illustration's child observer, metal bands, demonstration range, or nearby cannon.
- Later projectile-scattering fire lances are not silently inserted into every 1132 device.

## 6. Song-Jin explosive weapons and the gradual firearm transition

URL: https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-creighton-century-1907-2007/section/9e1a027f-5c02-4d6a-9df6-3f687329092d
- https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/21/75/84/61/2021758461/2021758461.pdf
- https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%9C%87%E5%A4%A9%E9%9B%B7

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Joseph Needham's survey links the development of the gun to substantial bamboo or paper fire-lance tubes while stressing intervening stages.
- Jin historical material describes fire lances and, in the 1232 siege of Kaifeng, iron-cased “thunder crash” bombs.
- Thirteenth-century technology increasingly used more explosive powder and stronger containers.

Editorial boundary:

- Vivid range, sound, and destructive-effect claims in dynastic histories may be rhetorical and are not repeated as laboratory measurements.
- The fire lance is not called “the first modern gun.”
- The article presents a broad developmental direction while acknowledging disputed object dates and incomplete technological lineages.

## 7. Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum — “The First Fireworks: Origins of the Rocket”

URL: https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/first-fireworks-origins-rocket

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Early Chinese terms for fire arrows, flying fire lances, rockets, and fireworks can be ambiguous and change across time.
- A secure identification of a rocket requires evidence that it moved by self-propulsion rather than a bow, throwing device, or human hand.
- Thirteenth-century descriptions of self-moving “ground rat” fireworks offer clearer evidence of reaction propulsion.
- Gunpowder and rocket development occurred through empirical experimentation rather than one planned invention event.

Editorial boundary:

- The Smithsonian's broad accidental-discovery narrative is qualified by the primary-text dating issues above.
- No single “first rocket” date is claimed where terminology remains ambiguous.
- The learner text explains the distinction conceptually without giving construction details.

## 8. Tonio Andrade, *The Gunpowder Age*

URL: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781400874446/html
- https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctvc77j74.1
- https://www.tonioandrade.com/_files/ugd/88be10_6d1a981ca79946488ba646c3630d550a.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese gunpowder and firearm development preceded clearer firearm evidence in West Asia and Europe.
- Firm evidence for firearms in Iran, Central Asia, and western Islamic regions is later and uneven; early-fourteenth-century European evidence is clearer.
- Gunpowder weapons did not erase earlier arms immediately and developed through sustained competition, adaptation, state finance, and production.
- Later improved firearm technology also moved eastward, including back into China.

Editorial boundary:

- A broad east-to-west chronology is not turned into one certain “Silk Road route.”
- The timing of a formula's appearance, the arrival of a firearm, and the widespread military use of cannon are treated as different questions.
- No region's craftspeople are portrayed as passive recipients incapable of local invention and adaptation.

## 9. Comparative scholarship on Eurasian transmission

URL: https://academic.oup.com/book/756/chapter/135406976
- https://academic.oup.com/reference/62399/reference-article-abstract/555375223
- https://journals.rcsi.science/2308-152X/article/view/352766
- https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/files/89635254/ACCEPTED_VERSION_Firearms.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Many historians identify Mongol conquest and empire-wide networks as major conduits for gunpowder knowledge.
- Movement of specialists, military encounters, and translation in Central and West Asia were likely important.
- The chronology and interpretation of particular Middle Eastern accounts remain disputed, especially before the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Editorial boundary:

- “The Mongols spread gunpowder” is presented as an influential explanation, not a complete manifest of every route and traveler.
- Trade, captives, artisans, envoys, warfare, and independent adaptation are retained as overlapping possibilities.
- The story does not claim that powder recipes and metal-barrel guns necessarily traveled together.

## 10. Fireworks and later civilian blasting

URL: https://ssec.si.edu/node/205
- https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/cen-09527-scitech3
- https://www.mininghistoryassociation.org/Journal/MHJ-v15-2008-Siems.pdf
- https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Blasting

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Gunpowder and related pyrotechnics developed celebratory uses involving light and sound as well as military uses.
- Black powder later served as a blasting agent in quarrying, mining, tunneling, and road work.
- Documented European mining trials in 1627 and a fatal accident in 1631 show both the later civil use and its extreme hazards.

Editorial boundary:

- A general discussion of later global use is not claimed as proof that every application began in Song China.
- Fireworks are not called ancient child-safe toys, and the learner article gives no method for making them.
- Mining sources are used only to support the existence and consequences of civil blasting; operational instructions are excluded.
