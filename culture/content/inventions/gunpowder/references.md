# Research Notes: Gunpowder: From Alchemy to Fireworks

This file does not render on the website. It records the sources, supported claims, and editorial boundaries for the published story.

## Ho Peng Yoke, “Gunpowder”

URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4020-4425-0_9774
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Chinese alchemists sought powerful medicines and elixirs, including preparations associated with longevity and immortality.
- The *Zhenyuan miaodao yaolüe* preserves a warning about burns and a building fire caused by heated materials including sulfur and saltpeter.
- Gunpowder developed gradually from this alchemical setting.

Editorial boundary:
- The accident is paraphrased without quantities or operational preparation steps.
- The story does not identify one inventor or treat the warning as a complete mature gunpowder formula.

## Joseph Needham, “The Guns of Kaifêng-Fu: China's Development of Man's First Chemical Explosive”

URL: https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-creighton-century-1907-2007/section/9e1a027f-5c02-4d6a-9df6-3f687329092d
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- A ninth-century Daoist warning describes a mixture that scorched experimenters and burned a building.
- The warning belongs near the beginning of the history of gunpowder.
- Gunpowder weapons developed across centuries rather than appearing all at once.

Editorial boundary:
- Needham's broad historical reconstruction is used for context, not to present one uncontested invention date.
- The children's story omits recipes, weapon construction, and disputed technical details.

## Peter A. Lorge, *The Asian Military Revolution: From Gunpowder to the Bomb*

URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/asian-military-revolution/1F83478D541811809B78D03427E1226A
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- The *Wujing Zongyao*, completed in 1044, is the earliest surviving text to directly record gunpowder formulas.
- Gunpowder weapons changed and became more powerful over many generations.

Editorial boundary:
- The article uses the book for broad chronology and gradual development.
- It does not turn the sequence of incendiaries, flame weapons, rockets, and later firearms into one instant invention.

## National Air and Space Museum, “The First Fireworks: Origins of the Rocket”

URL: https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/first-fireworks-origins-rocket
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Chinese alchemical experimentation contributed to the development of gunpowder.
- Fire arrows carrying burning material preceded clearer self-propelled rockets.
- Gunpowder technology later contributed to rockets and fireworks.

Editorial boundary:
- The story distinguishes bow-launched fire arrows from later gunpowder-powered rockets.
- It describes what the devices looked like without providing construction instructions.

## *Wujing Zongyao* (《武经总要》), Chinese Text Project

URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&remap=gb&res=817018
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- This eleventh-century official military work is associated with the earliest surviving written gunpowder formulas.
- It documents gunpowder knowledge entering an official military context.

Editorial boundary:
- The source supports the article's broad statement about an official military book recording several formulas.
- No formula, ratio, or weapon-making direction is reproduced in the children's story.
