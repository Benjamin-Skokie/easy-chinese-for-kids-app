# Research Notes: How Did Tree Bark Become Paper?

This file does not render on the website. It records the sources, supported claims, and editorial boundaries for the published story.

## 《后汉书·宦者列传》 (*Book of the Later Han*, “Biographies of Eunuchs”)

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E5%BE%8C%E6%BC%A2%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B778
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- The received history says Cài Lún used tree bark, hemp waste, worn cloth, and fishing nets to make paper.
- It records that he presented the work to the court in 105 CE and that the material later became known as “Marquis Cai paper.”
- The passage contrasts costly silk and heavy bamboo slips with the usefulness of paper.

Editorial boundary:
- The *Book of the Later Han* was compiled centuries after Cài Lún’s lifetime, so it is treated as a major historical account rather than an eyewitness record.
- The wording does not prove that Cài Lún worked alone or created the first paper from nothing.

## University of Oxford, “Origins of Paper”

URL: https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/origins-paper
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Archaeological paper fragments predate Cài Lún’s lifetime and the court record of 105 CE.
- Cài Lún’s importance can be understood through improvement and promotion rather than as the sole first inventor.
- Bamboo was heavy and silk costly as writing materials.

Editorial boundary:
- The article states only the broad conclusion that paper existed before Cài Lún; it does not claim that one disputed fragment settles the exact identity of the first paper.
- Archaeological dates and uses are summarized without presenting a single object as the beginning of the entire craft.

## University of Oxford, “The Ancient Papermaking Process”

URL: https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/ancient-papermaking-process
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Plant material was softened, cooked, broken into fibers, suspended in water, lifted on a fine screen, pressed, and dried.
- Chinese papermaking used varied plant materials, including inner-bark fibers, hemp, bamboo, and straw.
- Finished paper could be thin, lightweight, folded, or rolled.

Editorial boundary:
- The story explains the central fiber-and-water principle rather than presenting a complete historical workshop recipe.
- Processes and ingredients varied across time, place, and paper type; the illustration is a simplified modern reconstruction.

## UNESCO, “Traditional Handicrafts of Making Xuan Paper”

URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/traditional-handicrafts-of-making-xuan-paper-00201
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Xuan paper is handmade from the inner bark of the blue sandalwood tree and rice straw.
- Traditional production includes many stages such as soaking, washing, pulping, drying, and cutting.
- Chinese papermakers shaped different fibers into strong, smooth sheets used for calligraphy, painting, and printing.

Editorial boundary:
- Xuan paper is one specialized living tradition, not a universal recipe for every kind of Chinese paper.
- Its exceptionally long and complex production process is not projected backward unchanged onto Han-period papermaking.

## Academy of Chinese Studies, “Papermaking”

URL: https://chiculture.org.hk/en/china-five-thousand-years/2746
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:
- Paper is made by treating plant fibers, dispersing them into pulp, forming a sheet, and removing water.
- Paper existed in China before Cài Lún, while his innovations expanded usable materials and improved practical production.
- Bamboo and wooden slips, silk, and paper continued to overlap before paper became the dominant everyday writing material.

Editorial boundary:
- Cài Lún is described as an important improver and promoter, not the proven sole inventor of paper.
- The article avoids assigning one exact moment or individual to a craft that developed through repeated experimentation.
