# Research references: Paper Making

This is a private editorial research record. It does not render on the website.

Accessed: 2026-08-21

## 1. Fan Ye, *Book of the Later Han*, biography of Cai Lun

- Digital text portal: [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/)
URL: https://ctext.org/
- Corroborating quotation and textual discussion: [Toyama University, annotated *Shuowen Jiezi* study](https://www.hmt.u-toyama.ac.jp/uploads/moriga79.pdf)
- Supports: the received account that Cai Lun used tree bark, hemp waste, worn cloth, and fishing nets; presented the work in the first year of Yuanxing (105 CE); later paper was called “Caihou paper.”
- Editorial boundary: Fan Ye compiled the *Book of the Later Han* in the fifth century, more than three centuries after Cai's death. It is a major textual source, not a contemporary eyewitness report. The wording does not establish that Cai worked alone or created the first fiber sheet from nothing.

## 2. T. H. Barrett, “The Woman Who Invented Notepaper: Towards a Comparative Historiography of Paper and Print”

- [Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society, Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/woman-who-invented-notepaper-towards-a-comparative-historiography-of-paper-and-print/F4EDF2184F186400C08920F227007F29)
- Supports: archaeological evidence for forms of paper before 105 CE; early paper may often have served wrapping or other purposes; Cai Lun's recorded 105 presentation of improved writing paper remains historically significant.
- Boundary: this article discusses comparative historiography rather than providing a catalogue of every archaeological fragment.

## 3. Smithsonian Museum Conservation Institute abstracts on early Chinese paper

- [Pan Jixing, “On the origin of papermaking in the light of scientific research on recent archaeological discoveries”](https://mci.si.edu/node/1246765)
- [Wang Juhua and Li Yuhua, analysis of Han paper specimens](https://mci.si.edu/node/1184995)
- Supports: arguments that processed hemp/ramie papers predate 105 CE; scientific examination of fiber length and processing; the competing conclusion that Baqiao material may be compressed fiber padding rather than true formed paper.
- Boundary: these abstracts preserve conflicting technical interpretations. The story therefore does not present Baqiao as an uncontested “first paper.”

## 4. Harumi Kubo, review of the scholarly debate on paper before Cai Lun

- [History of Science Society of Japan, J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jhsj/47/247/47_150/_article/-char/ja/)
- Supports: the existence of an extended scholarly dispute over pre-Cai paper and calls for additional independent scientific analysis.
- Boundary: used to frame uncertainty, not to deny all pre-105 evidence.

## 5. Cornell University Library, Paper Preservation Tutorial

- [“Paper”](https://chinapreservationtutorial.library.cornell.edu/content/paper/)
- Supports: technical definition of paper as an interwoven mat formed by draining a suspension of cellulose fibers through a screen; drying and fiber bonding; historical variation in raw materials and later mechanization.
- Boundary: its short history repeats the traditional 105 CE origin date, which the story qualifies with archaeological scholarship.

## 6. Canadian Conservation Institute, “Caring for paper objects”

- [Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/paper-objects.html)
- Supports: paper as felted sheets of plant fibers containing cellulose; historical use of cotton, linen, straw, and wood; modern shift toward wood pulp.
- Boundary: used for materials science, not the attribution of invention.

## 7. Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Papyrus-Making in Egypt”

- [The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/papyrus-making-in-egypt)
- Supports: papyrus was made from the papyrus plant by a distinct strip-layering process and long predates Chinese paper.
- Boundary: papyrus is a writing support but is not made by dispersing and reforming individual fibers into a felted sheet.

## 8. UNESCO Silk Roads Programme, paper and knowledge transfer

- [“The Importance of Paper Making Technology in Cultural Exchange along the Silk Roads”](https://fr.unesco.org/silkroad/node/11083)
- [“The Invention and Transfusion of Printing Technology in East Asia”](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/did-you-know-invention-and-transfusion-printing-technology-east-asia-and-its-implications)
- Supports: multi-regional spread to the Korean Peninsula, Japan, Central Asia, the Islamic world, North Africa, Iberia, and other parts of Europe; connection between paper and printing.
- Boundary: the story avoids exact single-year arrival claims where transmission was gradual and evidence varies by region.

## 9. Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Paper ii. Paper and Papermaking”

- [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/paper-and-papermaking/)
- Supports: paper use and production in Central Asia and Iran; Jonathan Bloom's source-critical rejection of the simple claim that Chinese prisoners captured at Talas uniquely introduced papermaking.
- Boundary: prisoners may have contributed skills, but the later captive story cannot prove a single origin event.

## 10. Smithsonian Folklife Festival, “Paper”

- [Smithsonian](https://festival.si.edu/2002/the-silk-road/paper/smithsonian)
- Supports: scholarly doubt that 751 was the sole moment when papermaking crossed westward; technology could have moved before the Battle of Talas.
- Boundary: absence of surviving early non-Chinese examples limits certainty about exact workshops and dates.

## 11. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, European Paper Mills

- [European Paper Mills tentative-list dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6767/)
- Supports: artisan paper production became widespread in Europe from the thirteenth century onward and had links through earlier African and Mediterranean papermaking traditions.
- Boundary: a heritage nomination is used for the broad chronology of mills, not as proof of a single transmission route.

## 12. U.S. Environmental Protection Agency resources on paper life cycles

- [Water footprint of paper, EPA HERO record](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/3298587/)
- [Paper Task Force environmental comparison, EPA archive](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/conserve/tools/warm/pdfs/EnvironmentalDefenseFund.pdf)
- Supports: paper production has water, forestry, energy, emissions, recycling, and disposal dimensions; recycled and virgin fiber choices involve multiple tradeoffs.
- Boundary: exact impacts vary by fiber source, mill technology, energy mix, transport, product, and local recovery system. The story therefore avoids universal claims that one paper choice is always best.

## Editorial boundaries used in the story

- Cai Lun is described as a recorded improver, organizer, and promoter—not the proven lone inventor of paper.
- The 105 CE account is attributed to a later-compiled history rather than treated as a contemporary diary.
- Pre-105 fiber sheets are acknowledged, while disputed dating, processing, and use are stated.
- Papyrus, parchment, bamboo/wood slips, silk, and true felted-fiber paper are kept materially distinct.
- Bark, hemp waste, cloth, and fishing nets are not presented as a universal or mandatory recipe.
- Talas is not treated as a one-battle “secret transfer.”
- Paper's contribution to books and printing is not equated with automatic literacy or social equality.
- Environmental claims use a life-cycle frame and do not call paper cost-free, infinitely recyclable, or always preferable.
- All three illustrations are retained as modern imaginative or simplified process scenes, not documentary reconstructions.
