# 汉字是画出来的吗？ — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art — “Making Sense of the Future: The Oracle Bone and Shang Dynasty Divination”

URL: https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/for-educators/teaching-china-with-the-smithsonian/lesson-plans/making-sense-of-the-future-the-oracle-bone-and-shang-dynasty-divination/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Shang divination used cattle scapulae and turtle plastrons prepared with hollows, heated until they cracked, and then interpreted.
- Surviving inscriptions address subjects including rain, harvests, warfare, hunting, illness, childbirth, and offerings.
- A record could include an introduction, a charge or question, a prognostication, and a later verification.

Editorial boundary:

- Statements about spirits and royal ancestors describe Shang beliefs, not independently verified supernatural events.
- The story uses child-facing language for the process; practices and inscription formats varied.

## 3. Smithsonian Institution — “Inscribed Turtle Plastron”

## 4. Columbia University, Asia for Educators — “Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of the Late Shang Dynasty: On Childbearing”

URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_china-oracle-bone-childbearing.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A late-Shang inscription records a divination about Fù Hǎo’s childbirth and a verification added thirty-one days later stating that she gave birth to a girl.

Editorial boundary:

- The source’s historical translation also records the king’s gendered judgment. The children’s story reports the birth outcome without repeating or endorsing that value judgment.

## 5. Columbia University, Asia for Educators — “Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of the Late Shang Dynasty”

URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/ps/cup/oracle_bone_general.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The translated inscriptions include divinations about Wǔ Dīng’s sick tooth and whether particular ancestors were responsible.

Editorial boundary:

- Ancestral causation was the diviners’ proposed explanation within Shang belief; it is not presented as a medical diagnosis.

## 6. Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art — “Oracle Bone Script”

URL: https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/chinas-calligraphic-arts/oracle-bone-script/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Oracle-bone script is the earliest known systematic form of Chinese writing and was used from approximately the fourteenth through eleventh centuries BCE.
- The inscriptions were carved on animal bones and turtle shells for Shang divination.

Editorial boundary:

- “Earliest known” refers to currently documented systematic Chinese writing, not proof that no earlier writing ever existed.

## 7. Herbert F. Johnson Museum of Art, Cornell University — “Oracle Bone with Inscription”

URL: https://emuseum.cornell.edu/objects/24632/oracle-bone-with-inscription
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A surviving ox-bone fragment from about 1200–1045 BCE preserves carved script and burn marks from divination.
- The object confirms the material form and archaeological reality of Shang oracle bones.

Editorial boundary:

- The current object record supports the artifact description but not every detail of the nineteenth-century pharmacy discovery story.

## 8. University of California, Berkeley — Oral History with David N. Keightley

URL: https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/218134/files/keightley_david.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Keightley recounts the 1899 Wang Yirong pharmacy discovery account explicitly as a tradition.

Editorial boundary:

- The medicine-shop episode is a famous and widely repeated discovery narrative. The story therefore says “A famous story tells” / “一个流传很广的故事说” rather than presenting every anecdotal detail as fully documented history.

## 9. Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art — “Understanding Chinese Characters”

URL: https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/for-educators/teaching-china-with-the-smithsonian/lesson-plans/understanding-chinese-characters/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Chinese character forms evolved over time, and the Qin dynasty standardized the writing system further.
- Written forms can remain recognizable across different pronunciations.

Editorial boundary:

- The story says speakers “might” or “often” recognize the same written character. It does not claim that all regional speech varieties use identical vocabulary or writing in every context.

## 10. Princeton University — “The 6 Formation of Characters”

URL: https://commons.princeton.edu/chinesecharacters/the-6-formation-of-characters/
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Some characters are pictographic, while many characters combine a component that suggests meaning with one that suggests sound.

Editorial boundary:

- Pictographs are only one part of Chinese writing. By the oracle-bone period, the script could already express far more than simple pictures.

## Editorial synthesis

- The supplied Chinese and English manuscripts remain the final writing base. Only excess paragraph breaks were merged for page flow; the story’s wording and claims were not rewritten.
- The pharmacy discovery is clearly framed as a famous story or tradition. Oracle-bone artifacts, inscriptions, dates, and divination topics are treated as history and archaeology. Spirits’ and ancestors’ answers are identified as Shang belief.
- The pictographic opening of the character story is balanced by evidence that oracle-bone writing was already a developed system, including phonetic and semantic strategies.
- Scholarly material was used to verify claims and set boundaries, not added automatically as story copy.
