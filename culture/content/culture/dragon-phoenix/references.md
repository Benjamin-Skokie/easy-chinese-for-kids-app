# 龙和凤凰：中国最吉祥的伙伴 — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. The British Museum — “Dish” (PDF.593)

URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_PDF-593
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- In Chinese tradition, dragons are regarded as auspicious rather than primarily frightening.
- Dragons were understood as controllers of the water supply and later came to symbolize imperial authority.
- The object is a Ming-period porcelain dish made for imperial ceremonial use and decorated with two five-clawed dragons and clouds.

Editorial boundary:

- The dragon’s control of water belongs to traditional belief and symbolism, not natural history.
- The story compares broad tendencies in Chinese and European dragon traditions; it does not claim that every Chinese dragon is benevolent or that every European dragon is a monster.

## 2. The Metropolitan Museum of Art — “Panel with Five Phoenixes in a Garden”

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/42122
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The *fènghuáng*, though commonly translated as “phoenix,” is a distinct Chinese mythical bird with different associations from the fire-and-rebirth phoenix.
- It was regarded as the noblest of feathered creatures and was said to appear in times of peace and prosperity.
- The phoenix appeared in art commissioned by imperial courts.

Editorial boundary:

- The appearance of the phoenix in peaceful times is presented as tradition or legend, not as a historical sighting.
- Descriptions of its beauty and character are artistic interpretation rather than zoological fact.

## 3. The Metropolitan Museum of Art — “Dish with Dragon and Phoenix”

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/42543
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- On this late-sixteenth- to early-seventeenth-century porcelain dish, a five-clawed dragon associated with the emperor is paired with a phoenix often associated with the empress.
- Dragon-and-phoenix imagery appeared together on imperial porcelain centuries ago.

Editorial boundary:

- The emperor/empress association is historically grounded in imperial art, but the pair has carried other meanings in other periods and settings.
- “Strength and grace” and “power and peace” are child-facing summaries of the broader symbolic contrast, not fixed dictionary definitions for every dragon-and-phoenix image.

## 4. The Metropolitan Museum of Art — “Panel with Dragon, Phoenix, and Peonies”

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/62892
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A sixteenth-century silk-and-metal-thread tapestry depicts a dragon and phoenix together.
- The surrounding phoenix, peonies, rock, and *lingzhi* were read as symbols of an age of peace and prosperity.
- Dragon-and-phoenix designs appeared on silk as well as porcelain.

Editorial boundary:

- The story uses this object as evidence for a long artistic pairing, not proof that the pairing always had one unchanging meaning.

## 5. The Palace Museum — “龙凤呈祥——清代皇帝的婚姻”

URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/topic/wedding_index.html
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Dragon-and-phoenix language and imagery were part of Qing imperial wedding culture.
- Imperial weddings were state ceremonies with elaborate ritual and visual symbolism.

Editorial boundary:

- This source concerns Qing imperial marriages. It supports the historical wedding connection but does not by itself describe every regional or present-day Chinese wedding custom.

## 6. The British Museum — “Dragon and Phoenix Charm”

URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1999-0802-377
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- An eighteenth-century Chinese charm pairs a dragon and phoenix as symbols of a harmonious union.
- The object was probably associated with marriage.

Editorial boundary:

- This object demonstrates one documented marriage use; it does not establish a single origin date for the broader custom.

## 7. The British Museum — “Money Envelope; Hongbao”

URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_2014-4133-11
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- A circa-2014 red wedding envelope carries dragon-and-phoenix imagery, a double-happiness character, and gold-and-red decoration.
- The pairing continued to appear in a modern wedding context.

Editorial boundary:

- The object was acquired in London and documents one modern example, not the design of all contemporary Chinese wedding invitations or decorations.

## 8. Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding — “The First Newborn ‘Dragon-Phoenix’ Twin Pandas in 2007 Named and Adopted”

URL: https://www.panda.org.cn/en/news/news/2023-07-06/5721.html
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- “Dragon-phoenix twins” is used in Chinese for a male-and-female twin pair.

Editorial boundary:

- The story describes this as an affectionate everyday expression, not a medical category or a universal family belief.

## Editorial synthesis

- The story’s central movement—from water and agriculture, to imperial symbolism, to the phoenix, and finally to the paired wedding image—is consistent across `page.md`, `zh.md`, and `en.md`.
- Dragons and phoenixes are consistently identified as imagined or mythical creatures.
- Claims about the phoenix appearing under a good ruler are labeled as tradition or legend.
- The story’s descriptions of mood and contrast are literary explanations of symbolism, not claims that every historical object carried precisely the same interpretation.
- The supplied Chinese and English manuscripts remain the final writing base. Added section headings organize the webpage but do not change the story’s claims.
