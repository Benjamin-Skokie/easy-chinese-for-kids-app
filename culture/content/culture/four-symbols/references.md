## 1. 故宫博物院 — “天地之间”

Source: The Palace Museum, “天地之间”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/topic/zhongguo_skyearth.html
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- The Four Symbols are animal images associated with four regions of the sky: eastern Azure Dragon, western White Tiger, southern Vermilion Bird, and northern Xuanwu.
- Han-dynasty roof-end tiles frequently used Four Symbols designs.
- A *wǎdāng* is the round or semicircular decorated end attached to the front of a curved roof tile at the eaves.
- The four animal images grew from ancient interpretations of star groups rather than from a zoological creature list.

Editorial boundary:

- The story treats the animals as symbolic images for sky regions, not as literal outlines or photographs hidden among the stars.
- The generated illustrations are not reconstructions of a precise ancient star chart or exact Palace Museum objects.

## 3. 中国国家博物馆 — Han gray pottery

Source: National Museum of China, “彩绘青龙、白虎、朱雀纹陶壶”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202110/t20211027_251910.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- Gray pottery was the main Han pottery tradition and commonly appeared blue-gray.
- Some Han pottery was painted after firing, so color cannot be ruled out for every pottery object.

Editorial boundary:

- This source describes a painted burial jar, not a Four Symbols roof-end tile.
- The illustration therefore uses one cautious warm-gray clay material and does not invent modern blue, red, white, and black color-coding for the four roof-tile reliefs.

## 4. 故宫博物院 — “和林访古（下）”

Source: The Palace Museum, “和林访古（下）”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/historical/talk/208558.html
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- Pointed drip tiles first appeared at the front of flat roof tiles during the Tang dynasty.
- On later roofs, flat tiles with drip ends and curved tiles with *wǎdāng* ends work together to carry rain and snow away from the eaves and walls.

Editorial boundary:

- The roof illustration is presented as a Han-period teaching scene, so it omits the later Tang drip tiles entirely.
- The later alternating structure is retained here as an illustration research note, not a detour in the child-facing story.

## 6. 《淮南子·天文训》— Direction, season, phase, and animal correspondences

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Huainanzi*, “Tianwen Xun”
URL: https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=gb&node=3060&remap=gb&show=parallel
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- East is associated with wood, spring, and the Azure Dragon.
- South is associated with fire, summer, and the Vermilion Bird.
- West is associated with metal, autumn, and the White Tiger.
- North is associated with water, winter, and Xuanwu.

Editorial boundary:

- These are historical cosmological correspondences, not statements of modern astronomy or natural science.
- The child-facing circle of directions and seasons summarizes the source without presenting every additional deity, planet, musical pitch, stem, or cosmological category in the passage.

## 7. 故宫博物院 — “四神将”

Source: The Palace Museum, “四神将”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/239601.html
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- By the Warring States period, the twenty-eight lunar mansions and Four Symbols system was already known.
- The twenty-eight mansions were divided into four directional groups of seven.
- Daoist tradition later personified the Four Symbols as guardian generals.

Editorial boundary:

- “Station in the sky” is a child-facing analogy for *xiù*, not a literal translation or a claim that each mansion is one star.
- The article avoids giving a single invention date or inventor for a system that developed over time.

## 8. 故宫博物院 — “玄武纹砖”

Source: The Palace Museum, “玄武纹砖”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/sculpture/233922.html
Accessed: 2026-09-27

Supports:

- Xuanwu is traditionally shown as a tortoise entwined with a snake.
- Xuanwu is the northern member of the Four Symbols.
- Four Symbols imagery was widely used on Han mirrors, lacquerware, bricks, tiles, seals, and other objects.

Editorial boundary:

- Xuanwu is not identified with Xuánguī, the separate creature described in *Shan Hai Jing*.
- The story describes the tortoise-and-snake image without expanding into the much later history of the personified Daoist god Zhenwu.

## Editorial synthesis

- The specific *Liji* and *Huainanzi* passages remain named and linked in these references. The child-facing story explains the supported ideas without a second, repetitive tour through the bibliography.
- “Four Symbols” is the main child-facing term. “Four Gods” appears in museum terminology, but adding both names throughout would burden the story without changing its central idea.
- Zhūquè is the southern red bird throughout. The article does not rename it a phoenix or add a separate phoenix comparison that distracts from the sky-to-rooftop thread.
- The illustrations use modern picture-book art to explain historical ideas. They are not evidence and do not reproduce exact star positions or named artifacts.
