# Chinese Spirit in Idioms: Five Small Stories

Chinese people have many small stories passed down from generation to generation.

Some are only a few sentences long. But behind them are things people have respected for a very long time.

Later, these stories became idioms: often only four Chinese characters, but carrying a whole way of thinking about life.

---

## Grinding an Iron Rod into a Needle: Li Bai Skips School

When Li Bai was young, he hated studying.

One day, he secretly slipped out of school and ran into the mountains to play.

By a small stream, he saw an old woman with white hair sitting on a rock. In her hand was an iron rod. She dipped it in the stream and rubbed it against the stone:

*sha... sha...*

Li Bai was curious.

"Grandmother, what are you grinding?"

Without lifting her head, the old woman said, "I am grinding this iron rod into an embroidery needle."

Li Bai froze.

"An iron rod... into a needle? How long will that take?"

The old woman still did not lift her head.

"Grind one day, and it becomes a little shorter. Grind one year, and it becomes shorter still. Grind ten years, and it will become a needle. As long as I do not stop, one day it will happen."

Li Bai stood there for a long time.

Then he turned and went back to school.

Later, he became one of China's most famous poets. He wrote lines about waterfalls falling three thousand feet. If you remember that he once almost became a boy who ran away from study, you may hear something else inside that waterfall:

the patience of an iron rod slowly grinding against stone.

## Rising to Dance When the Rooster Crows

Zu Ti lived in the Eastern Jin dynasty. When he was young, he stayed at the home of a friend named Liu Kun.

The two young men both wanted to do something important with their lives.

Every night, Zu Ti slept with his ear close to the pillow.

He was waiting for one sound.

In the middle of the night, wild roosters outside the village began to crow.

Before the first call was even gone, Zu Ti kicked off his blanket and sat up. He shouted to Liu Kun, who was still snoring:

"The rooster crowed! Get up and practice sword!"

The two young men went into the cold courtyard before dawn and practiced sword fighting: ding, ding, dang, dang.

Villagers poked their heads out from warm blankets and muttered:

"Crazy... Why practice before the sky is even bright?"

Later, Zu Ti became a famous general of the Eastern Jin. He led troops north and recovered large areas of land.

## Cutting a Hole in the Wall to Borrow Light

In the Han dynasty, there was a child named Kuang Heng.

His family was so poor that at night they could not even afford an oil lamp.

But he wanted to read very badly.

The neighbor's house had light. But a thick earth wall stood between the two homes, and the light could not come through.

So Kuang Heng took a chisel and gently cut a small hole in the wall.

A thin thread of light from the neighbor's lamp slipped through.

Only a little light.

He held the bamboo slips close to his eyes and read character by character until the neighbor's lamp went out.

Later, Kuang Heng really became a government leader.

Different versions of the story add different details. But the scene people remember is the same:

a poor child borrowing light so he could keep reading.

## Water Drops Wear Through Stone

In the Song dynasty, a county leader named Zhang Yong was working on a court case.

He found that a worker in charge of money had hidden one copper coin inside his headscarf.

Zhang Yong ordered him to be punished in court.

The worker was angry.

"It is only one coin. What is the big deal? Can you kill me for one copper coin?"

Zhang Yong said:

"One coin a day becomes a thousand coins in a thousand days. A rope can saw through wood. Water drops can wear through stone."

If you steal one coin today, then another and another, it becomes a lot.

If a rope keeps rubbing wood, the wood can break.

If water drips in the same place again and again, stone can be worn into a hole.

Zhang Yong did not kill the worker. He put him in jail to think about what he had done.

The story spread.

"Water drops wear through stone" means that even something small and slow can change something hard, as long as it does not stop.

## Adding the Eyes to the Dragon

During the Northern and Southern Dynasties, there was a painter named Zhang Sengyou.

He was so famous for painting Buddhist pictures that even the emperor's family waited to hire him.

One day, on the white wall of Anle Temple in Jinling, he painted four great dragons.

People crowded around to watch. The dragons had shining scales and floating whiskers.

But none of them had eyes.

People asked why.

He said, "If I add the eyes, they will fly away."

Everyone laughed.

"Boasting again!"

In front of the crowd, Zhang Sengyou lifted his brush and dotted two pupils into the eyes of one white dragon.

The legend says thunder and lightning suddenly came. The wall cracked open, and the white dragon flew into the clouds.

The dragons without eyes stayed quietly on the wall.

Dotting the eyes is the final key step.

Everything before it matters. Everything after it depends on it.

"Adding eyes to the dragon" means the last important touch that makes the whole thing come alive.

Prepare for that final stroke.

Do not fear it.

## Five Idioms, One Idea

Grinding an iron rod into a needle.

Rising to dance when the rooster crows.

Cutting a wall to borrow light.

Water drops wearing through stone.

Adding eyes to the dragon.

Five stories. Five ordinary people.

An iron rod, a neighbor's lamp, a rooster before dawn, drops of water under the roof, and a dragon without eyes.

They are not trying to give a big speech.

They are simply saying:

do it.

Keep doing it.

No matter how slowly you move, do not stop.
