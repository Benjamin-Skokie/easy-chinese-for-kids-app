# Editorial References: Polo: A Favorite Horseback Game in the Tang Dynasty

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. The Metropolitan Museum of Art — “Internationalism in the Tang Dynasty (618–907)”

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/internationalism-in-the-tang-dynasty-618-906
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- Tang China exchanged goods, ideas, fashions, music, and other cultural practices with Central Asia and regions farther west.
- Polo was among the activities imported from Central Asian kingdoms and enthusiastically adopted in Tang elite circles.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says that polo traveled into China from lands farther west in Asia; it does not assign the game's invention to one precisely documented person, date, or route.
- The list of traveling clothes, music, food, and games is a child-facing summary of Tang international exchange.

## 2. Cleveland Museum of Art — “Polo Player”

URL: https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1964.181
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The painted earthenware figure dates to the late seventh or early eighth century in the Tang dynasty.
- The museum identifies the polo player as a woman in riding clothes and notes that polo was fashionable among both men and women in Tang China.

Editorial boundary:

- The illustration and prose use the museum object as evidence that women played polo; they do not reconstruct one specific historical match.
- “Riding at full speed” describes the figure's dynamic pose rather than a measured speed.

## 3. The Metropolitan Museum of Art — *Spirit and Ritual: The Morse Collection of Ancient Chinese Art*

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/spirit-and-ritual-the-morse-collection-of-ancient-chinese-art
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The publication discusses Tang tomb ceramics as representations of activities and fashions associated with life at the time.
- Its treatment of Tang equestrian figures provides context for horseback riding and polo among women.

Editorial boundary:

- Funerary figures provide material evidence for Tang imagery and elite culture; they do not show how common polo was among all women or social groups.
- The article therefore says “some Tang women, especially in wealthy families,” rather than suggesting universal participation.

## 5. Shaanxi History Museum — “Mural: Polo Game”

URL: https://en.sxhm.com/en/new/treasure/detail/18044.html
Accessed: 2026-09-05

Supports:

- The mural comes from the tomb of Prince Zhanghuai and depicts galloping riders swinging mallets in a polo game.
- The museum describes polo as widely popular during the Tang period and highly favored by the royal family.
- The museum places the game's movement into China within Silk Road exchange from Persia.

Editorial boundary:

- The mural supports the story's statements about court popularity and recognizable play; it is not treated as a record of one ordinary public match.
- The article does not claim that every Tang version was identical to modern polo.

URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1938-0524-113

Supports:
