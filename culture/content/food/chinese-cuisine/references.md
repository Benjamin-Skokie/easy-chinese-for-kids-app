# References for “What Does Chinese Food Taste Like?”

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Current child-facing version

Rewritten: 2026-09-02

- The article now follows five pictured meals rather than trying to survey every province or list every major cuisine.
- The “Eight Great Cuisines” are introduced as a useful starting guide, not a complete or ancient official map.
- Place names are kept only when a pictured food needs them. Detailed classification debates and Chinese American restaurant history were removed from the child-facing text.

## 1. *Global Food History* — “Special Issue on Chinese Culinary Regionalism: Introduction”

URL: https://doi.org/10.1080/20549547.2020.1770490

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The familiar standardized definition of Chinese regional cuisines grew from an earlier twentieth-century effort to describe a unified national cuisine.
- Common frameworks include both “Four Great Culinary Traditions” and “Eight Great Culinary Traditions.”
- A provincial label can be shorthand for multiple cooking traditions inside the same province; Cantonese cooking, for example, includes distinctions such as Guangzhou and Chaozhou traditions.

Editorial boundary:

- “Eight Great Cuisines” is presented as one useful modern framework, not an ancient official list or a complete map of food in China.
- The story does not assign each province one fixed cuisine or treat administrative borders as flavor borders.

## 2. Stanford University Press — scholarly introduction on Chinese food history and regional classification

URL: https://supress.sites-pro.stanford.edu/sites/supress/files/media/file/11964_Excerpt_from_the_Introduction.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Writers have used different systems—four, five, eight, and others—to classify Chinese regional cuisines.
- Republican-era restaurant writing used labels such as “Cantonese cuisine” as shorthand even when writers knew that Guangdong contained more specific local traditions.
- The status and boundaries of named cuisines changed in the modern period rather than remaining fixed since antiquity.

Editorial boundary:

- The article distinguishes the age of local cooking practices from the much newer act of selecting and ranking them as a national set.
- Areas left outside the eight are not described as lacking culinary traditions.

## 3. Liu et al. — “The prehistoric roots of Chinese cuisines: Mapping staple food systems of China, 6000 BC–220 AD” (*PLOS ONE*)

URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0240930

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Archaeological and isotopic evidence shows an early broad contrast between millet-heavy northern food systems and a more varied southern spectrum that included rice.
- Wheat and barley arrived from Southwest Asia after about 2000 BCE and created another changing east-west pattern of adoption.
- Communities combined crops and subsistence choices in varied ways over long periods.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing story uses “north” and “south” only as broad historical patterns, never rules about what every person ate.
- It does not project today's “north wheat, south rice” slogan unchanged into deep history or claim that climate alone determined diets.

## 4. Brian R. Dott — *The Chile Pepper in China: A Cultural Biography* (Columbia University Press)

URL: https://cup.columbia.edu/book/the-chile-pepper-in-china/9780231551304/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chilies originated in the Americas and were absent from China before the 1570s.
- The plant moved from newcomer to food, medicine, symbol, and marker of regional identity over centuries.
- The history of chilies in China is a history of cultural selection and adaptation, not instant adoption everywhere.

Editorial boundary:

- Ancient Sichuan food is not illustrated as chile-red, and chilies are not confused with the older, botanically different Sichuan pepper.
- The article does not state one proven entry ship, one exact route, or one person who introduced chilies to China.

## 5. Yu Weijie — “无心插柳：论辣椒在中国的选择性传播” (*美食研究*, summary hosted by South China Agricultural University)

URL: https://yjs.scau.edu.cn/2019/0512/c3176a185425/page.htm

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Some of the earliest surviving Chinese records of chilies come from Zhejiang and nearby southeastern coastal areas.
- Regions where chilies first appeared did not necessarily become the regions most identified with chile heat.
- Climate, crops, price, migration, older foodways, and cultural choices all contributed to the plant's selective spread.

Editorial boundary:

- Early documentation is not treated as proof of one exact first landing.
- Zhejiang's later flavor profile is used to challenge environmental determinism, not to deny that geography affects available ingredients.

## 6. Hongjie Wang — “Hot Peppers, Sichuan Cuisine and the Revolutions in Modern China” (*World History Connected*)

URL: https://journals.gmu.edu/whc/article/download/4424/2402?inline=1

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Modern chile-heavy Sichuan cooking could not predate the post-1492 global spread of Capsicum from the Americas.
- Sichuan cooks used older pungent ingredients such as ginger and Sichuan pepper before chilies arrived.
- Large Qing-period migration into Sichuan, especially from the Huguang region, formed part of the social setting in which later Sichuan food developed.

Editorial boundary:

- Migration is one important force among many, not a claim that newcomers erased all earlier Sichuan food.
- The article avoids the unsupported shortcut that damp weather automatically made Sichuan people eat chilies.

## 7. Ministry of Commerce “Time-Honored Brands” museum and China Daily — Chen Mapo tofu

URLs:

- https://lzhbwg.mofcom.gov.cn/edi_ecms_web_front/thb/detail/f7ab3a6e7ea0426d8fd104fc7c93df79
- https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/kindle/2015-09/06/content_21798522.htm

Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- The Chen Mapo restaurant brand dates itself to 1862 and connects the dish with the wife of a restaurant owner near Wanfu Bridge in Chengdu.
- The familiar name story explains *ma* through marks on Mrs. Chen's face and *po* as an older woman.
- A Chengdu guide published in 1909 listed Chen Mapo tofu among the city's well-known foods.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing story calls this the best-known account rather than an eyewitness record.
- The 1862 date, the customers, and the exact sequence of invention are not presented as independently proven in every detail.
- The English story does not translate *po* as “old woman,” which sounds unnecessarily blunt in modern American English. It keeps *Mapo* as a nickname and explains only the part needed to understand the name story.

## 8. Academy of Chinese Studies — “中国有多少个菜系？” and “Chinese Culinary Culture and Eight Regional Cuisines”

URLs:

- https://chiculture.org.hk/en/node/2698
- https://chiculture.org.hk/en/china-five-thousand-years/2699

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- There is no single agreed number of Chinese cuisines; some schemes count provinces while others divide provinces into several traditions.
- Cooking traditions depend on ingredients, seasoning, techniques, local custom, and historical exchange.
- Hong Kong tea and dim sum culture developed from older Guangzhou business customs while continuing to change and travel with migration.

Editorial boundary:

- Broad flavor rhymes are mentioned only as slogans that hide variation, not as factual laws.
- Regional summaries supply examples, not personality traits or fixed rules for every cook and household.

## 9. Hangzhou CPPCC — overview of classic Hangzhou dishes

URL: https://www.hzzx.gov.cn/cshz/content/2022-09/28/content_8363764.htm

Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Longjing shrimp, made with shrimp and Dragon Well tea, is a recognized Hangzhou dish.

Editorial boundary:

- The dish is one example from Hangzhou, not a summary of everything eaten in Zhejiang.
