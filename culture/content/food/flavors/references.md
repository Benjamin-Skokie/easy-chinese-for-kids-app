## Modern taste, smell, and oral sensation

Source: National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders, “Taste Disorders”
URL: https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/taste-disorders
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Modern sensory science recognizes at least five taste qualities: sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and umami.
- Aroma, temperature, texture, and the common chemical sense combine with taste to create the perception of flavor.
- Chili produces burning or irritation through sensory nerve endings rather than functioning like an ordinary basic taste.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not call the traditional Chinese five-flavor framework incorrect; it explains that it serves a different historical and cultural purpose.
- “At least five” avoids claiming that research on all possible taste qualities is permanently closed.

Source: InformedHealth.org / NCBI Bookshelf, “How does our sense of taste work?”
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK279408/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Flavor depends on taste together with smell, texture, and temperature.
- Umami is associated with glutamic and aspartic acids and occurs in foods such as tomatoes, meat, and cheese.
- Capsaicin activates heat-and-pain signals, so spicy heat is not classified as a basic taste.

Editorial boundary:

- The child activity uses ordinary familiar foods only; it does not ask children to ingest purified compounds or excessive seasoning.

Source: NCBI Bookshelf, “Trigeminal Chemoreception”
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11036/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Capsaicin is an irritant that produces burning through the trigeminal chemosensory system.

Editorial boundary:

- The article simplifies the neural pathway to age-appropriate language and does not claim that the trigeminal nerve is the only neural structure involved.

## Traditional five flavors as a historical framework

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Huangdi Neijing*, “Five Flavors”
URL: https://ctext.org/huangdi-neijing/wu-wei/ens
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A received classical Chinese medical text organizes foods and bodily correspondences through five flavors: sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, and salty.

Editorial boundary:

- This source documents a historical intellectual framework. The story does not present its organ correspondences as modern biomedical findings or use them to give medical advice.

Source: Lili Lai and Judith Farquhar, “Food and Medicine: Qualities and Efficacies in Chinese Ethnic Medicines,” University of Chicago Knowledge
URL: https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/y7n7t-vh546
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- *Wu wei* has operated in Chinese food and medicine as more than a list of sensations; it is also a framework for qualities, effects, and harmonizing practices.
- The relationship between food and medicine is culturally and historically complex.

Editorial boundary:

- Cultural uses of bitterness and medicinal-food ideas are described without promising that bitter foods prevent or cure disease.

## Chili pepper chronology

Source: Brian R. Dott, *The Chile Pepper in China: A Cultural Biography* (Columbia University Press, 2020), JSTOR record
URL: https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7312/dott19532
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chili peppers were introduced plants whose meanings and culinary uses developed over time in China.
- Surviving late-imperial evidence for chilies is uneven, especially before the nineteenth century.

Editorial boundary:

- The story does not claim one certain route into China or immediate nationwide adoption.

Source: Renmin University of China Institute of Qing History, research overview on the chili pepper’s introduction and spread
URL: https://iqh.ruc.edu.cn/old/qdhjylyjzx/xszl/hwyj/edf0ccf894ff45d4a9e72a4ece82b41e.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chilies originated in the Americas and probably entered China through more than one route.
- The earliest currently known explicit Chinese record is Gao Lian’s 1591 *Zunsheng bajian*; broader culinary adoption was gradual.

Editorial boundary:

- The exact arrival year and route remain uncertain. The article says the plant is recorded in 1591, not that it first crossed the border in that exact year.

Source: Jean Andrews, “Chilli Peppers,” *The Cambridge World History of Food*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-food/chilli-peppers/F955AD34FB4A4E66722A15E5D7A7B4C3
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Capsicum peppers were native to the Western Hemisphere and unknown in the Old World before transatlantic contact at the end of the fifteenth century.

Editorial boundary:

- This global chronology does not identify a single proven route into China.

## Regional cuisine and the limits of flavor maps

Source: Yu-Xiao Zhu et al., “Geography and Similarity of Regional Cuisines in China,” *PLOS ONE* 8(11), 2013
URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0079161
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- In the recipe dataset studied, geographic proximity was more important than climate proximity in explaining similarity among regional cuisines.
- Regional cuisines develop through cultural evolution and exchange rather than a one-factor climate rule.

Editorial boundary:

- This was a computational study of an online recipe dataset, not a complete census of every household. The article uses it only to reject climate determinism, not to claim that geography explains everything.

Source: Thomas O. Höllmann, *The Land of the Five Flavors: A Cultural History of Chinese Cuisine* (Columbia University Press, 2013)
URL: https://academic.oup.com/columbia-scholarship-online/book/23371
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese food history reflects regional customs, crops, foreign influences, trade routes, ethnic associations, economics, philosophy, and changing dietary concepts.

Editorial boundary:

- The story names several interacting forces and does not reduce regional preference to “south,” “north,” climate, or one supposedly fixed personality.
