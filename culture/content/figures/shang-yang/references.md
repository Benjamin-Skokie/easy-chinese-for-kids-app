# 商鞅：国家变强时，谁付出了代价？— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Biography, log story, reforms, and death narrative

Source: Chinese Text Project, Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian*, “Biography of Lord Shang”
URL: https://ctext.org/shiji/shang-jun-lie-zhuan/zhs
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Gongsun Yang came from Wey, served Duke Xiao of Qin, later received the Shang territory, and became known as Lord Shang or Shang Yang.
- The chapter narrates moving a log to establish credibility, household groups with mutual responsibility, military rewards, encouragement of farming, punishment around the crown prince, and the later inn refusal.
- After Duke Xiao's death in 338 BCE, Shang Yang was accused of rebellion, failed to escape to Wei, resisted from his fief, was killed, and then had his corpse torn apart and displayed; his family was destroyed.

Editorial boundary:

- Sima Qian wrote more than two centuries after Shang Yang. Famous dialogue, exact reward offers, the innkeeper's speech, and Shang Yang's sigh are treated as shaped historical narrative, not recordings.
- The article does not repeat the simplified claim that a living Shang Yang was necessarily torn apart by five horses or that an impartial court simply applied “his own law.”

## Institutional changes and their tradeoffs

Source: Yuri Pines, “Institutional Reforms and Reformers during the Warring States Period,” *The Oxford Handbook of Early China*
URL: https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34296/chapter-abstract/290749611
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shang Yang's reforms replaced much pedigree-based status with ranks of merit and linked advancement to military performance or agricultural production.
- Reforms strengthened bureaucracy, centralization, conscription, household registration, and an agriculture-centered war economy.

Editorial boundary:

- “Merit” is not treated as modern open opportunity: it often depended on warfare and operated inside a coercive, unequal monarchy.
- Qin's later strength and unification are not credited to Shang Yang alone.

Source: Andrew Chittick et al., “Imperial expansion, public investment, and the long path of history: China's initial political unification and its aftermath,” *Social Science History*
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522747/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qin reforms expanded the tax base and army, tied households into groups of five with shared responsibility, and redirected family and local obligations toward the state.
- Stronger state capacity helped mobilize resources for expansion.

Editorial boundary:

- “State capacity” is explained in concrete terms—registration, taxation, recruiting, rewarding, monitoring, and punishing—rather than celebrated as an abstract good.

## The composite *Book of Lord Shang*

Source: Yuri Pines, “Dating a Pre-Imperial Text: The Case Study of the Book of Lord Shang,” *Early China* 39 (2016)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/dating-a-preimperial-text-the-case-study-of-the-book-of-lord-shang/199B57467F51A62EBD492DE47DA3360A
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Modern scholarship generally treats the *Book of Lord Shang* as an accumulated work with chapters and passages produced at different times.
- Some material may relate closely to Shang Yang or his followers, while other chapters date after his death and even near Qin's unification.

Editorial boundary:

- The book is not called Shang Yang's single-authored diary, transcript, or finished reform manual.
- Ideas in later chapters are not automatically projected onto his private thoughts.

## Excavated Qin law and administration

Source: Robin D. S. Yates, “The State of Research in Early Chinese Legal History,” *Bamboo and Silk* 6 (2023)
URL: https://doi.org/10.1163/24689246-20230031
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shuihudi and Liye discoveries transformed study of Qin law by supplying legal and administrative documents beyond transmitted literature.
- Shuihudi Tomb 11 materials date from shortly before imperial unification, later than Shang Yang but within the developed Qin system.

Editorial boundary:

- No excavated slip is labeled as Shang Yang's autograph or treated as proof that he personally designed every later Qin procedure.

Source: Charles Sanft, “The Promulgation of Law in Qin and Western Han China,” *Early China* 44 (2021)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/abs/promulgation-of-law-in-qin-and-western-han-china/D5EE31803D74CDE3E0B8746E714155EC
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Excavated records show deliberate efforts to circulate legal orders among officials and the population.
- Couriers carried official decisions, but transmission to distant offices could be delayed.

Editorial boundary:

- Written law is not presented as frictionless, instantly known everywhere, or identical to modern rule of law.

Source: Maxim Korolkov, “State Control of Bureaucrats under the Qin: Techniques and Procedures,” *Early China* 39 (2016)
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-china/article/abs/state-control-of-bureaucrats-under-the-qin-techniques-and-procedures/26F23940F6B99404572C42C18A98A46B
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shuihudi texts document appointment, reporting, performance checks, salaries, and other techniques for controlling Qin officials.
- Qin state capacity depended on procedures and many working officials rather than one ruler or thinker.

Editorial boundary:

- The article distinguishes predictable written standards from a modern rights-protecting legal order that also binds the sovereign.
