# Mulan Shared Story and Facts Brief

This file is an editorial bridge between research and independently written Chinese and English stories. It does not render on the website and must not be used as line-by-line translation copy.

## Core story spine

1. Mulan lives with her family and weaves cloth.
2. An army notice calls her father to serve. The household has no adult son who can take his place: Mulan has no older brother, and the younger brother seen later in the poem is still a child.
3. Mulan chooses to go in her father's place. She buys a horse and riding gear, says goodbye, and rides away.
4. She travels past the Yellow River and farther north. The poem uses the changing sounds of home, water, and distant horses to show how far she has gone.
5. Mulan serves for many years. The poem gives cold nights, armor, marching, danger, and death, but no named battle, training scene, rank, special tactic, or exact deed that earns her reward.
6. The war ends. The khan offers Mulan an official position. She asks instead for a fast horse or camel to carry her home.
7. Her family welcomes her. Mulan changes into her old clothes and fixes her hair. Her fellow soldiers are astonished to learn that she is a woman.
8. The poem ends with a comparison to two hares running side by side.

## Factual boundaries

- The early ballad calls her Mulan. It does not give her the surname Hua, an exact hometown, or a confirmed date.
- The poem does not say that her father is old or sick.
- Do not invent private conversations, a daily disguise routine, punishment for discovery, named friends, a romance, a talking dragon, training scenes, individual battles, promotion to general, or a single act that saves the country.
- Early Reader prose may tell the familiar core story directly. It does not need to open with textual history or interrupt every scene with a warning.
- Images are modern story illustrations, not historical records.

## Early Reader English assignment

- Audience: U.S. kindergarten and first-grade listeners; supported readers around first grade and above.
- Write an original English read-aloud from this brief, not a translation of the Chinese paragraphs.
- Use warm, familiar American English, concrete actions, useful repetition, and sentences that are comfortable to say aloud.
- Keep unavoidable story words such as `army` and `soldier`. In the Early Reader version, use the familiar title `emperor`; in the Story version, use the poem's title `khan` and explain it with a short tooltip.
- Replace nonessential difficult words with familiar language instead of adding tooltips everywhere.
- Prefer natural phrases such as `could count on her`, `rode for miles and miles`, and `a fast horse to carry her home` over formal phrases such as `served well`, `received high honors`, or `requested transportation`.
- Preserve six story sections and an ending, but let English paragraph rhythm differ from Chinese.
- The story should feel substantial, not like a plot summary or a phonics worksheet.

## Story English assignment

- Audience: independent readers in roughly grades 2–4, plus younger children listening with an adult.
- Retell the complete story with clear scenes, emotional movement, and natural read-aloud rhythm.
- Use the poem's sounds—the loom, river, distant horses, night watch, and homecoming—to carry the reader through the journey.
- Keep the poem's meaningful gaps visible, but do not let historical cautions interrupt every scene.
- Do not add training, named battles, private conversations, a punishment for discovery, or a specific military achievement.

## Advanced English assignment

- Audience: older elementary readers, families, and teachers.
- Write engaging children's nonfiction, not a research paper and not a sentence-by-sentence translation.
- Distinguish the surviving ballad, scholarly interpretations, later traditions, and modern adaptations.
- Preserve uncertainty around the poem's date, Mulan's identity, Northern Wei connections, geography, length of service, and military deeds.
- Use concrete evidence before interpretation. Mark possibilities with language such as `may`, `perhaps`, `suggests`, and `we do not know`.
- Explain or tooltip unfamiliar historical terms without turning the main text into a glossary.
