# 鲁班：他真的发明了所有木工工具吗？— References and Editorial Notes

## Early textual figure and the Chu–Song story

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Mozi*, “Gongshu”
URL: https://ctext.org/mozi/gong-shu/zhs
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The text names Gongshu Pan as the maker of cloud ladders for Chu's proposed attack on Song.
- It narrates Mozi using his belt as a model city and wooden pieces as defensive equipment while Gongshu Pan attempts nine forms of attack.
- The king of Chu agrees in the narrative not to attack Song after Mozi explains that defenders are already prepared.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a Warring States narrative shaped to teach about war, ethics, and preparedness, not a word-for-word transcript or modern military report.
- The text links Gongshu Pan with cloud ladders; it does not prove that he invented every form of siege ladder or supply a complete engineering design.

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Mozi*, book 13, “Questions of Lu”
URL: https://ctext.org/mozi/book-13/zhs
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The text says Gongshu carved a bird from bamboo and wood that remained aloft for three days.
- Mozi responds that a small, useful cart part deserves more praise, turning the episode into a discussion of practical benefit and skill.

Editorial boundary:

- The wooden-bird passage is evidence that the story existed in an early text, not material proof of an airplane, glider, kite, or known mechanism.
- No surviving object, plan, or construction directions justify reconstructing the bird's flight.

## Names, legend growth, and the craft-hero tradition

Source: Chinese Social Sciences Net, “鲁班传说与中华民族共同体形成的三重逻辑”
URL: https://www.cssn.cn/wx/wx_zggdwx/202503/t20250304_5855623.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Early sources use forms including Gongshu Ban, Gongshu Pan, and Master Gongshu; later sources increasingly use Lu Ban.
- Stories about the wooden bird, cloud ladder, buildings, bridges, and magical acts accumulated over many centuries.
- Lu Ban became a widely shared cultural and occupational hero, with traditions spreading across regions and trades.

Editorial boundary:

- Later legend growth is not projected backward as a complete biography of one Warring States maker.
- The article treats Lu Ban as both an early textual figure and a cultural hero built by later craft communities.

## Saws, planes, ink lines, and collective tool development

Source: Richard Fullagar et al., *Archaeological Research in Asia*, “A functional study of denticulate sickles and knives, ground stone tools from the early Neolithic Peiligang culture, China”
URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S235222672100012X
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese Neolithic sites dating roughly 9,000–7,000 years ago contain stone implements with toothed cutting edges.
- Toothed cutting technology existed thousands of years before the period associated with Gongshu Ban.

Editorial boundary:

- Neolithic denticulate sickles and knives are not called identical to later iron woodworking saws.
- The evidence challenges a sudden single-inventor origin story without claiming one simple evolutionary line from every stone tool to a carpenter's saw.

Source: Yukio Yamaguchi, *Journal of Architecture, Planning and Environmental Engineering*, “Chinese Carpenter's Planes: Carpenter's Tools in Jiangnan District, China, Part 2”
URL: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aija/61/486/61_KJ00004221720/_article/-char/en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The history of Chinese carpenter's planes involves changing forms and tool families documented especially in Ming and later sources.
- Adzes, chisels, drawing knives, and planes must be studied as historically developing tools rather than a timeless set created at once.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not attribute the developed bench plane in the retained workshop image to Lu Ban personally or to the Warring States period.

Source: Zhenjing Traditional Woodwork Museum, Google Arts & Culture, “Traditional Chinese Woodworking Tool: Ink Line”
URL: https://artsandculture.google.com/story/traditional-chinese-woodworking-tool-ink-line-zhenjing-woodworking-institute/mAUhO8AhKclOKQ?hl=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The ink line is a common traditional carpentry tool for marking straight lines and can also serve as a plumb line.
- Individual woodworkers made and varied tools, leaving personal judgment and craft practice in their forms.

Editorial boundary:

- Later use and veneration do not establish Lu Ban as the documented first inventor of the ink line.

## The *Lu Ban Jing* and later workshop knowledge

Source: Palace Museum, “《鲁班经匠家镜》”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/ancient/yuanmingqing/148044.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Palace Museum copy is a Ming Wanli-period edition compiled by Wu Rong, collected by Zhang Yan, and checked by Zhou Yan.
- The text covers building methods, house and furniture dimensions, diagrams, materials, and period ideas about auspicious and inauspicious construction.
- The book is important evidence for Ming vernacular carpentry and furniture, with later expanded and reprinted editions.

Editorial boundary:

- The *Lu Ban Jing* is about two thousand years later than the period associated with Gongshu Ban and is not his personal writing.
- Its title shows Lu Ban's authority as a craft ancestor; it does not prove his authorship of the manual or every technique inside it.

Source: Wang Ermin, Academia Sinica, “中國民間市井營造工匠日用之《魯班經》”
URL: https://www.mh.sinica.edu.tw/MHDocument/Publication/Publication_622.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Textual study identifies the *Lu Ban Jing* as a Ming work rather than a Warring States book.
- Its contents reflect the work and contributions of multiple later craftspeople.

Editorial boundary:

- The manual can preserve valuable craft knowledge even though the famous ancestor named in its title did not author it.
