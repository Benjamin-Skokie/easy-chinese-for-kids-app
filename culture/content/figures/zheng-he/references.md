# 郑和与七次远航 — References

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Seven voyages and an ocean already busy with ships

Source: Columbia University, Asia for Educators, “The Ming Voyages”
URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/special/china_1000ce_mingvoyages.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The seven Ming expeditions took place from 1405 to 1433, more than six hundred years ago.
- The fleets traveled through waters that already had active ports, sailors, merchants, and established routes.
- The fleets were large, carried many kinds of people and supplies, and sometimes took part in armed conflict.

Editorial boundary:

- Zheng He is not described as the first person to sail or “discover” the Indian Ocean.

## Why Zheng He was chosen

Source: Lee Cheuk Yin, University of Brunei Darussalam, Institute of Asian Studies, “Zheng He and his Maritime Expeditions”
URL: https://ias.ubd.edu.bn/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/WORKING_PAPER_SERIES_81.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zheng He gained the emperor's trust through years of court and military service before he was chosen to command the expeditions.

Editorial boundary:

- His childhood name and the complicated circumstances of his capture are left out because they are not needed for this story.

## Navigation and teamwork at sea

Source: “An Open Knowledge System for Navigational Science: Zheng He's Maritime Expeditions and Sino-Foreign Overseas Exchange,” *Chinese Annals of History of Science and Technology*
URL: https://brill.com/view/journals/cahs/1/1/article-p50_50.xml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Crews used compasses, coastal observations, water-depth measurements, route knowledge, and experienced pilots.
- Successful voyages depended on knowledge shared by Chinese and foreign sailors.

Editorial boundary:

- Zheng He is not shown finding the way or completing the voyages by himself.

## The giraffe and the qilin

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Giraffe with Two Keepers”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/51649
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A giraffe reached the Yongle emperor's court in 1414 through the maritime diplomatic network.
- Court writers connected it with the *qilin* from Chinese stories.

Editorial boundary:

- Zheng He is not said to have personally found the giraffe in Africa, and the animal does not literally become a *qilin*.

## Armed conflict and prisoners

Source: Cambridge University Press, “Ming–Kotte War in Southeast Asia (1410–11),” in *The Making of the Modern Chinese Navy*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/making-of-the-modern-chinese-navy/mingkotte-war-in-southeast-asia-141011/22CC2980B942006F422B101BE962E6A6
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zheng He's forces fought in Sri Lanka and took the local ruler and members of his family to China.

Editorial boundary:

- The voyages are not presented as entirely peaceful or as continuous conquest.

## Why the official fleets stopped

Source: Xin Wen, “The Cessation of Zheng He's Voyages and the Beginning of Private Sailings,” *Journal of Chinese History*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/cessation-of-zheng-hes-voyages-and-the-beginning-of-private-sailings-fiscal-competition-between-emperors-and-bureaucrats/8FE45F3210A4DF174A4EC2B30868D433
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The expeditions ended for several financial and political reasons, including competing priorities and northern defense.
- The end of the emperor's giant fleets did not end private Chinese sailing.

Editorial boundary:

- The story does not give one simple cause or claim that China stopped going to sea.
