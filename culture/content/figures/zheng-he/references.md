# 郑和：船队为什么出发，又为什么停下？— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Voyage chronology, changing routes, and expedition scale

Source: Columbia University, Asia for Educators, “The Ming Voyages”
URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/special/china_1000ce_mingvoyages.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Seven state expeditions sailed between 1405 and 1433, with routes expanding from Southeast Asia and India to Hormuz, the Arabian Peninsula, and the East African coast.
- Fleets varied between voyages; the third voyage, for example, is described separately from the first in ships, troops, and destinations.
- The first voyage's return included armed action against Chen Zuyi at Palembang.

Editorial boundary:

- One voyage's ship and personnel totals are not applied to all seven voyages.
- Ports reached by branch squadrons are not automatically presented as places personally visited by Zheng He or every ship.
- The ports were part of established Indian Ocean networks and are not described as “discovered” by the Ming fleet.

Source: Cambridge University Press, *International Organization*, “Defending Hierarchy from the Moon to the Indian Ocean”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/defending-hierarchy-from-the-moon-to-the-indian-ocean-symbolic-capital-and-political-dominance-in-early-modern-china-and-the-cold-war/1A48863DA2EE573CA0899DF939CCEE1D
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The voyages occurred in 1405–07, 1407–09, 1409–11, 1413–15, 1417–19, 1421–22, and 1431–33.
- Their political purposes involved hierarchy, tribute relations, and the Yongle emperor's need to demonstrate and legitimize authority.
- Chinese and other merchants knew and used Indian Ocean routes long before the expeditions.

Editorial boundary:

- The voyages are not reduced to trade profit, peaceful friendship, exploration of an unknown ocean, or a secret search for the Jianwen emperor.

## Treasure-ship size controversy

Source: Sally K. Church, *Monumenta Serica*, “Zheng He: An Investigation into the Plausibility of 450-ft Treasure Ships”
URL: https://doi.org/10.2307/40727457
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The later *Ming shi* gives 44 *zhang* by 18 *zhang* for the largest treasure ships, often converted to roughly 447 by 183 feet.
- Scholars debate the reliability of the recorded dimensions, the units, wooden-ship feasibility, and how early written and archaeological evidence should be combined.

Editorial boundary:

- No single proposed smaller size is presented as settled fact either.
- The retained fleet image is modern imagination, not a reliable scale reconstruction.

Source: University of Washington Press, *Fir and Empire*, “Wood and Water, Part II: Naval Timber”
URL: https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/fir-and-empire/section/d1fdd798-02e0-439b-9282-73f1bfd0d269
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shipbuilding for the Zheng He fleets required enormous timber and state organization.
- Naval architecture and treasure-ship-yard archaeology have produced serious doubts about ships over 400 feet long.

Editorial boundary:

- “The fleet was enormous” is retained; “the largest ships were certainly longer than a modern football field” is not.

## Ma He, capture, eunuch service, and command

Source: University of Brunei Darussalam, Institute of Asian Studies, Lee Cheuk Yin, “Zheng He and his Maritime Expeditions”
URL: https://ias.ubd.edu.bn/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/WORKING_PAPER_SERIES_81.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zheng He was born Ma He in Yunnan around 1371, came from a Muslim family, and was captured after Ming forces conquered Yunnan.
- He became a eunuch in Zhu Di's service, gained trust through court and military service, and later commanded imperial expeditions.

Editorial boundary:

- No private dialogue, exact emotions, or cinematic capture scene is invented.
- Muslim background is not treated as proof that Zheng He personally spoke every required language or directly negotiated every encounter.

## Navigation: monsoons, compass routes, and shared knowledge

Source: Shih-Shan Henry Tsai et al., *The Journal of Navigation*, “The Asian Monsoons and Zheng He's Voyages to the Western Ocean”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-navigation/article/abs/asian-monsoons-and-zheng-hes-voyages-to-the-western-ocean/48751F740571B1F57772AB38BD9E2AA1
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Voyage timing depended on the repeating seasonal patterns of Asian monsoons.
- Successful round trips required careful planning around changing wind direction, not simply courage and large ships.

Editorial boundary:

- Individual dates and routes are not invented from a generic monsoon pattern.

Source: *Journal of Asian History*, “An Open Knowledge System for Navigational Science: Zheng He's Maritime Expeditions and Sino-Foreign Overseas Exchange”
URL: https://brill.com/view/journals/cahs/1/1/article-p50_50.xml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Fleets used compass navigation, soundings, route information, and both Chinese and foreign pilots.
- Navigation knowledge drew on earlier Chinese, Islamic, South Asian, and regional maritime experience.
- Ming records preserve foreign navigators' participation, including during violent encounters.

Editorial boundary:

- The “Zheng He Navigation Chart” is treated as transmitted route knowledge, not a modern-looking map personally drawn by Zheng He.
- The retained planning image contains a modern map style and is labeled imaginative.

Source: Biran et al., *eLife*, “Red Lines in the Ocean: Sea Routes on Early Modern East Asian Maps”
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132550/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Route maps associated with Zheng He's voyages show coasts, compass bearings, and estimated travel times using mariners' vocabulary.
- Such maps differ in purpose and appearance from modern geographic world maps.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim that one surviving chart is Zheng He's autograph or a complete record of every voyage.

## Tribute, giraffe–qilin translation, and imperial legitimacy

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Giraffe with Two Keepers”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/51649
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- In 1414 a giraffe was presented to the Yongle emperor by a Bengal embassy.
- Court writers associated the giraffe with the auspicious *qilin* and used its arrival to praise and legitimize Yongle's reign.
- The animal's arrival was connected to the maritime expedition and Indian Ocean political-trade network.

Editorial boundary:

- The giraffe was not simply “found in Africa by Zheng He and brought straight to Nanjing.”
- The retained exchange image combines multiple people, goods, and moments and is not a verified scene.

Source: Oxford Academic / University of Hawai‘i Press, “How the Giraffe Became a Qilin: Intercultural Signification in Ming Dynasty Arts”
URL: https://doi.org/10.21313/hawaii/9780824846763.003.0010
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The giraffe's meaning was translated through different cultural traditions and political agendas.
- Identifying it as a *qilin* served a courtly claim that a powerful but peaceful ruler had received an auspicious sign.

Editorial boundary:

- “Giraffe became *qilin*” is explained as cultural interpretation, not literal zoological transformation or only a foolish mistake.

## Exchange, inscriptions, and armed conflict

Source: Sri Lanka National Commission for UNESCO, “Trilingual Inscription”
URL: https://slncu.lk/partnerships-networks/mow/trilingual-inscription/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Galle stone carries Chinese, Tamil, and Persian inscriptions and records religious offerings across traditions.
- It dates to the third-voyage period and survives as material evidence of cross-cultural contact.

Editorial boundary:

- The inscription supports exchange and multi-religious diplomacy but does not erase the military conflict that followed in Sri Lanka.

Source: Cambridge University Press, *The Making of the Modern Chinese Navy*, “Ming–Kotte War in Southeast Asia (1410–11)”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/making-of-the-modern-chinese-navy/mingkotte-war-in-southeast-asia-141011/22CC2980B942006F422B101BE962E6A6
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Zheng He's forces fought Alakeshvara's forces in Sri Lanka, took the stronghold, and carried the ruler and family members to China.
- Ming intervention affected Sri Lankan political leadership after the captives were released.

Editorial boundary:

- The conflict is not minimized as a tiny misunderstanding or enlarged into continuous conquest across the whole Indian Ocean.

## Why the state expeditions stopped—and why seafaring did not

Source: Xin Wen, *Journal of Chinese History*, “The Cessation of Zheng He's Voyages and the Beginning of Private Sailings”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/cessation-of-zheng-hes-voyages-and-the-beginning-of-private-sailings-fiscal-competition-between-emperors-and-bureaucrats/8FE45F3210A4DF174A4EC2B30868D433
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Explanations for cessation include fiscal pressure, political and ideological priorities, northern-frontier strategy, and competition between emperors, eunuch institutions, and civil bureaucracy over revenue and authority.
- Ending the giant official expeditions did not mean the end of private Chinese maritime activity.

Editorial boundary:

- The article gives no single-cause answer such as “the voyages were simply too expensive” or “Confucian officials hated ships.”
- It rejects the claim that China completely stopped sailing after 1433.

Source: Xihua University / Sichuan University, “郑和下西洋档案并非刘大夏烧毁——明代刘大夏销毁郑和下西洋档案考辨”
URL: https://aj.xhu.edu.cn/xhdxxbzskb/article/id/c7f0d466-da90-4ec2-a1f7-e28ac8b5b729?viewType=HTML
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The popular story that Liu Daxia personally burned the Zheng He archives is not securely supported by the surviving record.

Editorial boundary:

- Loss and dispersal of voyage documents are not reduced to one dramatic bonfire.

## Death and burial uncertainty

Source: Chen Pingping, *Journal of Nanjing Xiaozhuang University*, “‘Zheng He Died at Calicut in 1433’: Correction of an Error”
URL: https://sfzk.cbpt.cnki.net/portal/journal/portal/client/paper/4040e61ad42358a3dc495a275f566291
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Official histories do not give a secure place of death.
- The popular Calicut claim depends on disputed late evidence, and material concerning the seventh voyage raises questions about whether Zheng He personally went to Calicut.

Editorial boundary:

- The article reports the strong 1433/final-voyage tradition but does not state Calicut death, burial at sea, or return of the body as certain.
- The Nanjing tomb or memorial tradition does not by itself establish where the body was buried.
