# Zheng He: Sailing Far with Treasure and Peace

## One of the Largest Ocean Fleets in the World

In 1405, during the Ming dynasty, more than 200 ships waited at Liujiagang in Taicang, Jiangsu.

The largest were called "treasure ships."

Old records say some were more than 120 meters long, longer than a modern soccer field. Scholars still debate that number, but everyone agrees the fleet was enormous.

More than 27,000 people gathered at the docks.

There were sailors, soldiers, translators, doctors, star-watchers, and many other workers.

The man leading this huge fleet was Zheng He.

Over the next 28 years, Zheng He sailed seven times.

He went to Southeast Asia, India, the Arabian Peninsula, and as far as the east coast of Africa, near today's Kenya and Somalia.

---

## Not to Fight, but to Give Gifts

Zheng He's fleet was heavily armed.

It had soldiers, cannons, and gunpowder weapons. If it had wanted to conquer port cities, few kingdoms around Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean could have stopped it.

But that was not its main purpose.

What came off Zheng He's ships was not mainly soldiers.

It was silk, porcelain, tea, copper coins, and books.

He gave these gifts to local kings.

The kings gave gifts back: spices, ivory, jewels, and giraffes.

Zheng He brought a giraffe back to Nanjing.

Many Ming people had never seen such an animal. Some thought it was the legendary *qilin*, a lucky creature from old stories.

Crowds in Nanjing rushed to see this "lucky beast."

Imagine the giraffe standing in front of the palace, staring and chewing leaves, while court officials kneel all around.

Zheng He's seven voyages were mainly about peaceful contact, official visits, and trade.

Sometimes his fleet did get involved in conflict, such as fighting pirates or helping a threatened king return to power.

But it did not start wars to take over lands overseas.

---

## A Muslim Eunuch Leads the Great Fleet

Zheng He's original name was Ma He.

He was a Hui Muslim from Yunnan.

When Ming soldiers took Yunnan, they captured him as a young boy, a little over ten years old. He was sent into palace service as a eunuch, a special kind of palace servant.

Later he served Zhu Di, the Prince of Yan.

He earned trust through military service. After Zhu Di took the throne as the Yongle Emperor, Zheng He was promoted.

Then he was chosen to lead the voyages to the Western Seas.

He had survived the harsh world inside the palace.

He had sharp political sense, strong loyalty, and the ability to deal with people from many lands.

Because he was Muslim, he could speak more naturally with Muslim rulers when the fleet reached the Arabian Peninsula and the Swahili Coast of East Africa.

His fleet also included Indian monks. In Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka, they helped the fleet meet local courts.

A Muslim boy from Yunnan became the top commander of one of the greatest fleets in the world.

That life already sounds like a legend.

---

## Why Did the Voyages Stop?

In 1433, Zheng He returned from his seventh voyage.

Soon after, he died on the way home, according to traditional stories, near Calicut in India. The fleet brought his remains back to Nanjing.

After the Yongle Emperor died, later rulers listened more to civil officials who thought the voyages cost too much.

Some sailing records were burned.

Ming rules against private sea travel also grew stricter.

The expensive ocean voyages finally stopped.

Today, you can still find traces of Zheng He in Southeast Asia.

Melaka has a temple tied to him. Semarang in Java remembers him too. Thailand and Indonesia have wells said to be tied to his landings.

Hundreds of years later, local Chinese communities still honored him as "Sanbao Gong."

A Chinese sailor slowly became almost like a god in the memories of people across the southern seas.

---

## Summary

Zheng He was a Muslim from Yunnan who was captured as a boy and sent into palace service.

Later, he became commander of a huge Ming fleet.

He sailed seven times to the Western Seas and reached as far as the east coast of Africa, earlier than Columbus crossed the Atlantic.

His fleet's main purpose was not taking over other lands. It was official contact, trade, and showing the power and wealth of Ming China.

Temples in Melaka, memories in Java, and stories across Southeast Asia show that people remembered him long after the voyages ended.

A child with a hard beginning traveled farther than almost anyone around him could have imagined.
