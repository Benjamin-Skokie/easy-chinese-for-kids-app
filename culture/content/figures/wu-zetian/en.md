# Wu Zetian: The Only Female Emperor in Chinese History

## A Fourteen-Year-Old Girl Walks Through the Palace Gate

In 637, a fourteen-year-old girl was sent into Emperor Taizong's palace.

Her name was Wu Zetian.

On the day she entered the palace, her mother cried hard. Once a girl entered the palace, she might never see her family again.

Wu Zetian looked at her mother and said something surprising:

"How do we know meeting the emperor will not be good luck?"

When a fourteen-year-old says that, you can imagine something about her.

She was not someone who only lowered her head in fear.

She may even have been curious.

What would happen inside the most serious and guarded place in the empire?

Emperor Taizong gave her the rank of *cairen*, one of the lower ranks for palace women.

She stayed at that rank for many years.

She may not have stood in the brightest place, but later people often noticed something important: from the edge of palace life, she watched and learned.

Which ministers handled which matters?

How did a report reach the emperor?

How did the palace and the court fit together?

These lessons later became her capital.

After Taizong died, palace women without children were usually sent to become Buddhist nuns.

Wu Zetian was sent to Ganye Temple. Her hair was shaved, and she put on a gray nun's robe.

Everyone thought her story had ended.

---

## Coming Back from the Nunnery

But her story had not ended.

Taizong's son Li Zhi, later Emperor Gaozong, had known Wu Zetian when she was still in the palace.

After he became emperor, she was brought back.

This time, she was no longer the low-ranking young woman standing near the edge.

Her position rose quickly, and before long she became one of the most watched women in the palace.

But second place was not enough.

At that time, the empress was Empress Wang. She came from a powerful family and had many supporters at court.

Then came one of the most famous and hardest-to-prove palace mysteries in Chinese history: the death of a baby princess.

Later people often told it as a key move in Wu Zetian's rise.

The exact truth is still difficult to know.

But the result was clear: Empress Wang was removed, and Wu Zetian became the new empress.

---

## Behind the Curtain

Emperor Gaozong's health grew worse, and Wu Zetian became more and more involved in government.

Later people used the phrase "listening to politics from behind a curtain" to describe the scene.

Ministers outside the curtain could not see her face.

But they could feel her judgment and will.

Who should be promoted? Who should be sent down? Where was grain needed? Where were soldiers needed?

She handled matters in an orderly way. You can see that even in the history books.

After Gaozong died, Wu Zetian put two of her sons on the throne one after another.

But it became clearer and clearer that the real person in power was Wu Zetian herself.

In 690, she officially became emperor and changed the dynasty name to Zhou.

In Chinese history, she is the only woman who officially sat on the emperor's throne.

That fact is solid. It does not need to be softened.

---

## How Did a Woman Rule an Empire?

The most important thing to study about Wu Zetian is more than the fact that she was a woman who became emperor.

It is that she really ruled the empire, and not badly.

She valued the civil service exams and was more willing to promote people from less powerful families.

This did not mean nobles suddenly disappeared.

But the path upward opened a little wider for people below them.

She also used special boxes where people could send reports, complaints, or secret messages directly to the ruler.

This could help expose corrupt officials.

It could also create a dark habit of spying and accusing others.

Both sides were real.

She cared about farming and local government too. She often judged officials by what they actually got done.

She promoted many capable people. Di Renjie was the most famous.

Later stories about what she said after Di Renjie died may sound very literary.

But they still show that she valued officials who could truly do the work.

---

## The Wordless Tablet: She Explains Nothing

Wu Zetian lived to be eighty-two.

She ruled as emperor for fifteen years.

Before she died, she returned the throne to the Li family of the Tang. The Tang dynasty she had replaced came back.

After her death, she was buried at Qianling with Emperor Gaozong.

In front of her tomb stands a huge stone tablet.

On it, there is not one word.

No one knows exactly why she left it blank.

Maybe she wanted later people to judge for themselves.

Maybe she knew her life, with all its achievements and faults, could not be neatly written in a few lines.

Wu Zetian did not leave a simple answer.

She left a question people keep turning back to:

If a woman could truly be emperor, and rule well enough to be remembered, then what were all those old rules really standing on?

---

## Summary

A fourteen-year-old girl walked through the palace gate and, step by step, reached the dragon throne.

She learned how the court worked from the edge of the palace.

Then she moved from behind the curtain to in front of it, and finally became emperor herself.

Her life had courage, ambition, political skill, blood, and arguments that have never fully gone away.

But one thing cannot be changed:

Wu Zetian really was emperor, and she really ruled the country.
