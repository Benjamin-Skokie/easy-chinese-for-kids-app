# Editorial References: Wu Zetian

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, Chinese history timeline

URL: https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/for-educators/teaching-china-with-the-smithsonian/interactives/timelines/timeline-of-chinese-history-art-and-culture/

Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- Wu Zetian's dates, her growing role while Emperor Gaozong was ill, her rule after his death, and the Zhou dynasty she established.
- The imperial examination system existed before Wu; she expanded its use rather than inventing it.

Editorial boundary:

- Wu is described as the only woman who formally ruled China with the title of emperor, not the only woman in Chinese history who ever held political power.

## 2. *Old Book of Tang*, “Annals of Empress Zetian”

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/%E8%88%8A%E5%94%90%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B76

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Wu entering the palace at fourteen, living at Ganye Temple after Taizong's death, returning during Gaozong's reign, and becoming empress in 655.
- Gaozong's illness, Wu's role in government, the removal of her sons, her use of the emperor title in 690, the 705 transfer of power, her death, and burial at Qianling.

Editorial boundary:

- This tenth-century history is important but not an eyewitness diary. The story keeps the main chronology and does not repeat its gendered insults or unverified palace scenes.

## 3. Victor Xiong, Ohio State University Origins, “Wu Zetian: The Only Woman Emperor in Chinese History”

URL: https://origins.osu.edu/index.php/read/wu-zetian-woman-emperor-china

Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- Empress Wang helping Wu return to court, Wu's rise to empress, the short reigns of her sons under her control, and her formal reign from 690 to 705.
- The combination of capable appointments, changes that widened some opportunities, and violent action against rivals.

Editorial boundary:

- Vivid claims about private conversations, motives, and violent palace scenes are not treated as certain merely because they appear in a modern retelling.
- Wu's achievement as a woman does not excuse the suffering caused by her use of power.

## 4. Research on appointments and the four petition boxes

URLs:

- https://www.xsyk021.com/article/id/890d4542-aed3-47ce-bd9c-f75b35f9cfa8?pageType=en
- https://www1.ihp.sinica.edu.tw/en/Publications/LegalHistoryStudy/1180/Article/507

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Wu's wider use of examinations and self-recommendation without claiming that family connections disappeared.
- The four petition boxes, their use for suggestions and complaints, and their connection to accusations, special investigators, torture, imprisonment, exile, and executions.

Editorial boundary:

- The boxes were not simply “spy boxes”: they could carry useful proposals as well as dangerous accusations.
- The story gives no invented victim total and does not describe methods of torture to children.

## 5. Qianling and the Stone with No Words

URL: https://www.chinaheritagequarterly.org/articles.php?issue=008&searchterm=008_qianling.inc

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The tall monument near Qianling commonly called Wu's “Stele without Characters,” and the later inscriptions cut into it.

Editorial boundary:

- No surviving evidence proves why the monument originally had no inscription. The story does not present “she wanted later generations to judge her” as fact.
