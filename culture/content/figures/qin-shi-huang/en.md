# Qin Shi Huang: The First Emperor to Unite China

## A Thirteen-Year-Old King

In 247 BCE, the state of Qin got a new king.

His name was Ying Zheng.

He was thirteen.

Think about that. At thirteen, many children are in middle school. Ying Zheng was already sitting on a throne, facing advisers and generals.

Of course, he did not truly control everything at first. The chief minister Lu Buwei and the queen mother held much of the power.

For the next nine years, Ying Zheng watched, learned, and waited.

At twenty-two, he took back full power.

Then he began a huge task: uniting the land.

From 230 BCE to 221 BCE, Qin destroyed six other states: Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan, and Qi.

It took less than ten years.

When the last state, Qi, surrendered, Chinese history saw a new kind of united empire.

Ying Zheng gave himself a new title: *Shi Huangdi*, the First Emperor.

He no longer wanted to be called "king." Kings belonged to separate states. He wanted a title above that.

From this moment, the word "emperor" entered more than 2,000 years of Chinese history.

---

## Same Wheel Tracks, Same Writing

After uniting the six states, Qin Shi Huang faced an awkward problem.

Every old state had its own writing style, its own ways to weigh and measure things, its own laws, and even different distances between carriage wheels.

Imagine riding a carriage from old Qin land into old Qi land. The road changes, and suddenly your wheels do not fit the tracks.

You might have to change carriages.

Qin Shi Huang ordered the country to make many things the same.

Writing was brought under one style, called small seal script. This is the idea behind "same writing."

From then on, people in different places had a closer shared way to write.

Carriage wheel tracks were also made the same size. Roads were repaired by the same rules. This is the idea behind "same wheel tracks."

Later China was able to stay connected through writing for a very long time.

Qin's step was harsh in many ways, but this part mattered deeply.

---

## Burning Books and Killing Learned People: The Darkest Page

Qin Shi Huang also did something people have criticized for more than 2,000 years: burning books and killing learned people who studied and taught old ideas.

He ordered many books destroyed, especially books from different thinkers and groups.

Books about Qin history, medicine, fortune-telling, and farming trees were allowed to stay. Many others were burned.

People who talked openly about old classics could be punished. People who used the past to criticize the present could face terrible danger.

According to *Records of the Grand Historian*, some learned people later criticized him, and more than 460 people were put to death.

This is the darkest mark on Qin Shi Huang's life.

He wanted to use fear to force people's minds into one shape.

But ideas are hard to kill.

Some burned books were secretly hidden in walls, tombs, or people's memories.

When the Qin dynasty fell, many of those ideas came back out from hidden corners.

Ideas can live longer than empires.

---

## The Tomb and the Terracotta Army: An Underground Empire

Qin Shi Huang feared death. But he knew death would come.

So he wanted to build an empire underground too.

Ancient books describe his tomb in amazing ways: palaces under the earth, a ceiling like the night sky, rivers made with mercury, and hidden crossbows guarding the entrance.

Which details are true? Which are only old records? We still cannot say for sure.

But we do know this: the tomb is enormous, and many of its secrets are still unopened.

Qin Shi Huang's main tomb has not been opened today.

It is not because people are not curious.

It is because protecting the objects inside matters more.

When the Terracotta Army was dug up, bright paint on the clay soldiers quickly broke down after touching the air. That lesson was painful.

The underground palace may keep sleeping for a long time.

It lies quietly at the foot of Mount Li, waiting until people have better ways to protect what they find.

---

## Summary

Qin Shi Huang became king at thirteen, took full power at twenty-two, and united the six states at thirty-nine.

In less than ten years, Qin destroyed six rival states.

After that, he made writing, wheel tracks, measurements, and laws more the same across the land.

He did things that changed history in huge ways.

He also did cruel things, including burning books and killing learned people.

Even today, it is hard to describe him with only "good" or "bad."

He built unity with power, fear, rules, and ambition.

That is why his shadow still stands so large in Chinese history.
