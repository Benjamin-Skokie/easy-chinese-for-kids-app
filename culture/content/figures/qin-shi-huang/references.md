# Editorial References: Qin Shi Huang

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian*, “Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin”

URL: https://ctext.org/shiji/qin-shi-huang-ben-ji/zh
- https://ctext.org/shiji/qin-shi-huang-ben-ji/ens

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ying Zheng's accession at age thirteen; Qin's already extensive territory and strong state apparatus at that time.
- The conquest sequence, creation of the title *huangdi* in 221 BCE, commandery-and-county government, imperial tours, construction programs, northern campaign, and death at Shaqiu in 210 BCE.
- Li Si's 213 BCE book-burning proposal, its exemptions, and the separate investigation and punishment of more than 460 learned men in 212 BCE.
- The search for immortality, expeditions by *fangshi*, and the literary description of the mausoleum's mercury waterways and mechanical crossbows.

Editorial boundary:

- Sima Qian wrote in the Han dynasty roughly a century after Qin Shi Huang's death; the text is a foundational historical source, not a contemporary transcript.
- Direct speeches, private motives, exact labor totals, court scenes, and the palace succession narrative are identified as the *Records'* account rather than eyewitness certainty.
- “More than 460” is not presented as an independently verified victim list, and the disputed word *keng* is not silently converted into one certain execution method.

## 2. Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian*, “Biographies of the Assassins”

URL: https://ctext.org/shiji/ci-ke-lie-zhuan/zh
- https://ctext.org/shiji/ci-ke-lie-zhuan/zhs

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The narrative of Crown Prince Dan, Jing Ke, Qin Wuyang, the map, concealed dagger, torn sleeve, pursuit, court physician Xia Wuju, and failed assassination.
- The chapter's own closing source chain, which connects some information to people acquainted with Xia Wuju and others said to know the affair.

Editorial boundary:

- The assassination attempt and principal people are treated as historically grounded, while dialogue, expressions, and moment-by-moment action remain literary narration recorded later.
- The story is explicitly not treated as a video-like record of the Qin court.
- “Running around a pillar” is familiar in later retellings; the article does not claim that every modern visual detail appears identically in the earliest account.

## 3. Qin chronology, title, administration, and standardization — Metropolitan Museum of Art

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2017/age-of-empires/exhibition-galleries
- https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/qin-dynasty-221-206-b-c
- https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Arts_of_Ancient_China_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art_Bulletin_v_32_no_2_1973_1974.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ying Zheng's dates, accession in 247 BCE, unification in 221 BCE, new imperial title, centralized commanderies, and Qin's standardization of script, currency, weights, and measures.
- Qin and Han road and canal networks and the long institutional legacy of the short Qin dynasty.
- Qin was already militarily dominant before the final conquests.

Editorial boundary:

- “Standardized” is not expanded into “invented.” Writing, money, measurement, roads, and territorial administration all had histories before 221 BCE.
- Museum overview language about a universal script is softened in light of archaeological scholarship showing gradual, uneven implementation.
- Political unification is not equated with cultural sameness or universal acceptance.

## 4. Thies Staack, “The Pragmatics of Standardization: Document Standards and Their Implementation in Qin Administration”

URL: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0041977X23000228

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- More than 38,000 written wooden documents from Liye provide archaeological evidence for Qin administration in a recently conquered region.
- Standardization can be studied as actual administrative practice rather than only as a single imperial command.
- Qin documents show both structured norms and the practical questions involved in making records compatible across offices.

Editorial boundary:

- The article presents implementation as a process, not as all local practice changing instantly in 221 BCE.
- No exact invented day is assigned to the disappearance of regional writing or measurement habits.
- “Same writing” is not interpreted as everyone speaking the same language.

## 5. Imre Galambos, “The Myth of the Qin Unification of Writing in Han Sources”

URL: https://real.mtak.hu/46320/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Archaeological writing from before and after Qin unification does not show a sudden, comprehensive replacement by one completely uniform script.
- Even official edict plates attached to standardized weights display variation in script.
- The familiar Han-era story of a single complete script reform requires qualification.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not deny Qin efforts to establish written norms; it distinguishes policy, later memory, and uneven practice.
- The children's story avoids claiming that every older character form vanished or that small seal script became everyone's handwriting overnight.

## 6. Qin frontier walls, state extraction, and labor

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522747/
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/making-use-of-the-land-the-political-ecology-of-chinas-first-empire/5C80E8CC40548B2953F72246C49A6CF7
- https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10216532

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qin efforts connected walls earlier built along the northern limits of Warring States polities rather than creating all northern walls from nothing.
- Qin government drew heavily on household registration, taxation, military service, corvée, forced relocation, grain, and transport.
- Recent archaeological modeling emphasizes the large direct labor and logistical burden of Qin-Han frontier defenses and its possible contribution to political collapse.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not give a precise death toll or repeat ancient mobilization figures as surviving payroll records.
- The wall is described as a changing frontier system, not the mostly Ming brick monument seen by tourists today.
- Infrastructure benefits do not erase coercion; coercion does not mean that every worker had an identical status or experience.

## 7. Wilt L. Idema, *Meng Jiangnü Brings Down the Great Wall: Ten Versions of a Chinese Legend*

URL: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvcwnjwg
- https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780295800127_A26577239/preview-9780295800127_A26577239.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Meng Jiangnü story exists in many versions, genres, periods, and regions, with major elements changing according to the tellers' purposes.
- Its familiar connection to Qin Shi Huang and a conscripted wall builder is a developed legend, not a Qin administrative record.
- The legend became a powerful vehicle for grief, loyalty, labor suffering, and resistance to oppressive authority.

Editorial boundary:

- Meng Jiangnü is not treated as a documented Qin worker's wife.
- The story is valued as evidence of cultural memory and changing storytelling, not used to calculate wall deaths or reconstruct an event.

## 8. Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor — UNESCO World Heritage Centre

URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/441/
- https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/5928
- https://whc.unesco.org/archive/2013/whc13-37com-8e-en.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The mausoleum complex began in the third century BCE and includes the burial mound, walls, architectural remains, many accompanying pits, terracotta warriors and horses, chariots, and weapons.
- The Terracotta Army site is about 1.5 kilometers east of the burial mound and is only one part of the wider complex.
- The archaeological site was discovered in 1974; many statues and features remain unexcavated.
- Conservation and site management are central considerations in archaeological work.

Editorial boundary:

- The Terracotta Army is not described as the opened central tomb or as a portrait gallery of named soldiers.
- The article says the main burial chamber has not been fully excavated; it does not claim that its contents are already known.
- The modern illustration is labeled as art rather than a site photograph or accurate reconstruction.

## 9. Mercury surveys at the burial mound

URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0375674285900160
- https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-67305-x

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A 1980s soil survey reported a strong mercury anomaly over the central part of the burial mound.
- A 2020 atmospheric lidar study detected mercury emissions above the mound and evaluated mercury as a tracer gas.
- These results are relevant to Sima Qian's much earlier account of mercury used in the tomb.

Editorial boundary:

- A mercury anomaly is described as consistent with the textual account, not proof of flowing rivers, their layout, or an exact quantity.
- Mercury detected at the mausoleum is not evidence taken from Qin Shi Huang's body and does not establish his cause of death.
- Claims that an immortality elixir killed him remain hypotheses unless direct medical evidence is found.

## 10. Image audit

Files reviewed:

- `images/young-king-ying-zheng.webp`
- `images/making-rules-match.webp`
- `images/terracotta-army.webp`

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- All three files are modern illustration-style images rather than photographs of Qin Shi Huang or complete documentary views of Qin events.
- The young king image imagines a face, throne room, clothing, crown, and attendants.
- The standards image combines symbolic writing, weights, coins, vehicles, road, and architecture; their exact forms and scale should not be read as one archaeological assemblage.
- The Terracotta Army image is a stylized reconstruction whose figures, arrangement, colors, lighting, and preservation do not match a documentary excavation photograph.

Editorial boundary:

- Captions explicitly identify what each image cannot prove.
- No visual detail is used as evidence for Qin clothing, architecture, standardized dimensions, excavation condition, or Qin Shi Huang's appearance.
