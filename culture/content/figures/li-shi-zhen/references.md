# Li Shizhen — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Life, family, examinations, and medical career

Source: Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Biographie de Li Shizhen”
URL: https://classes.bnf.fr/dossitsm/b-lishiz.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li Shizhen lived from 1518 to 1593, was born in Qizhou in present-day Hubei, and came from a family connected with medicine and medicinal trade.
- He entered district study at about fourteen, did not succeed in three later examination attempts, and returned to the family medical path.
- Later biographies connect him with the Prince of Chu's establishment and the Imperial Medical Institute in Beijing.
- He practiced medicine, wrote other medical works, and brought textual access and medical experience to his materia medica project.

Editorial boundary:

- The source says he probably served as an ordinary physician at the Imperial Medical Institute; exact offices and timing are not presented as perfectly settled.
- The article does not repeat dramatic cure-and-promotion scenes as documented fact.
- Passing into local student status is explained rather than treating *xiucai* as a simple modern degree.

Source: Hong Kong Education Bureau, “The Biography of Li Shizhen”
URL: https://www.edb.gov.hk/attachment/en/curriculum-development/kla/eng-edu/famous_scientists/Common/files/Reading_1_Li_Shizhen.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Educational summaries consistently report local examination success at fourteen, three failed higher examinations, and study with his physician father.
- Li later became known for medical practice and spent decades compiling *Bencao Gangmu*.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a modern educational biography, not a contemporary Ming diary.
- Its storytelling details are used only where they agree with stronger library and historical sources; dialogue, private emotions, and exact family scenes are not invented.

## Collaboration, sources, and limits of “lone genius” stories

Source: Paul U. Unschuld and collaborators, introduction to the *Ben cao gang mu Dictionary* project, University of California Press
URL: https://content.ucpress.edu/chapters/12851.ch01.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li directed the compilation and wrote that family members supported the project; the true size of the team is unknown.
- The work gathered herbal, animal and human, mineral, and manufactured substances from many old texts and contemporary regional sources.
- Its broad net produced heterogeneous data from different periods and cultural settings.
- The text preserves demonological illness terms and exorcistic or apotropaic therapies alongside other pharmaceutical knowledge.
- Modern philological checking finds copying changes and probable writing errors in some quotations.

Editorial boundary:

- The book is not described as the unaided creation of one isolated man.
- The earlier sentence claiming that family members “helped him find things and sort his notes” has been removed. The evidence supports family participation but does not establish those exact jobs.
- “He investigated widely” is not expanded into a precise national travel route, a claim that he handled all 1,892 substances, or a claim that he personally tested every medicine.
- Historical treatment claims and illness names do not map automatically onto modern diagnoses or proven therapies.

Source: National Archives Administration of China, *General Catalog for Chinese Medical Classics in China* entry for *Ben Cao Gang Mu*
URL: https://www.saac.gov.cn/mowcn/cn/c100513/202104/156cc3110b0147a3bc6b426a7f7683d4/files/f6d2857b2caa4e629c2572c61cf41cfa.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- The early edition credits Li Shizhen's descendants with revising the text and preparing its illustrations.
- This gives a specific, documented form of family participation without inventing note-sorting or fieldwork roles.

Source: Cambridge University Press, Federico Marcon, “The ‘book’ as fieldwork: ‘textual institutions’ and nature knowledge in early modern Japan”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjhs-themes/article/book-as-fieldwork-textual-institutions-and-nature-knowledge-in-early-modern-japan/537F9296139E71D87DD189258E7D2C10
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Later materia medica study joined canonical texts to actual specimens, fieldnotes, group observation, glossaries, and commentary.
- Readers repeatedly revised, supplemented, and localized *Bencao Gangmu* rather than treating it as a frozen final answer.

Editorial boundary:

- Later Japanese field practice does not prove an identical itinerary or method for Li himself.
- The source is used to show how knowledge grows through communities of books and observers, not to retroactively call every premodern practice “modern science.”

## Structure, counts, illustrations, and edition-dependent numbers

Source: Library of Congress, “Ben cao gang mu: Wu shi er juan, fu tu er juan”
URL: https://www.loc.gov/item/2021666452/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The 1603 edition has 52 *juan* of text and two *juan* of illustrations.
- Its catalog record counts 1,892 entries, including 374 additions associated with Li, and about 11,000 prescriptions.
- The record identifies Wang Shizhen's 1590 preface and Zhang Dingsi's 1603 reprint preface.
- The digitized copy lets readers examine woodblock-printed pages and illustrations as physical-book evidence.

Editorial boundary:

- “About 1,100 illustrations” is identified as a common summary count; image sets and counting can vary by edition and copy.
- “About 11,000 prescriptions” is preferable to false precision when sources use different rounding; one traditional count is 11,096.
- Entries are medicinal substances or materia medica items, not 1,892 modern, clinically proven drugs.

Source: National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University College of Medicine OpenCourseWare, “Bencao gangmu (Compendium of Materia Medica)”
URL: https://ocw.nycu.edu.tw/?p=163599&post_type=course_page
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The work contains mineral-related categories such as water, fire, earth, metals, and stones; plant categories; and animal categories.
- Modern study treats the book as important to East Asian medical history, natural history, classification, and pharmaceutical education.

Editorial boundary:

- The classification is historically systematic but not the same as modern evolutionary taxonomy.
- The presence of plants, foods, animals, minerals, and manufactured substances makes “herbal encyclopedia” too narrow.

Source: PubMed, “Literature research of *Compendium of Materia Medica* quoted from *Bencao Tujing*”
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33794582/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li quoted earlier illustrated materia medica extensively, deleted repetition, added missing material, and corrected some errors.
- One issue he addressed was mismatch between pictures and explanatory text.

Editorial boundary:

- Correcting many earlier problems does not mean Li corrected every error or that all retained reports were independently verified.
- “Scientific” in a modern paper's praise is not expanded into the claim that the sixteenth-century work used every method of modern science.

## Compilation and publication chronology

Source: UNESCO Memory of the World, “Ben Cao Gang Mu (Compendium of Materia Medica)”
URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/ben-cao-gang-mu-bencaogangmu-compendium-materia-medica?hub=1081
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li compiled the work over about twenty-seven years and completed a first draft in 1578.
- The compendium covers plants, animals, minerals, and other substances believed to possess medicinal properties.
- It was inscribed on the Memory of the World Register in 2011.

Editorial boundary:

- UNESCO recognition concerns documentary heritage, not modern approval of every treatment claim.
- The word “believed” is essential: historical inclusion does not prove efficacy or safety.

Source: UNESCO Memory of the World nomination document, “Ben Cao Gang Mu”
URL: https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/china_materia_medica.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The nomination describes a Jinling edition printed by Hu Chenglong and dates it to 1593.
- The preserved rare book provides evidence for the physical history of early editions.

Editorial boundary:

- Some catalogs date the edition to 1593, when block cutting began, while current publication histories commonly give 1596 for the issued first edition.
- Bibliographic dates alone cannot prove a detailed deathbed scene or exactly what Li saw before his death in 1593.
- The children's story uses 1596 for the first issued edition and treats 1593 as the beginning of block cutting, not as proof that Li held a finished printed set.

## Transmission and transformation outside China

Source: Cambridge University Press, Federico Marcon, “The ‘book’ as fieldwork: ‘textual institutions’ and nature knowledge in early modern Japan”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjhs-themes/article/book-as-fieldwork-textual-institutions-and-nature-knowledge-in-early-modern-japan/537F9296139E71D87DD189258E7D2C10
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Hayashi Razan officially introduced the book in Japan in 1607.
- Japanese local reprints began in 1637; later editions added reading marks, illustrations, annotations, and further observations.
- For about two centuries, Japanese materia medica scholars used the work as a major framework while continually updating and localizing it.

Editorial boundary:

- Transmission is not described as passive copying or universal agreement.
- Japanese scholars had to reconcile Chinese names and descriptions with different local plants and animals.

Source: Oxford University Press, Sooyoung An, “How Chinese Materia Medica Informed Nineteenth-Century British Pharmaceutical Knowledge”
URL: https://academic.oup.com/shm/article-pdf/35/3/996/45627439/hkac014.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Nineteenth-century British pharmaceutical researchers consulted *Bencao Gangmu* and other Chinese sources.
- Reception involved collaboration with Sinologists and processes of selecting, translating, omitting, and testing information.

Editorial boundary:

- European interest is not presented as simple acceptance or as the moment the book became “scientific.”
- The historical circulation of knowledge occurred within unequal imperial and intellectual networks as well as scholarly exchange.

Source: National Library of Medicine catalog, “Compendium of materia medica: bencao gangmu”
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nlmcatalog/101213390
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Luo Xiwen's 2003 edition is cataloged as the first full English translation of the entire compendium.
- The translation comprises 52 volumes arranged in six physical books and includes illustrations and references.

Editorial boundary:

- Earlier European readers encountered selected material and partial translations; they did not possess this complete English translation centuries earlier.

## Modern safety boundary

Source: U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Natural Doesn't Necessarily Mean Safer, or Better”
URL: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/know-science/natural-doesnt-mean-better
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Natural substances can be toxic, cause serious side effects, or fail to show benefit when studied.
- Plants and herbal products contain chemical compounds, and safety depends on the substance, preparation, and exposure.

Editorial boundary:

- Historical use is not modern proof of effectiveness or safety.
- The story gives no dosage, preparation instructions, or recommendation to use any remedy.

Source: U.S. National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements, “Botanical Dietary Supplements: Background Information”
URL: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/botanicalbackground-Consumer/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Botanical safety depends on chemical makeup, effects in the body, preparation, and amount.
- Concentrated preparations can have different risks from familiar foods or teas.

Editorial boundary:

- Children are explicitly told not to gather, taste, identify, mix, or self-prescribe materials from the book or internet.
- Illness, accidental ingestion, or poisoning requires a trusted adult and qualified medical or poison-control help.
