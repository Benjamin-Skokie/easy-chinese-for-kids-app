## Core biography, disputed origins, court service, and exile

Source: Academy of Chinese Studies, “Li Bai”
URL: https://chiculture.org.hk/en/china-five-thousand-years/1937
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li Bai's ancestral home, family background, and birthplace are not definitively established across surviving sources.
- A widely accepted reconstruction places his birth near Suyab and his childhood in Sichuan, but this remains a reconstruction rather than an uncontested birth record.
- Emperor Xuanzong summoned him to Chang'an in 742 to serve as a *Hanlin daizhao*, a literary attendant awaiting assignments.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not state that Suyab is certainly his birthplace or invent a profession, ethnicity, or secret history for his father.
- “Hanlin attendant” is explained as a palace literary role, not translated into a misleading modern government title.

Source: Poetry Foundation, “Li Po”
URL: https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poets/li-po
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Li Bai was probably born in Central Asia, grew up in Sichuan, traveled widely, and was appointed to the Hanlin Academy in 742.
- His court career was brief, and his surviving image as a wandering poet covers only part of a life that also included political hopes and setbacks.

Editorial boundary:

- General biographical orientation from this source is checked against Tang-era prefaces, tomb inscriptions, the Tang histories, and Li Bai's own poems for contested details.

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Li Hanlin Ji* biographical materials
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?if=en&res=775723
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang and later biographical materials disagree on Li Bai's origins and some stages of his life.
- He was imprisoned after his involvement with Prince Li Lin, sentenced to exile in Yelang, and pardoned before completing the journey.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim certainty about Li Bai's motives for joining Prince Li Lin or present him as either clearly innocent or knowingly rebellious.
- His political choices and their consequences are included instead of reducing his life to wine and travel.

## Poems and textual transmission

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Li Taibai Quanji*, volume 6, including “Quiet Night Thought”
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=810697&if=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A transmitted collected-text version reads “床前看月光” and “举头望山月,” rather than the two “明月” phrases familiar in current school recitation.
- The Chinese quotation in the article preserves the familiar modern version while explicitly explaining that variants exist.

Editorial boundary:

- No one surviving printed edition is treated as Li Bai's autograph manuscript.
- English translations in the article are original child-facing renderings for this project, not copied from a modern copyrighted translator.

Source: China Writers Association, “李白《静夜思》的文本奥秘”
URL: https://www.chinawriter.com.cn/n1/2020/1116/c404064-31932366.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Song and Ming printed sources preserve several variants of “Quiet Night Thought.”
- The combination familiar today developed through later anthologies and editorial transmission; it should not be presented as an unchanged autograph text surviving from the Tang.

Editorial boundary:

- The article gives children the basic history of variant readings without deciding speculative questions such as whether “床” means a bed or a well railing in the original scene.
- The familiar text is not called “wrong” or “fake”; it is described as a historically transmitted version.

Source: Chinese Text Project, *Quan Tang Shi*, “Viewing the Waterfall at Mount Lu, Two Poems”
URL: https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=gb&node=139483
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The quoted Chinese line “飞流直下三千尺，疑是银河落九天” belongs to Li Bai's transmitted poetry.
- Exaggeration and cosmic comparison are central to the poem's effect.

Editorial boundary:

- Poetic language is evidence of literary technique, not literal measurement or a record of an exact physical viewing position.
- The retained waterfall image is labeled as modern poetic imagination, not historical documentation.

## Li Bai and Du Fu

Source: Poetry Foundation, “A Bird Translates Silence”
URL: https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/158883/a-bird-translates-silence
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Du Fu met Li Bai in 744, and Du Fu's surviving poems preserve admiration, memory, and concern for the older poet.
- Their in-person meetings were limited even though the friendship became enormously important in literary memory.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not invent daily conversations, years of shared travel, or a modern “best friends” partnership.
- The poems support a real relationship but do not document every private feeling or event.

## Iron pestle and court legends

Source: Zhu Mu, Southern Song dynasty, *Fangyu Shenglan*, “Grinding an Iron Pestle into a Needle” (public-domain text presentation)
URL: https://www.gushiwen.cn/shiwenv_0251658e318b.aspx
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A Song dynasty geographical work records the traditional account of young Li Bai meeting an old woman grinding an iron pestle into a needle.
- The text itself introduces the story as something handed down by tradition.

Editorial boundary:

- The source is centuries later than Li Bai and cannot establish a witnessed childhood event.
- The story is presented as a perseverance tale attached to Li Bai, not as verified biography.

Source: *Gujin Shiwen Leiju*, “Gao Lishi Removes the Boots” tradition (public-domain text presentation)
URL: https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/SK1970a/chapter/1leppqama9gyr
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Later literary collections transmitted the tale of a drunken Li Bai ordering Gao Lishi to remove his boots and connected it with Yang Guifei.

Editorial boundary:

- “Gao Lishi removes the boots” and “Yang Guifei grinds the ink” are labeled later court legends, not eyewitness history.
- The article does not use these stories to claim that Li Bai never cared about office or power.

## Death and the moon-catching legend

Source: Peking University journal archive, “关于李白‘捉月’传说——兼及临终传说的传记意义”
URL: https://ccj.pku.edu.cn/article/info?id=271014461
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The drowning-while-catching-the-moon story developed as a symbolic ending suited to Li Bai's poetic image.
- Sources nearer Li Bai's lifetime instead place his death amid illness at Dangtu; the exact medical cause is not securely known.

Editorial boundary:

- The article labels “catching the moon” as legend and does not claim that Li Bai drowned.
- It says the historical evidence better supports death after illness, without inventing a diagnosis.
