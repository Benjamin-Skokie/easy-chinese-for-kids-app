# Cloud Hair and Flower Faces: Tang Women's Hair Buns and Gold Pins

## Architecture on the Head

Tang dynasty women built their hair like architecture.

They were not satisfied with a simple ponytail or braid.

They wanted to build *ji*, or hair buns.

High buns, cloud buns, half-turned buns, startled-swan buns, thrown-home buns, falling-horse buns: the names alone make a long list.

Some buns were high. Some were low. Some rose upward. Some folded backward. From the side, they could look like a flower, a cloud, or a bird about to turn.

When Bai Juyi wrote about Yang Guifei's "cloud hair, flower face, and gold *buyao*," he was describing this glowing feeling around the whole head.

What if a woman's own hair was not enough?

Then she used false hair, padding, or frames to help.

Tang noblewomen could place hairpieces under real hair or use other materials to hold the shape up.

Some later descriptions and modern recreations talk about wooden molds or fixed hair shells. Details may have differed from place to place.

But one thing is clear:

Tang women were very good at using tools to make hair tall and grand.

---

## The Rise and Fall of the Half-Turned Bun

The half-turned bun is often seen as one of the most typical hairstyles of the Tang dynasty.

In Dunhuang murals and tomb paintings, you can see high hair and backward-turning lines. The shape looks full of movement.

Later, tastes kept changing. Higher and more complicated styles appeared one after another.

In that age, hair really seemed like a contest.

Everyone wanted to comb the clouds of the sky onto her head.

---

## A Head Full of Pearls and Green: The Secret of Hairpins

After making a high bun, the work was not finished.

You still had to put things into it.

Hairpins, double-pronged pins, *buyao*, combs, and forehead flowers could fill the hair with shining ornaments.

When a woman walked, they could make tiny ringing sounds.

Rules about who could use gold, silver, or kingfisher-blue feather decorations changed by time and event. But richer materials usually belonged closer to wealthy and powerful families.

In the palace, phoenix hairpins and *buyao* could flash under lamplight like stars hung in the hair.

The most beautiful ornament was often the gold *buyao*.

It was not a stiff pin. It was made to tremble gently with movement.

Take one step, and it swayed.

Read Bai Juyi's line "cloud hair, flower face, gold *buyao*," and you can almost see someone slowly walking closer, with gold light shaking above her head.

---

## Huadian: A Tiny Flower on the Forehead

Tang women also placed decorations on the forehead.

These were called *huadian*.

They were not real tattoos. A woman cut a very thin piece of material and pasted it between the brows or on the forehead.

Simple ones could be small dots. Complicated ones could be plum blossoms, birds, or cloud shapes.

There is a famous story about Shangguan Wan'er and plum-blossom makeup. One version says she used it to cover a scar on her forehead, and then women in the palace copied the style.

This story is often told as a beauty legend. It is memorable, even if we should treat it like a story, not a certain record.

The materials for *huadian* could be special too. Some shone like pearl. Some had a bright blue-green glow.

Later, tastes changed, and this little flower on the forehead did not stay popular forever.

Makeup changes.

The taste of an age changes too.

But when you look at Tang images, you may still feel that women of the high Tang did not even leave a tiny shine on the forehead to chance.

---

## Summary

Tang women built architecture with hair.

They used hairpins and *buyao* to turn the head into a tiny shining tower. Forehead flowers, gold light near the temples, and swinging beads made a person look as if she had walked out of a painting.

They were doing more than decorating the face.

They were combing the lively confidence of their age onto the top of the head.
