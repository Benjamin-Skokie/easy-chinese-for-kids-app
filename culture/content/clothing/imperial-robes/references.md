# “一件黄衣服能证明什么？”— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Yellow and changing dress rules

Source: *New Book of Tang*, “Treatise on Chariots and Attire,” Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=383137&if=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang Gaozu used ocher-yellow robes as regular court wear.
- Some red-yellow imperial shades were restricted from officials and commoners, while yellow and white also appear as permitted colors for lower-status wearers in parts of the same dress record.

Editorial boundary:

- Tang “yellow” was not one modern standardized color. The story does not claim that every yellow object was universally forbidden or that one Tang rule continued unchanged through every later dynasty.

Source: Palace Museum, “清代宫廷服饰制度及其文化内涵”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/classify_detail/262014.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qing regulations distinguished bright yellow, apricot yellow, golden yellow, blue, and blue-black according to wearer and garment category.
- Bright yellow was used by the emperor, empress dowager, and empress, so it was not a male-emperor-only clue.
- Qing court wardrobes included formal, festive, regular, travel, military, rain, and informal clothing.

Editorial boundary:

- The detailed color and rank system is identified specifically as Qing. It is not projected backward onto the Qin, Han, Tang, Song, Yuan, or Ming courts.

Source: Palace Museum, “皇帝朝服”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/241579.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qing imperial court robes could be bright yellow, blue, red, or pale moon-white for different ritual occasions.
- Twelve Symbols, five-clawed dragons, and color were regulated together on Qing court dress.

Editorial boundary:

- “The emperor wore yellow” is not treated as a complete description of Qing ceremonial clothing.

## Dragons, rank, and garment type

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Peacocks and Dragons, Oh My!”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/peacocks-and-dragons
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Five-clawed dragons could identify a Qing imperial-family wearer without proving that the wearer was the emperor.
- A robe with nine dragons but without the imperial Twelve Symbols can be distinguished from an emperor’s robe.

Editorial boundary:

- This identification concerns specific Qing objects. It is not used as a timeless dragon-claw rule for every Chinese dynasty.

Source: Palace Museum, “吉服袍”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/241311.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qing festive robes were worn for birthdays, holidays, and other celebratory occasions.
- The regulated imperial festive robe included nine golden dragons and the Twelve Symbols.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses “dragon robe” cautiously because modern English and Chinese labels can blur distinctions among festive robes, court robes, outer coats, women’s dress, and garments for other imperial-family ranks.

Source: Palace Museum, “月白色缎绣彩云金龙纹男夹朝袍”
URL: https://intl.dpm.org.cn/Textiles/85236.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A surviving Qing imperial court robe shows how dragon number and placement, Twelve Symbols, color, materials, and advanced *kesi* techniques worked together.
- Its pale moon-white color is connected to the ritual of worshipping the moon, demonstrating that an emperor’s highest formal clothing was not always yellow.

Editorial boundary:

- One surviving robe is an object case study, not a template for all dynasties or all Qing clothing.

## Twelve Symbols: texts, regulations, and objects

Source: Palace Museum, “十二章”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/241316.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The received Twelve Symbols list and conventional later interpretations of their meanings.
- Their long association with regulated rank and imperial virtue.

Editorial boundary:

- The account of origins in the legendary age of Shun and Yu belongs to classical tradition, not archaeological proof of surviving garments from that era.
- Symbol meanings are presented as historical interpretations, not as fixed meanings that every viewer held in every century.

Source: Palace Museum, “明清紫禁城的物质文化”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2024/07/22/u669dd42b7fb7d.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ming imperial *gunmian* served as the highest ceremonial ensemble for sacrifices, major audiences, and investitures.
- Dingling yielded a reconstructed crown and two *kesi* Twelve-Symbol garments labeled *gunfu*.

Editorial boundary:

- The article distinguishes excavated Ming material from classical descriptions and from Qing court dress rather than blending them into one costume.

Source: Palace Museum, Fang Hongjun, “清代后妃礼、吉服为何装饰十二章纹？”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2020/05/12/u5eba19a2e4895.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Surviving Qing women’s formal and festive robes can carry Twelve Symbols even where prescriptive texts emphasize imperial male use.
- Court regulations and surviving material practice do not always align perfectly.

Editorial boundary:

- Women’s robes are identified by their own categories and users; they are not collapsed into the phrase “the emperor’s dragon robe.”

## Makers and textile labor

Source: Palace Museum, “清代宫廷服饰制度及其文化内涵”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/classify_detail/262014.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Court designs could be prepared in Beijing and sent to the Jiangning, Suzhou, and Hangzhou imperial weaving bureaus.
- The centers produced different textiles and used specialized techniques including brocade, satin, gauze, *kesi*, embroidery, and patterned velvet.

Editorial boundary:

- Surviving institutional descriptions foreground bureaus and officials more often than individual craftspeople. The article recognizes collective labor without inventing named workers, dialogue, or working conditions not documented by these sources.

Source: Palace Museum, “江南织造”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/243105.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Jiangning, Suzhou, and Hangzhou operated as Qing imperial textile-production institutions under the Imperial Household Department.

Editorial boundary:

- The bureau system is Qing-specific in this discussion, although earlier courts also maintained textile-production institutions.
