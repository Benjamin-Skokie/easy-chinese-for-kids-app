# Editorial References: Hanfu

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Gaku Kuroda — “Hanfu Revival Inspired Activities and Their Venues in Shanghai, China” (*Geographical Sciences*)

URL: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/chirikagaku/77/1/77_23/_article/-char/en

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Full-scale activity in the present-day hanfu revival began around 2003.
- Wearers differ in their commitment to historical authenticity; many accept freer combinations and do not follow historical formality or etiquette closely.
- Historical sightseeing sites became common gathering and photography spaces, both because the clothing feels appropriate there and because wearers face fewer unusual looks.

Editorial boundary:

- “2003” is described as a key public starting point for a modern movement, not the birthday of all historical Han Chinese clothing.
- Revival participants are not treated as one group with a single political, religious, or aesthetic motive.

## 2. “Fashion and Society in China in the 2000s: New Developments and Sociocultural Complexities”

URL: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316715897_Fashion_and_society_in_China_in_the_2000s_New_developments_and_sociocultural_complexities

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The public re-emergence of hanfu can be traced to highly visible dress and gatherings in 2003–2004.
- The contemporary definition was deliberately constructed broadly to include dress associated with multiple pre-Qing dynasties.
- Early public gatherings and online discussion helped make a modern category visually recognizable.

Editorial boundary:

- The current umbrella category is not projected backward as the ordinary name every ancient wearer used for every garment.
- The article distinguishes historical garment evidence from a modern movement's selection and organization of that evidence.

## 3. “Chinese Women in Hanfu: A Gendered Subject Narrative in the Cultural Nationalist Movement” (*Women's Studies*)

URL: https://doi.org/10.1080/00497878.2025.2483459

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Contemporary hanfu is a modern reconstruction and identity practice, interacting with commercial fashion and other dress systems.
- Women are central participants, consumers, public presenters, and makers of meaning within the movement.
- Hanfu can express cultural or national identity while also giving individuals room for aesthetic and gendered self-expression.

Editorial boundary:

- The story neither endorses a claim of culturally pure, unbroken clothing nor dismisses all revival practice as empty costume.
- Nationalist controversy is acknowledged, but it is not assigned as the motive of every wearer.

## 4. Zheng Huiwen and Lee Sang-Rye — “The Study of Contemporary Chinese Women's Hanfu Styles”

URL: https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART002549656

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Contemporary commercial hanfu can be grouped into historically oriented, improved, new, and mix-and-match styles.
- Modern designers vary shape, color, motif, fabric, and construction while drawing on historical references.
- Online retail and social-media examples show that “modern hanfu” is not one standardized outfit.

Editorial boundary:

- The study's typology is used to demonstrate present diversity, not imposed as the only correct classification.
- A product's online label is not treated as proof of archaeological accuracy.

## 5. China National Museum — Tang official and everyday-dress figures

URLs:

- https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252266.shtml
- https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252268.shtml
- https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/gudai/detail6.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang formal dress could include layered round- and crossed-collar garments, wide sleeves, skirts, headwear, belts, and many accessories.
- Rank could be marked through headwear, color, material, pattern, and accessories rather than one collar alone.
- Tang everyday dress widely used round collars, narrower sleeves, boots, belts, and riding-friendly forms connected with northern and Central Asian exchange.
- Men and women adopted and adapted clothing associated with “Hu” styles; those forms then continued to influence later Chinese and neighboring dress.

Editorial boundary:

- Exchange is described as mutual historical formation, not foreign “contamination” of a pure costume.
- A round collar, boot, or narrow sleeve is never used by itself to assign one ethnic identity to a real person.

## 6. Metropolitan Museum of Art — *When Silk Was Gold: Central Asian and Chinese Textiles*

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/zh/met-publications/when-silk-was-gold-central-asian-and-chinese-textiles

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Textiles, weaving methods, patterns, and luxury goods moved between China and Central Asia, especially in the Tang and Mongol periods.
- Trade, diplomatic gifts, empire, and religious connections moved styles across long distances.
- Exposure to foreign goods could stimulate new local artistic production.

Editorial boundary:

- “Chinese” and “Central Asian” are not treated as two sealed wardrobes.
- Movement of a motif or technique does not automatically identify the ethnicity of a later wearer or maker.

## 7. Rachel Silberstein — “Material Girls: Silk and Self-Fashioning in Tang China (618–907)” (*Fashion Theory*)

URL: https://doi.org/10.1080/1362704X.2016.1138679

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang dress regulations assigned colors and other signs to status, and law punished some violations.
- Expanding commerce, imitation, and competition weakened clothing's ability to communicate rank with perfect accuracy.
- Dress was a form of self-fashioning, not only passive obedience to a code.

Editorial boundary:

- Prescriptive rules are evidence for an ideal social order, not a photograph of universal practice.
- The article does not teach children to infer a person's exact rank from one modern illustration.

## 8. Metropolitan Museum of Art — “Man's Audience Robe (Chaofu)”

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/69062

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A fully pleated chaofu was formal Qing men's court dress.
- Mid-eighteenth-century regulations reserved five-clawed dragon robes for the emperor and heir apparent, but the museum notes that the rule was often overlooked in the nineteenth century.
- Nobles, high civil and military officials, and imperial guards wore blue-black audience robes.

Editorial boundary:

- The object demonstrates both regulation and imperfect enforcement.
- One surviving elite robe does not represent the daily clothing of all Qing subjects.

## 9. University of Washington — *Manchus and Han: Ethnic Relations and Political Power in Late Qing and Early Republican China*, “Separate and Unequal”

URL: https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/6dc24326-22ee-4cdc-9f66-52376c2d4f10/section/3622c09f-14c9-41ab-92ce-43dbf65db15a

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qing rules and institutions maintained distinctions between Manchu and Han subjects.
- Han women were not required to adopt Manchu dress in the same way as men and retained distinctive clothing traditions into the nineteenth century.
- Dress distinctions were linked with larger political and social inequalities.

Editorial boundary:

- The gender difference is not turned into the false claim that every Han woman wore unchanged Ming clothing for 250 years.
- “Han” and “Manchu” describe historically changing communities, not visually pure styles that never mixed.

## 10. Antonia Finnane — *Changing Clothes in China: Fashion, History, Nation*

URL: https://studylib.net/doc/26245913/changing-clothes-in-china--antonia-finnane-

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- By the late Qing, laboring Han men and women commonly wore jackets and trousers, while social position affected robes and outer garments.
- Manchu and Han men's clothing converged in important contexts, while Manchu and Han women retained visible distinctions that could also blur in practice.
- Qing-era clothing was shaped by mediation, fashion, gender, class, and modern change rather than a single top-down replacement.

Editorial boundary:

- The narrative does not reduce Qing dress history to “the Manchus banned all hanfu.”
- Coercive early Qing hair and dress policies remain visible; later adaptation is not used to excuse their violence.

## 11. Ashmolean Museum and Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art — Qing court robes

URLs:

- https://www.ashmolean.org/mans-formal-robes
- https://asia-archive.si.edu/new-arrival-a-robe-for-royalty/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Qing elite formal robes combined elements associated with Manchu riding dress and older Chinese court traditions.
- Narrower sleeves, trousers, boots, slits, and riding construction existed alongside pleated skirts, dragons, and inherited ritual symbols.
- Court clothing communicated imperial identity, status, and Manchu origins through a deliberately mixed system.

Editorial boundary:

- “Manchu” and “Chinese” elements are not described as two chemically pure sets.
- Court clothing is not generalized to every gender, class, region, or daily setting in the Qing empire.

## 12. S. A. Yatsenko — “Early Turks: Male Costume in Chinese Art”

URL: https://www.transoxiana.com.ar/14/yatsenko_turk_costume_chinese_art.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ethnic identification from costume in classical and medieval art is difficult and often approximate.
- Many early Tang tomb figures are schematic and omit details needed for secure clothing classification.
- Similar garments could belong to several regions, and people living in China could quickly adopt local costume elements.

Editorial boundary:

- No historical person's ethnicity is assigned from one collar, sleeve, boot, hairstyle, or modern drawing.
- Funerary art is treated as a specific visual source requiring archaeological context, not a candid photograph.
