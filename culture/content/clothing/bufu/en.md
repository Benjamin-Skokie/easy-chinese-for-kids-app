# Official Rank Badges: An "ID Card" on Clothing

## Wearing the Official Seal on the Chest

Before the Ming dynasty, official clothing already showed rank. But from far away, it was not always as easy to read as later *bufu*, or rank-badge robes.

By the Ming dynasty, the square badges sewn on the chest and back became especially clear.

Civil officials usually wore birds.

Military officials usually wore animals.

Different ranks used different designs.

That meant people could often look at an official's clothing and roughly know how high his rank was.

It was almost like wearing an ID card on the front of the robe.

---

## For Civil Officials, Look at Birds. For Generals, Look at Beasts.

Civil officials had birds embroidered on their rank badges.

First rank used the crane.

Second rank used the golden pheasant.

Third rank used the peacock.

Fourth rank used the wild goose.

Fifth rank used the silver pheasant.

Sixth rank used the egret.

Seventh rank used a water bird called *xichi*.

Eighth rank used the oriole.

Ninth rank used the quail.

The crane was treated as the highest bird, so top officials used the crane. A tiny ninth-rank official used the quail.

A crane rises into the blue sky. A quail hides in the grass.

The difference in status was easy to see.

Military officials wore land animals.

First rank used the *qilin*, a lucky mythical creature.

Second rank used the lion.

Third rank used the leopard.

Fourth rank used the tiger.

Fifth rank used the bear.

Sixth rank used a fierce animal called *biao*.

Seventh and eighth ranks often used the rhinoceros.

Ninth rank often used the sea horse.

Because the *qilin* was a noble creature from legend, it sat at the top.

If you look at court portraits, a group of officials can seem to be wearing their own bird-and-beast name tags on their chests.

From far away, the scene is both lively and very orderly.

---

## The Censor's Mythical Beast

One kind of official badge was especially unusual.

Censors and officials whose job was to criticize problems often used a one-horned mythical beast called the *xiezhi*.

In legend, the *xiezhi* could tell right from wrong. It would use its horn to strike a bad person.

So when people saw this design, they knew what kind of official it was:

someone who watched others, investigated, argued, and pointed out mistakes.

When officials in these watchdog offices wore the *xiezhi* badge, it was easy to think:

"This person is here to keep an eye on people."

Later, the *xiezhi* also became a symbol of fairness and law. Even today, many people still connect it with judging right and wrong.

---

## Qing Rank Badges: A Little More Human Detail

The Qing dynasty kept the rank-badge system from the Ming, but also changed some details.

For example, members of the emperor's family often used dragon or python-like designs, which were not the same as ordinary officials' badges.

The larger pattern for civil officials, from cranes down to quails, continued. Military badges also kept the bird-and-beast idea, though some details changed.

Qing official dress also had a very visible accessory:

court beads.

Rules decided who could wear them, how they should be worn, and when they should be worn.

If you stand before a Qing court portrait, you may notice that the badge is not the only clue. Even the string of beads around the neck helps tell people who this person is.

---

## When the Badge Did Not Match the Person

Rank badges looked strict and orderly, but the system was not perfect.

There were people in history who wore official clothing they should not have worn, used designs above their rank, or borrowed clothing to make themselves look more important.

That is why the court had to keep making rules: do not wear birds and beasts that do not belong to you.

The most interesting thing about *bufu* is that it made identity so visible.

If you were promoted, the badge was honor.

If you lost power, the badge could also remind everyone exactly where you now stood.

The bird or beast on your chest was more than a pattern.

It was a silent explanation of your place in the empire.

---

## Summary

In the Ming and Qing dynasties, officials often wore square rank badges on the chest and back of their robes.

Civil officials were read by birds. Military officials were read by beasts. Censors could wear the *xiezhi*, a mythical creature that knew right from wrong.

A *bufu* badge was like an identity card worn on the body. It embroidered a person's position in the empire clearly for others to see.
