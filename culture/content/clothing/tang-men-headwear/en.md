# Can a Hat Identify a Man from the Tang Dynasty?

## Clue One: Is It a Cloth Wrap, a Cap, a Crown, or a Futou?

Imagine that an archaeology team hands you a picture and lets you see only the top of the person's head. Would you call the object a cloth wrap, a cap, or a formal crown? Historical categories did not always fit into neat boxes. In general, a *jin* was associated with wrapped cloth, a cap was sewn or formed into shape, and a crown-like headpiece often belonged to ritual, rank, or formal dress. But words and shapes changed over time. A modern dictionary cannot permanently label every old object.

One of the best-known kinds of everyday headwear for men in the Tang dynasty was the *futou*. It had appeared before the Tang. Early forms were closer to gauze or cloth wrapped around a hair knot, with the corners tied in place. Tang makers also used an inner support called a *jinzi*. It could give the top a low, tall, rounded, or pointed outline. The two parts at the back were often called the futou's “feet.” They were not real feet, of course, but cloth corners or extensions that developed from them.

The man in the picture wears soft black cloth, so the image gives us a good question—but not an answer. We cannot see the cloth pattern or the support inside, and we do not know which date the artist used. The honest caption is not “This is an early Tang man.” It is “This is a modern idea of a soft wrapped form.” That is the detective's first rule: looking similar is not the same as being proved.

## Clue Two: Three Hats Are Not a Simple Evolution Line

Now examine the second picture. The left form is soft, the middle one is stiff, and the right one has two very long “wings.” It invites a smooth story: everyone wore soft cloth in the early Tang, everyone changed to a hard cap in the middle, and everyone grew wings by the end. Real history looks more like a branching tree. Several forms could exist at the same time, and an older style did not vanish the day a new one appeared.

Early Tang futou often had a lower top, and the feet at the back could hang softly. Later, some styles used taller inner supports. Cord, silk, or firmer materials could help lift the feet. By the late Tang, some futou had a much harder frame and could be put on like a cap instead of being wrapped each time. Makers in the Five Dynasties period continued experimenting with upward, crossed, and other arrangements. In the Song dynasty, stiff futou with horizontal extended feet—and many variations on them—became especially noticeable.

The extremely long horizontal wings on the right therefore work better as a comparison with Song and later hats. They are not evidence that every Tang official looked that way. Even the “feet” were not always the same: they could droop, rise, stiffen, cross, or extend. Shape can help date an image, but the clue becomes strong only when the artwork's date, findspot, and setting agree with it.

## Clue Three: Is Every Rider a General?

In the third picture, long streamers fly behind a rider and give him the dramatic entrance of a general. In a real battle, however, a soldier might need a helmet to protect his head. Warrior figures from Tang tombs do show helmets worn with armor. A military man might wear a futou when he was not armored, but one black hat is not enough reason to shout, “The general has arrived!”

Futou were used widely in the Tang and did not belong only to officials. One horse-handler figure in the National Museum of China wears a futou with a narrow-sleeved outfit suited to movement. Officials, attendants, performers, and other men may also wear futou in paintings and figures. On the other hand, a participant in a formal ceremony might wear a regulated crown, an armored warrior might wear a helmet, and people in different regions might use soft wraps, felt caps, rolled-brim hats, or pointed caps. What children, workers, and ordinary households could obtain—and what they were doing—also affected whether and how they covered the head.

The court did write dress regulations that connected colors, materials, and forms with rank and occasion. But a regulation tells us how people were supposed to dress. It is not a photograph of every street. Some people obeyed, some crossed the rules, and some changed clothes only for ceremonies. To guess an identity, a detective must also inspect the robe, belt, shoes, weapons, pose, and nearby figures. A hat can remove a few answers, but it cannot announce the answer alone.

## Clue Four: Can a “Foreign-Style Hat” Prove Someone's Identity?

Tang Chang'an and Luoyang brought together merchants, envoys, craftspeople, musicians, and soldiers from many regions. Hats, fabrics, and making techniques traveled with them. Tomb images show pointed, rolled-brim, felt, and other styles that researchers may group under the broad label “foreign-style hats.” At the same time, a camel rider described as a foreign visitor might wear a soft futou common in Tang China. Clothing had already crossed boundaries. A hat is not an ancestry test.

The historical Chinese label *hu* could refer broadly to people and things from north, west, or much farther away. It was not one modern ethnic group with a clear border. Painters and figure makers also used familiar faces, beards, poses, and hats to represent visitors from distant places. Such details may preserve useful information, but they could also be simplified, exaggerated, or repeated as artistic types.

When we see a pointed cap, the better questions are: “Where else does this shape appear? Where was the tomb? Is there an inscription, another object, or surviving cloth that supports the identification?” The poor method is to use one hat or face to assign an identity to someone who has been silent for more than a thousand years. The most interesting part of Tang headwear is not that every community had an unchanging uniform. It is that styles traveled, met, and were remade by local people.

## Final Clue: Tomb Figures and Murals Are Not Street Photos

Much of what we know about Tang futou comes from clay tomb figures, tomb murals, Dunhuang paintings, and portraits of donors. Each source had a job of its own. Civil officials, warriors, and attendants in a tomb could form a procession, display status, or provide symbolic protection. Dunhuang donor portraits showed people who supported a cave and its religious activities. An artist might aim for beauty, dignity, or an easily recognized role without recording every seam in a piece of cloth.

The findspot and position inside an image therefore matter. Did the hat come from a dated tomb, or is it an object later purchased without context? Is the figure guarding an entrance or following the tomb owner? After pigments faded, was a section that now looks black originally another color? A conclusion becomes stronger when the same shape appears in dated murals, figures, and written sources.

A modern reconstruction adds another layer of choices. Its maker must guess at hidden backs, materials, and tying methods, and may enlarge wings or streamers for dramatic effect. A reconstruction can help us form questions, but it cannot prove its own guesses. A careful “hat detective” writes a confidence label beside every answer: *possible*, *probable*, or *not enough evidence yet*.

---

## The Detective's Answer: A Hat Is a Clue, Not an ID Card

A hat may help us narrow a date or guess the occasion. It cannot securely identify a person's office, job, or community—and it certainly cannot place the long wings of a Song official's hat on every Tang man.

Behind one futou lie cloth corners, frames, fashion, regulations, and cultural exchange. Only when we combine it with the whole outfit, archaeological context, written records, and the purpose of the artwork does a “story about a black hat” become a real investigation supported by evidence.
