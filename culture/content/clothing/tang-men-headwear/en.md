# Futou and Black Gauze Caps: Men's Headwear in the Tang Dynasty

## A Crown Wrapped from One Piece of Cloth

Tang dynasty men often wore a kind of headwear that did not look like a modern hat.

It looked more like a wrapped headscarf.

More exactly, a man took a square piece of black gauze or thin cloth and wrapped it from the back of the head toward the front. The four corners were gathered above the forehead, and the remaining parts hung down behind the head.

This was called a *futou*.

Early *futou* were soft. They really looked like cloth wrapped around the head.

But soft cloth does not hold shape easily. So later, people began to add supports inside. First came inner frames or pads, and later came stiffer methods that could hold a fixed shape.

By the late Tang and Five Dynasties period, the *futou* had slowly moved from "wrapped cloth" toward something more like a hat.

---

## The "Class" Inside a Futou

If you walked down a street in Tang Chang'an, you might sometimes guess a man's identity or what kind of event he was attending by the shape of the *futou* on his head.

Emperors, the emperor's relatives, officials, musicians, and performers could appear in different forms of headwear in paintings.

One style, with parts lifted upward toward the sky, looked especially striking from far away. It was almost like two small horns pointing up.

The most impressive images were often of generals.

Helmet, cloak, and *futou* lines all moved together in the wind. When the horse ran, the bands behind the head flew backward like two small flags.

At one glance, you knew what the painter wanted:

this person should already win half the scene before doing anything.

---

## From Soft and Droopy to Hard and Stiff

After the Song dynasty, official headwear kept changing.

The black gauze cap became a very familiar image of an official's hat. It had long wings stretching out on both sides.

There is a popular story that Emperor Taizu of Song, Zhao Kuangyin, made the side wings long to stop officials from whispering to one another in court.

The story is fun and easy to remember.

But it is probably more like a later tale than a certain fact.

Still, the black gauze cap became so famous that it slowly turned into a symbol of office itself.

To "lose the black gauze cap" meant to lose an official job.

Something worn on the head had become a symbol of power in language.

---

## Futou Travels to Japan

Tang *futou* also influenced nearby places.

In some Japanese court headwear, people often think they can still see traces left by Tang dynasty styles.

---

## Summary

Tang men often wore *futou*. At first, it was more like wrapped black gauze. Later, it became more shaped and structured.

It was not just about looking good. It was tied to rank, ceremony, and the taste of the time.

Later, the black gauze cap became a way to talk about official position itself.

A piece of headwear walked into history, and then into everyday sayings.
