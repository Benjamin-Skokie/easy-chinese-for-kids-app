# 唐朝男子为什么要戴幞头？ — References and Editorial Notes

## 0. Long hair, care for the body, and variation

Source: Peking University journal platform, Lin Yun, “髡发种种”

URL: https://ccj.pku.edu.cn/article/info?id=286674275

Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Han Chinese men and women traditionally kept their hair long.
- The teaching that body and hair came from one's parents encouraged people not to damage or remove the hair casually.
- Cutting or shaving the hair could also carry meanings of punishment and shame in earlier Chinese history.

Editorial boundary:

- The article gives this as one important cultural reason, not a private motive shared by every Tang man.
- Tang China included communities with different hair customs. The child-facing text explicitly avoids claiming that every man wore one identical hairstyle.
- The teaching is not treated as a literal claim that nobody ever trimmed, cut, or shaved hair.

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Scissors” (Tang dynasty, 7th–9th century)

URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/42184

Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Tang China had scissors; the museum dates this surviving silver pair to the 7th–9th century.
- Long hairstyles cannot be explained by a lack of cutting tools.

Source: Alain Arrault, “Daily Activities in the Calendars of Medieval China (Ninth and Tenth Centuries): The Case of the Body Care Activities,” *The NCCU History Review* 45 (2016)

URL: https://tci.ncl.edu.tw/cgi-bin/gs32/gsweb.cgi?o=dnclresource&s=id%3D%22A16020895%22.&searchmode=basic&tcihsspage=tcisearch_opt1_search

Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Ninth- and tenth-century calendars list washing hair, washing the head, removing white hairs, and shaving the head among body-care activities.
- Medieval practice was more varied than the oversimplified claim that nobody ever cut or shaved hair.

## 1. Palace Museum — “幞头”

URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240564.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Futou began as cloth wrapped around the head and hair before the Tang, was also called a soft wrap, and was associated in early accounts with convenience during military activity.
- In Tang forms, two cloth corners were tied while two decorative parts hung behind as the “feet”; some feet were later reinforced and raised.
- By the late Tang, some futou used rigid framing, lining, and metal support and could be put on as a formed cap without wrapping it again in front of a mirror.
- Five Dynasties and Song futou developed upward, crossed, curled, and extended feet; headwear shape can help scholars date paintings.

Editorial boundary:

- The chronology is not turned into one smooth sequence followed by every wearer.
- Song extended-foot futou are not presented as the standard hat of all Tang men.

## 2. Palace Museum — “巾子幞头”

URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/239535.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A Tang futou could use two corners tied on top and two feet tied or hung behind.
- An inner support called a *jinzi* was widely used in the high Tang.
- Futou with inner support appeared on people from different backgrounds living in Tang China.

Editorial boundary:

- A futou is not used to assign ethnicity.
- The modern illustration does not reveal its hidden construction and is not labeled as an exact high-Tang reconstruction.

## 3. National Museum of China — “三彩釉陶牵马俑”

URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252267.shtml

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A Tang horse-handler figure wears a futou with a narrow-sleeved, turned-collar outfit.
- Futou were widely used in Tang men's everyday dress and were not restricted to senior officials.
- The museum notes that futou were so normal in Tang men's dress that even nearly unclothed wrestling performers were still shown wearing them.
- The museum summarizes changes in the height and shape of the top, inner support, and feet across the Tang.

Editorial boundary:

- One funerary figure is evidence for a represented role, not proof that every worker or every Tang man dressed identically.
- “Widely used” is not rewritten as “always worn by everyone.”

## 4. National Museum of China — Tang warrior figures

URLs:

- https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211116_252265.shtml

- Some warrior figures had a ritual or protective place within a tomb assemblage.

Editorial boundary:

- The local image `general-futou-in-wind.webp` is not treated as evidence that Tang generals fought in futou with long flying streamers.

## 5. National Museum of China — “大唐风华” exhibition

URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/Portals/0/web/zt/20180904dtfh/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang objects and paintings show soft futou, pointed or rolled-brim caps, male-style dress on some women, and clothing connected with exchange across regions.
- One camel-rider figure identified by the museum as a visitor from afar wears a black soft futou, showing that clothing categories crossed social and geographic boundaries.
- The exhibition presents tomb murals and figures as material evidence for Tang life, fashion, exchange, and belief.

Editorial boundary:

- “Hu” is treated as a broad historical label, not one modern ethnicity.
- No person's ancestry is inferred from one cap, beard, face, or modern reconstruction.

## 6. Li Donglei and Liang Hui'e — “唐代幞头形制变迁及其文化内涵”

URL: https://www.fx361.com/page/2019/1105/19412221.shtml

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Futou changed substantially within the Tang rather than having one fixed “Tang look.”
- Futou were used across social levels, while materials, construction, and forms differed.
- Crown and headwear systems could express status and political meaning.

Editorial boundary:

- The secondary web mirror is used only for broad points consistent with museum and journal sources.
- Court symbolism is not treated as a complete description of ordinary street practice.

## 7. “敦煌供养人幞头的形制变化” (*Journal of Ningxia Normal University*)

URL: https://gysb.cbpt.cnki.net/portal/journal/portal/client/paper/fd2cda00b9450addd9e21cffd10a6b2e

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Dunhuang donor images record changes from softer wrapped futou toward harder feet in different periods.
- Five Dynasties and Song donor images inherited and changed earlier forms.
- Regional religious images can add dated evidence to a broader chronology.

Editorial boundary:

- Donor portraits are portraits made within a religious and regional setting, not candid pictures of every Tang street.
- Dunhuang evidence is not automatically generalized to the whole empire.

## 8. Chen Juan and Li Fucheng — “承唐启宋的五代十国幞头形制及其成因” (*Journal of Silk*, 2023)

URL: https://doi.org/10.3969/j.issn.1001-7003.2023.11.18

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period was a major transition between Tang and Song futou forms.
- Some forms inherited Tang features, while other arrangements were new developments that fed into Song headwear.
- Comparing texts and images reveals multiple forms rather than a single linear evolution.

Editorial boundary:

- Iconic long horizontal Song wings are not moved backward onto all late-Tang officials.
- The story avoids giving one exact invention date for every type of foot or frame.

## 9. S. A. Yatsenko — “Early Turks: Male Costume in Chinese Art”

URL: https://www.transoxiana.com.ar/14/yatsenko_turk_costume_chinese_art.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ethnic identification from costume in medieval Chinese art is difficult and often approximate.
- Early Tang tomb figures may be schematic and omit details needed for secure clothing classification.
- Similar garments traveled across regions, and newcomers could adopt local forms.

Editorial boundary:

- No historical figure's community is assigned from one hat or facial feature.
- Tomb art is read together with archaeological context rather than treated as a photograph.

## 10. Beijing University Institute of Humanities and Social Sciences — “头饰背后的政治史”

URL: https://www.ihss.pku.edu.cn/templates/zs_zp/index.aspx?contentid=4022&nodeid=150&page=ContentPage

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Tang courts used headwear forms and dress rules to express political status and authority.
- Named futou styles changed within the dynasty and could be connected with specific court settings.

Editorial boundary:

- Prescriptive court dress is not treated as proof of what every person wore in daily life.
- A hat remains only one clue; full costume, occasion, text, and find context are required.

## Removed or withheld claims

- The former claim that Tang futou directly shaped specific Japanese court headwear was removed because the old story supplied no reliable source and the claim was not necessary to explain Tang men's headwear.
- The popular story that Song Taizu lengthened hat wings to stop officials whispering was removed from the child-facing text. It is a memorable later explanation, not evidence for Tang headwear, and it distracts from the securely supported chronology.
- “Black gauze hat” as a synonym for official office was removed because it belongs mainly to later headwear and language history, not the central Tang investigation.
