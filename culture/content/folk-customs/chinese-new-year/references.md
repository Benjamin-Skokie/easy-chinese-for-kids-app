# Guo Nian: The Story of Nian — References and Editorial Notes

This file supports fact-checking and does not render on the website.

## Current child-facing draft (2026-09-04)

- The article title is now `过年：年兽的故事 / Guo Nian: The Story of Nian`, matching its two-part path: first explaining what Chinese people mean by `过年`, then telling the Nian legend.
- The first page distinguishes `过年`, `春节 / Spring Festival`, `Chinese New Year`, and the broader English term `Lunar New Year` before moving directly into the Nian legend.
- The separate household-preparation section was removed so that the naming explanation leads straight into the question behind the story: why does Chinese use the expression `过年`?
- The Nian story is divided into three readable scenes. A repetitive passage in which the villagers perform the same actions again the following year was removed.
- Nian remains clearly labeled as a folk legend, not the proven historical origin of Chinese New Year customs.

## Names and the traditional Chinese calendar

Source: University of California, Berkeley, “Chinese New Year”
URL: https://multiverse.ssl.berkeley.edu/Calendar-in-the-Sky/Articles/chinese-new-year
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- *Spring Festival*, *Chinese New Year*, and *Lunar New Year* are all used in English.
- *Chinese New Year* identifies the Chinese celebration directly.
- *Lunar New Year* can be a broader name for new-year celebrations in several Asian cultures whose traditions are not identical.
- The traditional Chinese calendar uses lunar months while keeping the calendar connected to the solar seasons.

Editorial boundary:

- The names overlap but are not perfectly interchangeable in every context.
- The English title keeps the Chinese phrase *Guo Nian* and identifies the Nian story directly. The opening explains the phrase in plain English.
- The common Chinese expression `农历新年` is mentioned, but the calendar is not described as using only the moon.

## Why the holiday is also called Spring Festival

Source: Nanjing University Institute for Advanced Studies in Social Sciences, “纪念我国农历春节100年”
URL: https://jsas.nju.edu.cn/0a/17/c21381a330263/pagem.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Source: Shenzhen Municipal Archives, “一锤定音！汉代如何奠定我们两千多年的春节格局”
URL: https://www.szdag.gov.cn/gzdt/gzdt/content/post_1656812.html
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The traditional-calendar New Year had older names including 元旦, 元日, and 岁首.
- After January 1 became New Year's Day in the Gregorian calendar, “Spring Festival” was approved in 1914 as the distinct modern name for the traditional-calendar New Year.

Editorial boundary:

- The 1914 date concerns the modern official name. It does not mean that Chinese New Year celebrations began in 1914.
- The child-facing page explains the naming relationship without adding a long administrative history.

## Nian and the colors and sounds of New Year

Source: Shanghai Municipal People's Government, “Chinese New Year's Day”
URL: https://english.shanghai.gov.cn/en-KeyDatesAndCustoms/20260122/0f8629ce52fc4a1d824e0421b9b4b392.html
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Source: Embassy of the People's Republic of China in Nepal, “Guo Nian — Celebrating the Lunar New Year”
URL: https://np.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/Culture/Feature/200410/t20041027_1997783.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Source: China.org.cn / Shanghai Daily, “A Spring Festival myth lives on”
URL: https://www.china.org.cn/english/LivinginChina/Spring-festival-2008/241859.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- A widely told folk legend says Nian feared red, bright fire, and loud noise.
- One common version has an old man stay behind, decorate a house in red, light it brightly, and burn bamboo to frighten Nian away.
- Red decorations, firecrackers, and the Nian story are closely connected in popular explanations of the holiday.
- Before modern firecrackers, burning bamboo supplied the cracking sounds described in common accounts.

Editorial boundary:

- The article always frames these events as a folk tale. It does not claim that historians have proven Nian created the customs.
- Firecrackers appear as part of a legend and tradition, not as an instruction for children.
