# Mid-Autumn Festival — References and Editorial Notes

This file supports fact-checking and does not render on the website.

## Current child-facing draft (2026-09-04)

- The article now follows one story-shaped path: a brief Mid-Autumn introduction, the shadows on the moon, Wu Gang and the self-healing osmanthus tree, the Jade Rabbit pounding medicine, and the gradual weaving together of several moon legends.
- Most of the bilingual story was supplied by the editor. Short connective passages introduce the festival and carry the reader from reunion and mooncakes into the moon legends.
- The previous sections on mooncake history and regional styles, wooden molds, osmanthus, and Su Shi were removed so that they do not interrupt the new story.
- Legends are identified as legends. The article does not claim that the figures are physically visible on the moon or that all of their stories began as one narrative.

## Wu Gang and the self-healing osmanthus tree

Source: Duan Chengshi, *Youyang Zazu* (《酉阳杂俎》), “Tianzhi” (《天咫》), via Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=565446&if=en&remap=gb
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Wu Gang was said to have committed an offense while learning the ways of the immortals and was punished by being ordered to cut a tree on the moon.
- The phrase “树创随合” says that the wound in the tree closes after it is cut.

Editorial boundary:

- This is a Tang-dynasty record of an old legend, not verified history or a description of the physical moon.
- The text says that Wu Gang committed an offense but does not provide a detailed dramatic backstory. The child-facing version therefore does not invent one.
- The ancient text calls the legendary tree 桂 and does not identify it with a precise modern botanical species. English translations vary, using *cassia*, *laurel*, or *osmanthus*. The child-facing version uses “osmanthus tree,” the clearest modern cultural rendering; “cassia tree” could misleadingly suggest Chinese cinnamon.

Source: Hong Kong Heritage Museum, *Cups of Floral Delights*, “Eighth Month — Osmanthus”
URL: https://hk.heritage.museum/documents/Exhibitions/Permanent/chineseart/cups_of_floral_delights_booklet.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- This museum booklet identifies the tree in the Wu Gang legend as an osmanthus tree (*Osmanthus fragrans*).
- It says that Wu Gang was condemned to cut the tree, which immediately heals and grows again.

## Chang'e, the Jade Rabbit, and the lunar tree in Tang art

Source: Palace Museum, “Bronze Mirror with Chang'e and the Jade Rabbit” (嫦娥玉兔菱花镜)
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/bronze/233188.html
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- This Tang-dynasty mirror shows Chang'e, a 桂 tree, a rabbit holding a pestle and pounding medicine, and a toad in a moon-palace scene.
- The Palace Museum notes early literary traditions of a rabbit on the moon pounding medicine and of a 桂 tree on the moon.

Editorial boundary:

- The mirror shows that these familiar moon images appeared together in Tang art. It does not prove that their separate legends began together or that the rabbit's medicine was originally made especially for Chang'e.
- The earlier `chang-e-story.webp` illustration is no longer used in the article because it made Chang'e the visual focus of a section about the Jade Rabbit.

## The early medicine-pounding rabbit

Source: Xinhua, “如果文物会说话｜这个中秋和文物‘皮’一下！听它们讲传奇往事”
URL: https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2023-09/28/c_1212278162.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The *Dongtao Xing* (《董逃行》) tradition describes a white rabbit pounding a magical medicine and offering it to the emperor.
- The medicine is associated with becoming an immortal or gaining extraordinary longevity.

Editorial boundary:

- The early passage does not say that the rabbit was making medicine especially for Chang'e. The article preserves that distinction.
- The date segment in the editor-supplied URL was corrected from `2023-09-29` to the working direct page dated `2023-09-28`; the article identifier is unchanged.

## Moon legends associated with Mid-Autumn Festival

Source: China Intangible Cultural Heritage Network, “承中秋韵 话节日情——中秋节的源流与文化意蕴”
URL: https://www.ihchina.cn/news_1_details/10423.html
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Chang'e Flying to the Moon, the Jade Rabbit Pounding Medicine, and Wu Gang Cutting the Gui Tree are major legends associated with the moon and Mid-Autumn Festival.
- The source describes these traditions as stories with variants that developed over time.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article may present the figures as familiar parts of an imagined moon world, but it must not collapse all variants into one original story.
