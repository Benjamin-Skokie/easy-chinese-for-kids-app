# Chinese Zodiac — References and Editorial Notes

This file supports fact-checking and does not render on the website.

## Current Child-Facing Draft (2026-09-03, revised)

- The opening now explains the Chinese zodiac before asking why the rat is first.
- It now explains why the animal cycle was useful: animals gave the older twelve-branch time cycle vivid, memorable names.
- A child-sized two-wheel explanation shows how ten Heavenly Stems and twelve Earthly Branches form the sixty-year *jiǎzǐ* cycle.
- The article distinguishes the Chinese animal-year system from the Western sky-sign system and explicitly rejects one-to-one matches such as Rat–Aries.
- The Heavenly Court and Jade Emperor are introduced as parts of Chinese mythology. The animals are explicitly described as visitors invited from Earth, not as animals that all live in Heaven.
- The race is told as a lively legend, including the popular cat-and-rat variation, then clearly separated from the zodiac's documented history.
- The reference notes distinguish early Qin-period animal lists, which were not fully identical to today's zodiac, from the later Eastern Han record of the familiar twelve animals; those extra dates are not forced into the child-facing story.
- Personality predictions and fortune-telling claims are omitted.

## Why twelve animals, why sixty years, and why English says “zodiac”

Supports:

- The twelve zodiac animals are paired with the twelve Earthly Branches, while the twelve branches and ten Heavenly Stems combine in sequence to form a sixty-year cycle.
- The first stem-branch pair is 甲子 (*Jiǎzǐ*), and the sixty-pair cycle is commonly called a *jiǎzǐ*.
- The Chinese zodiac gives one animal to each year in a repeating twelve-year cycle.
- The Western zodiac divides the Sun's apparent path across the sky into twelve signs. It is commonly associated with a person's month and day of birth rather than a twelve-year animal cycle.

Editorial boundary:

- The exact origin of the twelve-part branch system and the original reason for pairing each animal remain debated. The child-facing text explains what the system did and why animal labels are memorable without claiming that one known person invented it for one proven purpose.
- “Chinese zodiac” is an English comparison label. The article does not claim that the Chinese and Western systems share one origin or that their twelve signs correspond one-to-one.

Supports:

- In a popular legend, the Jade Emperor calls a race, and the rat rides on the ox and jumps ahead near the finish.
- A familiar variation adds the cat, which misses its place after the rat leaves it behind.
- The Great Race is one of several stories told to explain the animal order.
- Qin-period bamboo texts contain early zodiac-like animal systems more than two thousand years ago, but their animals were not always identical to today's list.
- Wang Chong's Eastern Han text *Lunheng*, written around two thousand years ago, records the familiar set of twelve animals used today.

Editorial boundary:

- The Jade Emperor's race is not presented as historical fact.
- Details about the other animals vary among retellings. The draft uses a common child-facing version and signals variation where needed.
