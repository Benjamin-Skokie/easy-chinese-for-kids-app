# The Abacus: A Clackety Calculator

## One Wooden Frame, A Few Rows of Beads, and More Than a Thousand Years of Math

Before electronic calculators, what did people in China use to do math?

The abacus.

It was a rectangular wooden frame with a beam across the middle. On each rod, there were beads above the beam and beads below it. One upper bead counted as five lower beads.

Click-clack, click-clack.

That sound was once the music of Chinese accounting rooms.

When a skilled person used an abacus, the beads could fly under the fingers. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division could happen faster than you might press buttons on a calculator. You have to tap one key at a time. An abacus master could move several fingers at once.

Sometimes the person barely looked at the abacus. The eyes glanced at the account book. The fingers moved. In a rush of little wooden sounds, the answer was already there.

## An Abacus in a Song Dynasty Painting

When was the abacus invented?

That is hard to say exactly.

But a famous Song dynasty painting gives us a very clear clue. In Zhang Zeduan's Along the River During the Qingming Festival, if you look at the counter of Zhao Taicheng's medicine shop, you can see an abacus sitting there.

This is one of the earliest picture records of an abacus in the world. It tells us that by the Northern Song dynasty, people were already using abacuses in everyday business.

In the Ming dynasty, a mathematician named Cheng Dawei wrote a famous abacus textbook called General Source of Computational Methods. Many people used it to learn bead calculation.

The book was full of easy-to-chant rules:

"One up one, two up two..."

Young students chanted the rules while moving the beads, almost as if they were singing. The book was printed again and again. It helped spread abacus learning into accounting rooms and schools all over China.

## The Abacus Goes Overseas

The abacus traveled to Japan, too.

Japanese users changed it into a simpler form, with one bead above and four beads below. For a long time, Japanese elementary schools taught abacus calculation. Even today, many children still study it in special classes.

There are also abacus competitions. Dozens of players move their beads at the same time. The click-clack sound keeps going from beginning to end, like a tiny drum concert.

The abacus also traveled west along trade routes toward Russia.

The Russian abacus looks different. Its rods are horizontal, with ten beads on each rod and no middle beam. People push the beads left and right with their fingers.

It does not look much like the Chinese abacus, but the basic idea is still the same: let your hands help your mind count.

## The Shine on an Old Abacus

After a wooden abacus has been used for decades, the bead holes and wooden rods become wonderfully smooth.

You do not have to push hard. The beads seem to slide into place by themselves.

Old accountants sometimes said an abacus had become "well seasoned," meaning it had been used so much that it felt easy in the hand.

An abacus used for 20 or 30 years can look beautiful just sitting on a table. The dark wooden frame may shine from years of hands touching it. The beads may be worn smooth from thousands and thousands of calculations.

Move one bead, and it still makes that clear little sound.

For a second, it feels as if an accountant from a hundred years ago has just finished a bill, gone next door for noodles, and might come back at any moment.

---

## Summary

The abacus is only a wooden frame with rows of beads, but it helped people calculate for more than a thousand years. From a Song dynasty medicine shop, to Ming dynasty chanting rules, to fast Japanese abacus contests, it may be one of the most elegant and long-lasting calculating tools humans ever made.

The most charming thing about a good abacus is more than speed. It is the rhythm between person and beads. When the fingers know the answer, the beads seem to arrive there first.
