# How Did Paper Come from Tree Bark?

## The Age of Writing on Bamboo: One Book, One Ox Cart

Before paper was invented, people in China wrote on bamboo slips.

They split bamboo into long, thin strips. Then they tied the strips together with string. That was a book.

One copy of the Analects might take dozens of bamboo slips and weigh several pounds. The Records of the Grand Historian had more than 500,000 Chinese characters. If it had all been written on bamboo, the slips would have been so heavy that an ox cart would be needed to pull them.

Old books say that Qin Shihuang read official papers by weight. They were not counted by small piles. They were weighed by the *shi*, a unit of about 120 pounds. One day, a *shi* of documents. The next day, another *shi*.

Some wealthy people also wrote on silk. Silk was light and smooth, but it was far too expensive. One roll of silk could make clothes for a family for a season.

So bamboo was heavy and hard on the hands, but it was still the cheaper choice.

In those days, a scholar going out did not carry a backpack of books. He might carry a bundle of bamboo slips.

Looking up information was not just brain work.

It was body work.

## Baqiao Paper: Early Tries in the Western Han

Strictly speaking, Cai Lun did not create paper out of nothing.

Archaeologists found pieces of hemp-fiber paper at Baqiao in Xi'an. They came from the Western Han dynasty, about 200 years before Cai Lun.

But this was not yet good writing paper.

The fibers were too rough. When ink touched it, the ink spread out and blurred. It was almost impossible to write on neatly.

Baqiao paper was more like an accidental discovery. Craftspeople washing hemp fibers may have noticed that, after drying, the fibers could stick together into a thin layer. Maybe someone tried making a few marks on it, saw that it did not work well, and set it aside.

The real breakthrough still needed one key person.

## Cai Lun: Finding a Civilization in Waste

In the Eastern Han dynasty, one person inside the palace kept thinking about this problem.

His name was Cai Lun. He was in charge of making useful objects for the imperial court.

After watching earlier hemp-fiber paper and learning from craftspeople in different places, Cai Lun made an important choice:

Do not use only hemp. Change the materials.

He mixed tree bark, hemp ends, old rags, and worn-out fishing nets. These were all things people might throw away.

He crushed them into pulp, spread the pulp on a bamboo screen, pressed it flat, and dried it.

The sheet peeled from the screen was thin, smooth, and useful. Ink did not spread everywhere. Building on earlier attempts, Cai Lun helped make paper that was much more ready for real writing.

In the year 105 CE, Cai Lun presented his paper to Emperor He of Han.

The emperor tried it. The ink stayed clear. The paper was much lighter than bamboo slips and much cheaper than silk. Emperor He was pleased, and the method spread more quickly.

After that, paper slowly became the main writing material for Chinese scholars. From the Eastern Han to the Sui and Tang dynasties, bamboo slips gradually stepped off the stage of history.

## Paper-Making Travels West

In the Tang dynasty, the Tang army fought an Arab army at the Battle of Talas.

The Tang army lost, and some people traveling with the army were captured. Later stories and records often connect this battle with the spread of paper-making to the west.

The Arab world did develop paper workshops in places such as Samarkand. From there, paper-making moved step by step into the Islamic world.

Samarkand paper soon became popular in the Middle East.

Scholars in Baghdad found that copying books on paper was much easier than copying them on parchment made from animal skin. A book copied on parchment could be thick and expensive. A book copied on paper could be cheap enough for many students to buy.

Arab papermakers improved the process. They polished the paper more and added a thin glue-like coating, which helped make the paper whiter and smoother.

Then paper-making traveled with Arab traders to North Africa. Later, it crossed the Strait of Gibraltar into Europe.

Before this, many Europeans wrote books on parchment, which cost a lot. Ordinary people had little chance to see many books.

After paper arrived, things slowly began to change. Later printing became easier to spread, too. Books had a better chance to move from the hands of a few people into the hands of many more.

Paper-making was not the only reason knowledge spread farther.

But it gave knowledge a very important material to travel on.

## The Life of a Sheet of Paper

Paper seems fragile.

It fears fire, water, and hungry insects.

But if it is protected well, it can live much longer than you might think.

In the Dunhuang Library Cave, tens of thousands of paper scrolls were preserved. Some are about 1,600 years old. In the dry desert climate of northwest China, the paper survived safely.

Pieces of Han dynasty hemp paper found in Xinjiang still show visible color.

Today's paper is still related to the bark paper Cai Lun held in his hands. The materials have changed. We often use wood pulp now. The process uses machines and factory lines.

But the basic idea is still the same:

break plant fibers apart,
turn them into pulp,
spread them flat,
press them dry.

The idea from nearly 2,000 years ago still lives inside the textbooks you use today.

A plain white sheet of paper looks simple. But humans spent a very long time finding such a good surface for writing.

---

## Summary

More than 1,900 years ago, Cai Lun improved paper-making with tree bark, hemp ends, old rags, and worn-out fishing nets. Later, paper-making traveled west and helped replace bamboo slips, parchment, and other older writing materials.

Without this thin sheet of paper, books would have been harder to make light and cheap. Knowledge would not have traveled so far.
