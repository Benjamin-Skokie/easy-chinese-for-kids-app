# Editorial References: Water Margin

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Accessed: 2026-09-01

## 1. Setting, Mount Liang, and the shape of the story

Source: Library of Congress, “The Story of Water Margin”
URL: https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2017/07/the-story-of-water-margin-%E6%B0%B4%E6%BB%B8%E5%82%B3/

Supports:

- The story's early-twelfth-century Northern Song setting, the gathering of people from many walks of life, the novel's repeated picture of corrupt officials, and Song Jiang's role as leader.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says clearly that this is the world of the novel, not a claim that every official or every person in the Northern Song lived this way.
- “Mount Liang” is explained as a defended stronghold beside a broad marsh. The alternate English title *Outlaws of the Marsh* helps explain the landscape.

## 5. Life at the stronghold and Song Jiang's plan

Source: Wikisource, *Water Margin* (100-chapter edition), Chapter 21
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%B0%B4%E6%BB%B8%E5%82%B3_%28100%E5%9B%9E%E6%9C%AC%29/%E7%AC%AC021%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms that Yan Poxi discovers the letter connecting Song Jiang to the Mount Liang group, threatens to bring it to court, and is killed by Song Jiang during the confrontation.

Source: Wikisource, *Water Margin* (120-chapter edition), Chapter 71
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%B0%B4%E6%BB%B8%E5%82%B3_%28120%E5%9B%9E%E6%9C%AC%29/%E7%AC%AC071%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the gathering and ranking of 108 leaders, the stronghold's gates, land camps, water camps, storehouses, scouting roles, and Song Jiang's wish for a court pardon.
- Also supports the raids on officials and rich households described by the novel as harming ordinary people.

Editorial boundary:

- The article explains the two parts of *haohan* and uses “brave hero” as the child-facing English sense, while making clear that the Mount Liang characters are not perfect superheroes.
- The child-facing article keeps the chain of events clear—the discovered letter, the threat to report Song Jiang, the quarrel, and his flight after a serious crime—but deliberately leaves the killing itself off the page. The source note preserves the exact event for editorial checking.
- It gives concrete daily work inside the stronghold instead of treating Liangshan as one long gathering or feast.

## 6. Amnesty, campaigns, and the poisoned wine

Source: Wikisource, *Water Margin* (120-chapter edition), Chapter 82
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%B0%B4%E6%BB%B8%E5%82%B3_%28120%E5%9B%9E%E6%9C%AC%29/%E7%AC%AC082%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the court's pardon, the formal acceptance by the Mount Liang group, and their new service to the state.

Source: Wikisource, *Water Margin* (100-chapter edition), Chapter 100
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%B0%B4%E6%BB%B8%E5%82%B3_%28100%E5%9B%9E%E6%9C%AC%29/%E7%AC%AC100%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the losses during the campaigns, Song Jiang's appointment to govern Chuzhou and oversee its soldiers, and the poisoned wine sent to him in the emperor's name by hostile officials.
- Confirms the emperor's final dream of the Mount Liang gathering and his order to build a temple in memory of the dead leaders.

Editorial boundary:

- The article follows the full ending shared by the 100- and 120-chapter traditions. Some shortened editions end before the pardon and later campaigns.
- *Zhao'an* is explained as both forgiveness for past crimes and recruitment into government service, rather than translated only as “pardon.”

## Image note

The three pictures are modern children's-book illustrations. They are not exact reconstructions of Northern Song clothing, the hay depot, the Mount Liang stronghold, or Song Jiang's final cup.
