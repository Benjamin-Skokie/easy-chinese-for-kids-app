# Editorial References: Shan Hai Jing

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- The book's long compilation history, its route-like mountain descriptions, the creatures of the Southern Mountains, the Jingwei story, and the editorial choice to present the material through concrete encounters rather than abstract praise.

## 1. When and how the book took shape

Source: Cambridge University Press, *Time out of Joint*, chapter excerpt
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/64EA64CE425951C77D90340A7F5AE534/9781108425728c1_20-74.pdf/time-out-of-joint.pdf

- Describes *Shan Hai Jing* as a late Warring States or Han compilation, roughly from the third to first centuries BCE.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article says only that different people gathered the material over a long time more than two thousand years ago. It does not turn the opening into a debate over exact dating or authorship.
- “Compilation” is shown through the image of people adding mountains, ceremonies, tales, and reports rather than left as an unexplained academic word.

## 2. The route through the Southern Mountains

Source: Wikisource, *Shan Hai Jing*, “Classic of the Southern Mountains”
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%B1%B1%E6%B5%B7%E7%B6%93/%E5%8D%97%E5%B1%B1%E7%B6%93

- Confirms the repeated route form in which the text travels from one named mountain to the next and records minerals, plants, rivers, and creatures.
- Confirms the *lushu*: horse-like body, white head, tiger-like markings, red tail, and a call like singing.
- Confirms the *xuangui*: turtle-like body, bird head, snake tail, and a call like splitting wood.
- Confirms the Qingqiu nine-tailed fox and its cry like a human baby.

Editorial boundary:

- The article keeps the original creatures' visual and sound details but omits the passages about eating people, eating animal flesh as medicine, and wearing animal parts. Those details would pull this short children's journey toward violence and away from the creature encounters the page is trying to stage.
- Exact *li* distances are replaced by simple travel language because the young reader needs the direction and sequence, not an ancient unit conversion.

## 3. Jingwei carries stones to the sea

Source: Wikisource, *Shan Hai Jing*, “Classic of the Northern Mountains”
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%B1%B1%E6%B5%B7%E7%B6%93/%E5%8C%97%E5%B1%B1%E7%B6%93

- Confirms Fajiu Mountain, the bird's patterned head, white beak and red feet, the Flame Emperor's daughter who drowns in the Eastern Sea, and Jingwei's repeated carrying of wood and stones from the western mountains to fill the sea.

Editorial boundary:

- The sea does not speak or laugh in the primary passage, so the rewritten story removes the invented dialogue found in the earlier draft.
- The article now states Jingwei's goal before showing her return for another stone. The section ends with her repeated journey still unfinished, following the source's continuing-action description without claiming that the sea was ever filled.

## 4. The child drawing a new creature

The fourth section is openly presented as a modern boy's imaginative response to an illustrated book, not as a claim that the dragon-like creature in his sketchbook appears in the ancient text. Its horns, scales, four clawed legs, and curling tail describe the existing illustration.

Editorial boundary:

- This section demonstrates the creature-building pattern after readers have already met three source-based examples. It does not tell children that the book “inspires imagination” and expect them to accept the claim without experiencing it.
