### Yinglong / 应龙

Sources:

- *Shan Hai Jing*, “Classic of the Great Wilderness: North”: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/山海經/大荒北經
- *Shan Hai Jing*, “Classic of the Great Wilderness: East”: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/山海經/大荒東經
- *Shan Hai Jing*, Sibu Congkan edition, volume 14, Guo Pu's commentary: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/山海經_(四部叢刊本)/卷第十四

The primary text describes Yinglong gathering water during the Yellow Emperor's conflict with Chiyou, the wind and rain spirits' storm, and Nüba stopping the rain. It also connects Yinglong's later home in the south with the south's rainy weather. The eastern passage describes drought after the dragon cannot return above and people making its likeness to obtain rain. The reading keeps these as events and explanations within the myth, without graphic battle details or claims about real weather.

The wings have a different textual status: Guo Pu's ancient commentary explains Yinglong as “龙有翼者也.” The primary passages do not themselves specify wing anatomy or body color. The card naturally identifies the scholar's contribution; the illustration's colors, wing construction, pose, and detailed dragon anatomy are artistic choices. Its waterside placement expresses its connection with water, not a precise source location.

### Southern Mountains: three distinct journeys

Source: *Shan Hai Jing*, “Classic of the Southern Mountains”
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hans/山海經/南山經

- **First southern journey:** Lushu lives on Niuyang Mountain; Xuangui lives in the Guai River flowing from that mountain. The river flows **east into the Xianyi waters**. They are drawn within one mountain-and-river scene, not on unrelated peaks. Qingqiu Mountain lies farther **east** along the same itinerary, with Di, Danyuan, and Ji mountains between it and Niuyang. Those intermediate stops are omitted rather than represented as nonexistent. Qingqiu's Ying River flows **south into Jiyi Marsh**, a different water system from the Guai River.
- **Second southern journey:** Changyou Mountain appears before Luwu Mountain on a route continuing east through many intervening mountains. Changyou's home is explicitly **without plants and rich in water**. Luwu's upper slopes are also **without plants**, with metal and stone. Gudiao belongs in its waters: the Zegeng River flows **south into the Pang River**. The Pang River is recorded earlier in this itinerary as flowing **east to the sea**. The two creature scenes preserve the west-to-east order without claiming that Changyou and Luwu are adjacent mountains.
- **Third southern journey:** Fenghuang's Danxue Mountain is on a separate itinerary. The Dan River flows **south to the sea named Bohai in the passage**. The short child-facing label says “sea”; it does not transplant the modern northern Chinese Bohai onto this southern route. Danxue is not drawn as the next stop after Luwu.
- Blank gaps separate these three journeys. Their page rows and spacing do not assert a north-south relationship between the three itineraries.
