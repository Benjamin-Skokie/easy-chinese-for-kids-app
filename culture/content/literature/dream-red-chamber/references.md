# Editorial References: Dream of the Red Chamber

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

Accessed: 2026-09-01

Supports:

- The historical setting and household structure, the main character relationships, Grand View Garden, the flower burial, the arranged marriage, the Jia family's fall, and Baoyu's departure.

## 1. Qing background, the noble household, and the stone

Source: National Palace Museum, “The Splendor of Dream of the Red Chamber”
URL: https://www.npm.gov.tw/Publications-Content.aspx?l=2&sno=04013689

- Places Cao Xueqin in the High Qing period and describes the novel as a remembrance of a wealthy, prestigious family at its height and during its decline.
- Connects the Jia family's material world with the generational wealth and court-connected household in which Cao grew up.

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Cultivated Landscapes”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2002/cultivated-landscapes

- Describes Grand View Garden as an image of the prosperity and material splendor of eighteenth-century Qing society.

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 1
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC001%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the stone left over from Nüwa's repair of the sky, its wish to enter the human world, and the novel's deliberate refusal to name an exact dynasty and date.
- Describes the Crimson Pearl Plant, the young immortal who keeps it alive by watering it with dew, and its promise to repay that kindness with a lifetime of tears when both enter the human world.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article says that Cao wrote in the Qing dynasty while carefully noting that the fictional story itself does not name a dynasty.
- The Nüwa tooltip gives the short myth orientation a new reader needs; the paragraph then explains her role in this particular story.

## 2. Daiyu's arrival and the three cousins

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 3
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC003%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms Daiyu's arrival at her maternal grandmother's home after her mother's death, the large Jia household, Baoyu's upbringing among the girls, and Baoyu and Daiyu's immediate sense of recognition.
- Both children feel that the other looks familiar. Baoyu says that he has seen this “sister” before and describes their meeting as a reunion after a long separation.

Source: University of Minnesota Libraries, “Cousins in the Garden”
URL: https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/cousins-in-the-garden/

- Describes Baoyu, Daiyu, and Baochai as three young cousins living near one another in Grand View Garden and explains why either young woman could be considered a possible bride for Baoyu.

Source: University of Minnesota Libraries, “Jia Baoyu,” “Lin Daiyu,” and “Xue Baochai”
URLs:
- https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/jia-baoyu/
- https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/lin-daiyu/
- https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/xue-baochai/

- Supports the character relationships, Daiyu's sensitivity and poetic gift, Baochai's steadiness, and the unhappy jade-and-gold marriage.
- The Baoyu entry connects his jade to the stone and discusses his earlier bond with Daiyu's plant incarnation. The Baochai entry explains that the words on her gold locket match those on Baoyu's jade, forming the proposed “gold-and-jade” marriage.

Editorial boundary:

- “Cousin” is used without burdening young readers with a complete Jia-Wang-Xue-Lin family tree. Daiyu and Baochai are introduced through their separate arrivals at the Jia household rather than through a full genealogy.
- The short name explanation is linguistic rather than a claim that the three names encode the whole plot: *Baoyu* means “precious jade”; *Daiyu* shares *yu* (“jade”); *Baochai* shares *bao* (“precious”), while *chai* is a hair ornament. The plant-and-immortal story and the jade-and-gold match are separately supported above.

## 3. Why Grand View Garden was built

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 16
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC016%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the emperor's new order allowing a ranked palace consort to request a visit to her parents when her family had a large, separate residence where the royal party could stay and security guards could be posted.
- Establishes that meeting these conditions allowed the family to submit a request; it did not give a consort automatic permission to travel home whenever she wished.

Source: National Palace Museum, “The Stately Elegance—The Splendor of Dream of the Red Chamber”
URL: https://theme.npm.edu.tw/NPMdreamredchamber/en/page-2

- Confirms that the Jia family builds Grand View Garden to receive the homecoming imperial consort Jia Yuanchun.

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 18
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC018%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms Yuanchun's brief visit, the extensive preparations and objects placed in the garden, her naming of its buildings, and her return to the palace.

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 23
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC023%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms Yuanchun's request that Baoyu, Baochai, Daiyu, and the other young women move into the garden rather than leave it locked and empty.

## 4. Daiyu buries the flowers

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 27
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC027%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms Daiyu's hurt after the closed-gate misunderstanding, the spring flower festival, her return to the flower mound, and the *Song of Burying Flowers*.
- The poem explicitly links fading flowers with Daiyu's fear of aging, death, and being left without anyone to mourn or bury her.

Source: University of Minnesota Libraries, “Lin Daiyu”
URL: https://open.lib.umn.edu/redchamber/chapter/lin-daiyu/

- Places the flower burial in Chapters 23 and 27 and connects it to Daiyu's character and larger story.

Editorial boundary:

- The article paraphrases the poem instead of giving young readers a long literal translation. It makes the emotional connection concrete: Daiyu is an orphan, often ill, uncertain of her place in the Jia home, and unsure of her future with Baoyu.

## 5. The family falls, the marriage is arranged, and Baoyu leaves

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 97
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC097%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the plan that lets the confused Baoyu believe he is marrying Daiyu, Baochai's wedding, and Daiyu's fatal decline after learning of the marriage.

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 105
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC105%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms the official search and confiscation of the Jia household.

Source: Wikisource, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, Chapter 120
URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%A8%93%E5%A4%A2/%E7%AC%AC120%E5%9B%9E

- Confirms Baoyu's final appearance in the snow, his bow to his father, and his departure with a monk and a Daoist.

Editorial boundary:

- The article follows the familiar 120-chapter story. Cao Xueqin's authorship of the last forty chapters is disputed; the first printed 120-chapter edition was prepared after his death. The child-facing story does not interrupt the narrative with this textual-history question, but the reference record states the boundary.
- Baochai is not presented as a villain or blamed for the marriage. The adults arrange it, and the result is painful for Baochai as well as for Baoyu and Daiyu.
- The reasons for the Jia decline are presented as a chain—unchecked spending, internal theft and misuse of power, loss of court protection, investigation, and confiscation—rather than as one unexplained collapse.
