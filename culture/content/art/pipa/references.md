# “四根弦，为什么像藏着一支乐队？”— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Name, routes, and changes in playing position

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, J. Kenneth Moore, “The Pipa”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/the-pipa
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The modern Chinese pipa is a four-stringed plucked lute connected with West and Central Asian prototypes that appeared in China by the Northern Wei period.
- Earlier forms were held horizontally, used twisted silk strings, and were played with a triangular plectrum; during and after the Tang period, fingernail plucking and a more upright position developed.
- The terms *pi* and *pa* were associated with forward and backward plectrum strokes.

Editorial boundary:

- The article describes long-term localization through migration, performance, trade, making, and teaching rather than a single foreign origin point or one inventor.
- “Pipa” did not always name exactly the same physical object, so early references are not automatically modern concert pipas.
- The Met essay is used for a broad development outline, not to claim that every region changed position and technique on the same date.

Source: Shanghai Conservatory of Music Journal, “琵琶的前世今生——琵琶来历与演变史论”
URL: https://xuebao.ccom.edu.cn/CN/Y2019/V0/I1/64
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The instrument’s development involved movement from horizontal to slanted and upright holding, a flatter back, added frets on the soundboard, wider range, and a change from plectrum/inner-fingernail methods toward fingernail-surface playing.

Editorial boundary:

- Changes are presented as a process with overlaps and regional variations, not a straight ladder in which every earlier form becomes obsolete at once.

Source: Smithsonian Freer and Sackler Galleries, “Master of the Chinese Pipa: Wu Man” program notes
URL: https://archive.asia.si.edu/podcasts/related/wuman/progNotes.asp
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Early terms related to forward and backward plucking could apply broadly to plucked instruments, while the bent-neck, pear-shaped lute arriving through Central Asian routes became central to later pipa development.
- Modern finger rolls use several fingers in rapid sequence to create an even, sustained tremolo.

Editorial boundary:

- The early broad use of the name and the later four-stringed instrument are not collapsed into one unchanged object.
- Technique descriptions explain what listeners hear but are not instructions for unsupervised handling.

## Dunhuang religious imagery and the backward-pipa pose

Source: International Dunhuang Programme / British Library, “Dunhuang’s Creative Culture”
URL: https://idp.bl.uk/discover/learning/dunhuang/dunhuang-articles/dunhuang-at-work/dunhuangs-creative-culture/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Buddhist institutions and art were central to Dunhuang, and cave imagery includes celestial orchestras and flying apsaras playing instruments.
- The Cave 112 image shows a central dancer in a backward-pipa pose flanked by six musicians, and Dunhuang also preserves historical music/dance notation manuscripts.

Editorial boundary:

- The mural is religious music-and-dance imagery, not a modern performance manual or proof that a complete difficult solo was routinely played behind the back.
- The story distinguishes visual evidence, manuscript notation, and living performance rather than converting the mural into an audio recording.

Source: Dunhuang Academy, “敦煌说丨盛唐古乐，惊艳千年”
URL: https://www.dha.ac.cn/info/1159/2723.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The well-known backward-pipa image appears in the music-and-dance scene on the south wall of Mogao Cave 112 and belongs to a larger ensemble scene.
- Dunhuang murals contain many different pipa images and playing positions.

Editorial boundary:

- The Academy’s vivid popular description of the pose as a high-level technique is not used to prove routine historical performance practice.
- The article allows a boundary between possible bodily movement, dance imagery, and practical complex pipa playing.

## Women musicians, court/popular exchange, and makers

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Female musician with lute,” late seventh century
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/44801
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A late seventh-century tomb figurine shows a woman playing a pipa horizontally with a triangular plectrum as part of a small ensemble.
- Female ensembles could connect elite and popular musical culture, and rank affected whether court musicians played seated or standing and how they were treated.

Editorial boundary:

- A tomb object documents an image and social idea, not the complete biography or sound of one real musician.
- Women musicians are presented as skilled workers and innovators, not decorative “exotic beauties.”

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Music and Art of China”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/zh/essays/music-and-art-of-china
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Migration from Central Asia shaped both court and popular music in sixth- and seventh-century China.
- Pipa-like lutes were transformed in China, and instruments moved together with performers, repertoires, and theory.

Editorial boundary:

- Localization is described as work by performers, patrons, and craftspeople over time, not as an instrument merely “finding its Chinese soul.”
- Court history is not allowed to erase popular and folk traditions for which records are often thinner.

## Bai Juyi and literary evidence

Source: Chinese Text Project, Bai Juyi, “琵琶行”
URL: https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?char=%E7%90%B5%E7%90%B6%E8%A1%8C&if=en&remap=gb
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The poem compares heavier strings to sudden rain, finer strings to private speech, and intermingled notes to large and small pearls falling on a jade plate.
- The poem constructs a parallel between a professional woman musician’s changing fortunes and the displaced poet.

Editorial boundary:

- Poetic similes are evidence of literary listening and reception, not acoustical measurements or a recording.
- Exact weather, moonlight, seating, gestures, faces, and dialogue are not added beyond the literary text.

Source: Taiwan Citation Index—Humanities and Social Sciences, Shimosada Masahiro, “试论白居易《琵琶引》——其作年与使之成立的四个文学系统,” *东华汉学* 20 (2014)
URL: https://tci.ncl.edu.tw/cgi-bin/gs32/gsweb.cgi?o=dnclresource&s=id%3D%22A15005143%22.&searchmode=basic&tcihsspage=tcisearch_opt1_search
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- One scholarly interpretation treats the poem as a fictional literary construction shaped by several literary traditions rather than a straightforward event report.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not declare all scholarship settled or the poem unrelated to Bai Juyi’s historical experience; it says scholars debate the construction of the preface and meeting.
- Literary value is not reduced because factual reconstruction is uncertain.

## Battle pieces, printed scores, and version transmission

Source: Taiwan Music Institute Open Museum, historical pipa score record
URL: https://tmi.openmuseum.tw/muse/digi_object/93d2fcdfed196bb08fbf49b8c83b4b00
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Traditional large pipa works could be grouped as literary/civil (*wenban*) and martial (*wuban*) pieces, but the surviving repertoire contains varied forms and regional transmissions.
- The Hua Qiuping collection, printed in the Jiaqing period (1819 in this catalog), was an early printed source for right-hand symbols and included *Ten Sides*, the piece now called *Ambush from Ten Sides*.
- Same-named pieces in different regional traditions can have different musical content.

Editorial boundary:

- Civil/martial labels are presented as useful traditional categories, not an absolute binary covering every work or every passage.
- A printed score documents a nineteenth-century version and transmission point, not the piece’s exact first composition or Han-era sound.

Source: Osaka University Institutional Knowledge Archive, “琵琶武曲〈十面埋伏〉〈霸王卸甲〉の版本間における変異性をめぐって”
URL: https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/48196/mra_037_053A.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- *Ambush from Ten Sides* and *The Warlord Removes His Armor* exist in multiple transmitted versions with meaningful differences.
- The Hua score is a major early printed witness, while later performance lineages altered and interpreted the works.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not turn section titles into a literal soundtrack of every event at Gaixia.
- Unknown first authorship and dating remain open; old subject matter does not date every note.

## Abing and recorded folk performance

Source: China National Academy of Arts Library / Art and Literature Museum, “世纪绝响——阿炳传世乐曲（共6首）”
URL: https://lib.zgysyjy.org.cn/ting_detail/598530.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The 1950 wire recordings preserve Abing’s three pipa performances: *Big Waves Washing the Sand*, *Dragon Boat*, and *Zhaojun Leaves the Frontier*.
- Archival transfer and restoration allowed the original wire recordings to remain available for study and listening.

Editorial boundary:

- Abing is included as a trained local folk musician and performer. Blindness is a disability he lived with, not the source of his musical ability.
- The three surviving recordings are not described as the only pipa pieces he ever knew.

## Twentieth-century strings, frets, nails, and ensembles

Source: Shanghai Conservatory of Music Museum, “中国现代乐器展厅”
URL: https://bowuguan.shcmusic.edu.cn/2010/0524/c546a7312/page.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- After the 1960s, common modern pipas had about twenty-three to twenty-five total raised stops, arranged for twelve-tone equal temperament.
- Silk strings were replaced by steel or nylon-wound steel strings, and the modern instrument serves solo, chamber, and ensemble roles.
- Civil and martial labels describe characteristic repertory and technique emphases.

Editorial boundary:

- Modern standardization is not called the final or universally superior form.
- The article uses “about six *xiang* and twenty-four *pin*” for a common modern form while acknowledging variation.

Source: Shanghai Conservatory of Music, “拾回蚕丝的声音” project report
URL: https://zgysyyw.shcmusic.edu.cn/2026/0121/c453a60075/page.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Twentieth-century change from thirteen to twenty-four frets and widespread replacement of silk by steel altered technique, pitch organization, and listening habits.
- Current researchers and performers are testing historic instruments and silk strings to study earlier sound systems and regional practice.

Editorial boundary:

- Revival work is presented as research and experiment, not proof that one material is more authentic or better for every repertory.
- Steel strings, artificial nails, and amplified/orchestral use are described as responses to modern performance needs with tradeoffs.

Source: Singapore National Youth Chinese Orchestra, “Pipa”
URL: https://snyco.com.sg/instruments/pipa/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Mid-twentieth-century concert adaptation included steel strings and artificial acrylic nails, expanding volume and tone-color possibilities in orchestral performance.

Editorial boundary:

- This supports modern professional practice, not a claim that every pipa player everywhere uses identical strings or nail material.

## Image and activity boundaries

- All three article images are retained and labeled as modern generated illustrations, not mural photographs, historical reconstructions, or documented performances.
- `1-pipa-dunhuang-player.webp` combines a near-modern upright pipa/player with an imagined mural-style background. It is explicitly not a copy of Mogao Cave 112 and cannot teach Tang playing position.
- `2-pipa-boat-night.webp` is an imagined scene inspired by *The Song of the Pipa Player*. It is not a portrait, a photograph, or evidence for an event in 816; its instrument is modernized.
- `3-pipa-fast-fingers.webp` is a stylized modern stage image; hand placement and instrument details cannot be used as technical instruction.
- The listening activity requires only an authorized recording and paper. Children are told not to handle another person’s pipa, force steel strings, imitate aggressive sweeps, remove frets, or experiment with attached artificial nails without a teacher.

## 2026 child-facing revision

- The main article now lets children hear several sounds from four strings, see the Dunhuang backward-pipa pose, listen to Bai Juyi's river-night poem, and imagine the battle in *Ambush from Ten Sides*.
- It does not treat the Dunhuang image as a technical playing guide, Bai Juyi's metaphors as a recording, or the modern battle piece as an unchanged ancient performance.
