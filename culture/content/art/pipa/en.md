# Pipa: Wood That Can Sing

## A "Mixed-Blood Princess" from Far Away

The pipa has an interesting past.

Today, many people think it is closely connected to plucked instruments that came from Central and Western Asia. After arriving in China, it changed for a long time until it became the pipa we know today.

Old books often explain the words *pi* and *pa* through the motions of plucking the strings.

This instrument was like a traveler from far away. After living in China for a long time, it slowly grew into its own shape.

By the Tang dynasty, the pipa was extremely popular.

You can see many pipas in Dunhuang murals. Flying figures hold them. Musicians hold them. They appear in banquets, dances, and music scenes.

The Tang pipa's shape and playing position were also changing. The neck, the body, and the way players held it slowly shifted. Many important features of today's pipa are tied to those changes.

## "The Thick Strings Clattered Like Sudden Rain"

The Tang poet Bai Juyi was sent away from the capital to Jiangzhou. He felt lonely and sad.

One autumn night, he was saying goodbye to a friend by the Xunyang River. Suddenly, he heard pipa music coming from the water.

He asked someone to invite the player over.

She was an older woman. She sat at the front of the boat, held the pipa, and began to play.

After hearing her, Bai Juyi wrote a long poem of more than 600 Chinese characters. It was called *The Song of the Pipa Player*, and it became famous for centuries.

He wrote:

"The thick strings clattered like sudden rain.
The thin strings whispered like private words."

The loud and soft sounds mixed together:

"Big pearls and little pearls falling onto a jade plate."

You can almost hear the clear *ding-ding* sound with your eyes closed.

That poem gave the pipa a powerful emotional world. It could be quick, sad, angry, and soft like a sigh.

Because of this poem, when many people think of the pipa, they do not first imagine sheet music.

They hear strings of falling sounds.

## Ambush from Ten Sides: A War on Four Strings

One famous pipa piece is called *Ambush from Ten Sides*.

It tells the story of the final battle between Chu and Han, when Han Xin surrounded Xiang Yu's army. Songs from Chu were heard on all sides, and the soldiers' hearts broke. Xiang Yu finally fought his way out toward the Wu River and died there.

This is a huge war story.

A movie might need thousands of horses and soldiers to show it.

But one pipa player can sit in front of you and tell the whole battle with four strings and ten fingers, without using a single word.

At the beginning, war drums beat and horns call. Armies line up.

You are hearing more than a pipa. You seem to hear horses crying, blades striking, and flags snapping in the wind.

In the middle, the music suddenly grows quiet. Far away, you hear the sad tune of Chu songs. That is the deadly moment called "songs of Chu on all sides."

At the final height of the music, Xiang Yu breaks out. Hooves race. The pipa player's fingers roll quickly over one string, like a lonely horse running through darkness with enemies everywhere.

Then one last sweep of the strings:

the blade falls,
the string breaks,
the man is gone.

The room is silent.

That is the magic of the pipa. It is an instrument, but it is also a storyteller.

## Playing the Pipa Behind the Back: Could It Really Be Done?

In Dunhuang murals, there is a famous flying figure who plays the pipa in a strange way.

She does not hold it in front of her. She carries it behind her back and plucks the strings backward.

The pose is astonishingly beautiful. But people still argue:

Was this a real playing method?

Or was it the painter's imagination?

Modern pipa players have tried it. They can make some notes, but playing a difficult melody that way is not easy.

So many people prefer to see it as artistic exaggeration. It shows the flying figure as light, magical, and free.

Whether or not it was common long ago, "playing the pipa backward" later became a vivid phrase. People use it to mean doing something from a new angle.

---

## Summary

The pipa came from outside China and slowly grew its own Chinese voice.

Bai Juyi's *Song of the Pipa Player* helped people remember its falling, whispering, stormy sounds. *Ambush from Ten Sides* lets listeners hear battle, horses, and danger on only four strings.

The pipa is like a piece of wood that can sing. It rests quietly in the arms, then suddenly opens a huge world.
