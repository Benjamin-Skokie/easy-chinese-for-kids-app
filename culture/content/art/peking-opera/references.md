# Editorial References: Peking Opera

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Beijing Municipal Government — history and formation of Peking opera

URLs:

- https://www.beijing.gov.cn/tsbj/jwwh/jingju/202007/t20200713_1947152.html
- https://english.beijing.gov.cn/beijinginfo/culture/202005/t20200511_1894129.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Sanqing troupe came to Beijing in 1790, with other Anhui troupes following.
- Han-opera performers later worked in Anhui troupes; *xipi* and *erhuang* were central to the developing form.
- Peking opera gradually absorbed elements of Kunqu, Qinqiang, Yiyang traditions, Beijing speech, and other local music and performance practices.
- The form became clearly established around the mid-nineteenth century rather than appearing complete in 1790.

Editorial boundary:

- 1790 is treated as a useful landmark, not a one-day invention story.
- “More than two hundred years” does not erase older source traditions or imply that contributing genres disappeared.

## 2. Huangshan Municipal Government and Hubei Intangible Cultural Heritage Network — Anhui and Han contributions

URLs:

- https://www.huangshan.gov.cn/zjhs/rwhz/hpxq/8294066.html
- https://wlt.hubei.gov.cn/hbsfwzwhycw/bhcc/fydt/whs/202005/t20200525_2295663.shtml

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Anhui troupes already carried multiple vocal systems and were known for absorbing other theatrical material.
- The meeting of Anhui and Han performers and the interaction of *xipi* and *erhuang* were extended processes.
- Kunqu, Qinqiang, regional folk music, and other forms also contributed to Peking opera's development.

Editorial boundary:

- No single region, troupe, performer, melody, or date is credited as the sole creator.
- The article does not turn a complex history of exchange into a contest over which present-day province “owns” Peking opera.

## 3. UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — “Peking opera”

URLs:

- https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/peking-opera-00418
- https://ich.unesco.org/en/Decisions/5.COM/6.7

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Peking opera combines singing, reciting, acting, and martial arts.
- Music sets pace and atmosphere, shapes characters, and guides stories; strings and winds are prominent in “civil” plays, while percussion is prominent in “martial” plays.
- Performance uses conventionalized and symbolic movements, while traditional sets and props are often minimal.
- Master-student teaching, observation, and imitation remain important modes of transmission.
- Peking opera was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List in 2010.

Editorial boundary:

- Inscription is not described as a prize proving superiority over other opera traditions.
- “Symbolic” does not mean arbitrary, and “minimal” does not mean that every stage always contains exactly one table and two chairs.

## 4. Beijing Municipal Bureau of Cultural Heritage — troupe organization and the orchestra

URL: https://wwj.beijing.gov.cn/bjww/resource/cms/article/362762/515628/2018052316192538218.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Peking opera ensemble was known as the *changmian* or *wen-wu chang* (“civil and martial ensemble”).
- Jinghu players and percussion leaders were essential artistic collaborators rather than background helpers.
- The lead percussionist coordinated the orchestra, stage pace, entrances, gestures, and combat sequences.
- Historical troupes also depended on costume, makeup, headgear, prop, stage, communication, and management workers.

Editorial boundary:

- A named star is not presented as producing a performance alone.
- Exact staffing and instrument balance could vary by troupe, period, play, and production.

## 5. National Centre for the Performing Arts — roles, face paint, and the four skills

URLs:

- https://www.chncpa.org/cgyl_278/yszl/wqzl/201704/t20170408_168389.shtml
- https://m.chncpa.org/zxdt/rdjjx/202008/t20200814_218509.shtml
- https://m.chncpa.org/zxdt/rdjjx/202007/t20200706_217100.shtml

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The principal role categories are sheng, dan, jing, and chou, each with subdivisions.
- Sheng roles are generally “plain-faced,” dan makeup varies by subtype, jing roles characteristically use elaborate painted faces, and chou roles often use a smaller white patch.
- Role categories incorporate age, identity, voice, costume, and performance specialization rather than moral value alone.
- Singing, reciting, acting, and combat are the four commonly named performance skills.

Editorial boundary:

- Sheng is not equated with “hero,” dan with “young woman,” jing with “villain,” or chou with “fool.”
- Not every character wears full face paint, and a role name does not guarantee that a character is good or bad.

## 6. UNESCO, NCPA, and opera instruction — music patterns, props, and symbolic action

URLs:

- https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/peking-opera-00418
- https://www.chncpa.org/zxdt_331/zxdtlm/mtsj_334/201705/t20170518_170819.shtml
- https://m.chncpa.org/zxdt/xxqg/202011/t20201130_223432.shtml

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Hands, eyes, torso, and feet follow trained choreography in symbolic performance.
- Costumes and small props can carry dramatic information when scenery is limited.
- Musical and percussion patterns coordinate stage action rather than merely decorating it.

Editorial boundary:

- The invisible-horse passage is a child-friendly reconstruction of how symbolic action works, not a transcript of one named play or a universal sequence for riding.
- “One table and two chairs” is explained as a flexible convention, not mandatory furniture in every production.

## 7. National Museum of China — “Peking Opera Maestro Mei Lanfang: His Art, His Life”

URLs:

- https://en.chnmuseum.cn/exhibition/exhibition_series/temporary_exhibitions/historical_and_cultural_exhibition/202201/t20220127_253498.html
- https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/202201mlf/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Mei Lanfang lived from 1894 to 1961, specialized in dan roles, and developed the influential Mei school.
- His work included changes to performance, costume, makeup, and repertoire.
- He toured Japan, the United States, the Soviet Union, and other countries and played an important role in international cultural exchange.

Editorial boundary:

- Mei is not called the sole reformer of Peking opera or the only performer responsible for its international circulation.
- His achievements do not erase other schools, collaborators, musicians, writers, teachers, and stage workers.

## 8. Beijing government and Hong Kong Polytechnic University — performers and gender history

URLs:

- https://english.beijing.gov.cn/beijinginfo/culture/202005/t20200511_1894129.html
- https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/10397/90602
- https://www.harvard-yenching.org/research/actresses-of-peking-opera/

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Women were restricted from public performance in Qing Beijing, contributing to an all-male stage and the development of male dan specialists.
- Female performers appeared increasingly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
- The 1912 change in the Qing prohibition allowed women to appear officially on Beijing stages, although later policies and social prejudice still shaped their work.
- Women performers became active professional makers of modern Peking opera rather than merely replacing men.

Editorial boundary:

- The history is not reduced to “men played women because women lacked talent.”
- Character gender, performer gender, cross-gender technique, government restriction, and professional opportunity are kept distinct.

## 9. Beijing cultural-history publications and modern opera schools — training changes

URLs:

- https://www.bjxch.gov.cn/file/20190731/1564552674708076492.pdf
- https://www.icm.gov.mo/rc/viewer/20010/906
- https://jw.beijing.gov.cn/bjzj/gdzyreport/zdreport/202103/P020210428407374692927.pdf
- https://jw.beijing.gov.cn/bjzj/gdzyreport/gdreport/202403/P020240411565956392339.pdf

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Older *keban* training could bind young students through strict contracts and hierarchies, long hours, performance duties, punishment, and limited general education.
- Twentieth-century opera schools increasingly added literacy and general subjects to performance training.
- Current professional schools combine specialized repertoire, movement, music, and makeup courses with broader educational systems and digital resources.

Editorial boundary:

- Not every historical teacher, troupe, or student's experience is assumed to have been identical.
- Discipline and difficult physical practice are distinguished from abuse; corporal punishment and denial of education are not romanticized as necessary traditions.
- The article states what modern training should protect without claiming that every current institution has identical safeguards.

## 10. Ministry of Education and National Centre for the Performing Arts — living transmission

URLs:

- https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/xxgk_jyta/jyta_twys/201812/t20181224_364666.html
- https://hudong.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xwfb/moe_2082/zl_2017n/ztzl_yscc/yscc_ccxx/201706/t20170619_307299.html
- https://en.chncpa.org/whatson/zdyc/202306/t20230613_252641.shtml
- https://english.beijing.gov.cn/consuminginbeijing/onedayinbeijing/museums/updatesofmuseums/202403/t20240319_3593979.html

Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Opera education appears in school curricula, clubs, workshops, performances, and digital resources.
- Contemporary performers use recordings, online lessons, short videos, and age-appropriate courses to reach new learners.
- Digital exhibitions and new presentation formats coexist with professional stage and master-student transmission.

Editorial boundary:

- New technology is not automatically called successful innovation.
- Subtitles, projections, small-theater work, and online education are examples of access and experiment, not replacements for live performance or proof that all productions use them.
