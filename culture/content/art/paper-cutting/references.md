## Chinese paper-cutting as a living and varied tradition

Source: UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, “Chinese paper-cut”
URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chinese-paper-cut-00219
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Chinese paper-cutting is practiced across China and among different ethnic groups.
- Motifs, techniques, regional styles, makers, and purposes vary.
- Paper-cuts may be used for home decoration, festivities, ceremonies, and other forms of social life.
- Artists devise their own motifs, and modern technologies are also used.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not present one regional style as the only authentic form of Chinese paper-cutting.
- “Predominantly female” in UNESCO's description does not mean that all paper-cut artists are women.

## Folded paper, scissors, repetition, and symmetry

Source: The British Museum, “papercut; 剪紙 (Chinese), Museum No. 1919,0101,0.230.1”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1919-0101-0-230-1
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The museum record describes historical paper flowers made from folded paper that was cut with scissors, unfolded, and pasted in layers.
- Folding a square sheet and cutting through the folded layers produced a symmetrical repeated design.

Editorial boundary:

- The object is a Tang-dynasty lotus flower rather than the butterfly used in the story's teaching example.
- The article explains the folding principle; it does not claim that every Chinese paper-cut is made by folding.

## Fish, abundance, and 年年有余

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Red lotus and fish”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/754013
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The Met explains the visual wordplay in which 鱼 (*yú*, fish) sounds like 余 (*yú*, abundance).
- Together with the lotus/year wordplay in that painting, the image conveys 年年有余, “abundance through the years.”

Editorial boundary:

- The Met object is a painting, not a paper-cut. It supports the fish/abundance wordplay used in many Chinese visual contexts.
- A fish image does not always have one fixed meaning; interpretation depends on the work and context.
