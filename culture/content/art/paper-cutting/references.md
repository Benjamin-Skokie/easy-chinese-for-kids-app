# Paper Cutting — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Living heritage, social groups, uses, and techniques

Source: UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, “Chinese paper-cut”
URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chinese-paper-cut-00219
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese paper-cut was inscribed on the Representative List in 2009.
- Traditions exist across China and among various ethnic groups.
- Rural women have played a central role in learning and transmitting the practice, often from childhood.
- Paper may be cut, carved with a chisel, left plain, colored, or worked with modern technologies.
- Motifs and uses vary by region, maker, and purpose, including home decoration, celebrations, and rituals.

Editorial boundary:

- “Predominantly female” does not mean women were or are the only makers.
- UNESCO inscription safeguards a changing practice and community knowledge; it does not certify one frozen style as the only authentic form.

## Paper before Cai Lun and the history of papermaking

Source: National Museum of China, “科技的力量 — 旱滩坡带字纸”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/202012kxyjs/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Plant-fiber paper made from reused hemp material existed in the Western Han period.
- In the Eastern Han, Cai Lun expanded available raw materials and improved papermaking technology.
- Archaeological paper survives only in fragments and requires material analysis.

Editorial boundary:

- Cai Lun should not be called the sole inventor of paper.
- Improvement of paper technology does not provide a date or named inventor for paper-cutting.

## Pre-paper openwork and earliest surviving paper-cuts

Source: China Intangible Cultural Heritage Network and Digital Museum, “中国入选联合国教科文组织非物质文化遗产名录项目之中国剪纸”
URL: https://www.ihchina.cn/news_1_details/22117.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Before paper, openwork designs were cut or carved from metal foil, leather, silk, and leaves.
- The earliest known surviving Chinese paper-cuts include five Northern Dynasties rosettes found near Turpan, with repeated folded designs.
- Later uses include decoration, stencils, ceramics, windows, rooms, and celebrations; professional paper-cut artists are documented by the Southern Song.

Editorial boundary:

- Pre-paper objects are related cutout traditions, not literally paper-cuts.
- “Earliest known surviving” is an archaeological statement, not proof of the craft's exact first invention date.
- The child-facing article avoids unsupported claims that paper-cutting appeared immediately after Cai Lun.

Source: China Intangible Cultural Heritage Network, Qiao Xiaoguang, “作为非物质文化遗产的剪纸艺术”
URL: https://www.ihchina.cn/luntan_details/18437.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Paper's fragility makes early physical examples difficult to preserve.
- Sixth-century fragments from Astana near Turpan can be reconstructed as geometric rosettes containing horse, monkey, and butterfly designs.
- The surviving examples already show a mature folded-rosette method, allowing scholars to infer that earlier development likely occurred.

Editorial boundary:

- The inference of earlier development remains an inference, not a dated object.
- Dry climate and burial context help explain survival but do not mean Turpan was the only early center of the craft.

## Scissors, knives, folded repeats, and color methods

Source: Pingliang Municipal Government, “崆峒剪纸”
URL: https://www.pingliang.gov.cn/zjpl/mycp/art/2022/art_cd580eb542b34240b73cf9e3314cf631.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Paper-cut traditions use both scissors and knife carving.
- Several sheets may be stacked or joined for repeated production, while knives are worked on a prepared soft surface.
- Local uses include doorway, room, lantern, celebration, gift, longevity, and embroidery-pattern papers.

Editorial boundary:

- This source describes Kongtong practice; its exact workstation and sequence are not universal.
- Knife work is described historically and artistically, not offered as an unsupervised children's activity.

Source: Huining County Government, “会宁剪纸的色彩（二）”
URL: https://www.huining.gov.cn/mlhn/hnyx/hnjz/art/2017/art_d8fcede85c504f38bc322b6cf41bd8b0.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- In one widespread color-lining method, colored papers are pasted behind a completed main cut.
- Dyed paper-cut methods can apply color across stacked pale sheets.

Editorial boundary:

- Color lining, separate-color work, and dyeing are different processes; the article gives one accessible example rather than collapsing them into a single method.

## Uses are not fixed by one name or one color

Source: Gansu Culture and Tourism, “非遗撷英：剪纸，指尖上的诗和远方”
URL: https://www.gswbj.gov.cn/a/2023/07/21/17875.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Local paper-cuts serve different purposes: interior decoration, festive display, embroidery patterns, local rituals, and temple hangings.
- Hollow-cut structures require makers to plan connected positive lines or separated negative shapes.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses these as examples of functional variety, not as a complete national classification.
- Red is common in celebrations, but color and meaning depend on event and place; some mourning or ritual papers use other colors.

## Motif language and contextual meanings

Source: China Intangible Cultural Heritage Network, Qiao Xiaoguang, “读懂剪纸的语言”
URL: https://www.ihchina.cn/Article/Index/detail?id=7481
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Some motifs use spoken puns, including fish for abundance and bats for good fortune.
- Fish-lotus, pomegranate, animal, plant, and composite motifs can carry themes related to fertility, continuity, prosperity, or local ritual life.
- Even shared themes and conventional forms are expressed differently by regions and individual makers.

Editorial boundary:

- Motifs do not form a universal one-image/one-meaning dictionary.
- Meaning must be interpreted with region, occasion, combination, maker, and audience; a fish or lotus may have additional or non-symbolic meanings.

## Regional tendencies are not absolute

Source: China Intangible Cultural Heritage Network, “略谈中国民间剪纸的主要流派”
URL: https://www.ihchina.cn/luntan_details/8074.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Scholars often summarize northern styles as bold and southern styles as delicate, but the source explicitly warns against a rigid north-south dividing line.
- Regional schools grow through local customs, cultural exchange, materials, and changing practice.
- Yuxian paper-cut combines knife-cut positive and negative forms with color.

Editorial boundary:

- “North” and “south” are orientation devices, not personality traits of people or rules for identifying an unsigned object.
- A work's line width alone cannot establish provenance.

Source: Hebei Provincial Department of Culture and Tourism, “蔚县剪纸”
URL: https://whly.hebei.gov.cn/c/2012-12-21/558129.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Yuxian practice developed specialized knives, stacked production, negative and positive cutting, and multicolor dyeing.
- Its detailed opera figures and vivid colors combine qualities often stereotyped as either northern or southern.
- Named and unnamed craftspeople both contributed to changes in tools and style.

Editorial boundary:

- Yuxian is a specific local tradition and not a stand-in for all northern paper-cutting.
- Named masters should not erase the many workshop, family, and community contributors around them.
