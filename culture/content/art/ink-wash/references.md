## Ink variation and “ink has five colors”

Source: Palace Museum, “‘墨分五色’”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240484.html
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- “墨分五色” is associated with Zhang Yanyuan’s phrase “运墨而五色具.”
- The “five colors” are not five literal pigments. The expression describes rich effects created through variations including ink concentration, dryness, and wetness.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing story explains the phrase as a way of noticing variety within black ink, not as one fixed list or calibrated five-step scale.

## Ink tones, washes, texture, and atmosphere

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Clearing Autumn Skies over Mountains and Valleys”
URL: https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm:fsg_F1916.538/
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The museum describes subtle gradations of ink in the foreground and softer texture strokes and layered ink washes in the background.
- These changes in brushwork and ink help create depth, mood, and atmospheric richness in a Chinese landscape handscroll.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses these effects to explain what ink can do; it does not identify the site’s painting as the work shown in the article illustrations.

## Leaving water unpainted

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Landscape in the Style of Shen Zhou”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/904721
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The Met notes that lightly coloring the river in this work was unusual because Chinese painters commonly indicated water by leaving the area unpainted.
- Boats, banks, ripples, and surrounding forms can allow an unpainted area to be understood as water.

Editorial boundary:

- Blank paper does not always mean water. The child-facing story explains how surrounding visual clues shape what a viewer sees.

## A handscroll more than seventy feet long

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Streams and Mountains without End”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/857205
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The Met describes this surviving Chinese landscape handscroll as more than seventy feet long.
- The work is meant to be viewed from right to left, with the landscape appearing as the handscroll unfurls.
- Its changing riverscape, varied ink tones, and interplay of solid and open space support the article’s journey metaphor.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses one unusually long surviving handscroll as an example; it does not imply that most handscrolls were this long.
