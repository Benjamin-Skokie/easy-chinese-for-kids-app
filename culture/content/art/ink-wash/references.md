# “一滴墨，怎样变成山、雨和雾？”— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Ink variation, paper, silk, and color

Source: Palace Museum, “‘墨分五色’”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/240484.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The phrase refers to effects produced by differences in ink concentration, dryness, and wetness and is connected with Zhang Yanyuan’s statement “运墨而五色具.”
- Historical and later lists of the “five” differ: examples include 焦、浓、重、淡、清 and 浓、淡、干、湿、黑.

Editorial boundary:

- “Five colors” is explained as an aesthetic and technical claim about variety, not five literal pigments or one universal calibrated scale.
- The article does not teach the old manuscript’s unsupported recipe of letting ink sit for thirty minutes or assign fixed subjects to each named shade.

Source: Palace Museum, “宣纸”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/241508.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Shengxuan readily absorbs water and ink and can create varied brush-and-ink effects.
- Shuxuan is treated with a sizing solution, making ink less likely to penetrate and supporting detailed drawing and repeated color application.

Editorial boundary:

- Not all Chinese paintings are on xuan paper, and not all papers called xuan behave identically.
- The child activity uses ordinary practice paper and washable paint; it is an observation of absorption, not a claim to reproduce historical materials.

Source: Smithsonian Museum Conservation Institute, John Winter, “Paints and supports in Far Eastern pictorial art”
URL: https://mci.si.edu/node/1202281
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Paper and silk were both important painting supports, with considerable variation in their structures and preparation.
- Traditional sizing for paper and silk could include animal glue and alum; support preparation affects how paint and ink behave.

Editorial boundary:

- The article distinguishes paper from silk and avoids claiming that ink always spreads in one predictable way.
- Historical sizing materials are described only generally; children are not instructed to mix or handle them.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Scientific Studies of Pigments in Chinese Paintings”
URL: https://asia.si.edu/research/publications-old/conversation-scientific-research-publications/scientific-studies-of-pigments-in-chinese-paintings/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Scientific study of more than two hundred Chinese paintings on paper and silk documents extensive pigment use from the Song dynasty through the early twentieth century.
- Imported colorants such as cochineal and Prussian blue entered Chinese palettes, with professional painters playing an important role in their use.

Editorial boundary:

- Water-and-ink painting is not presented as proof that Chinese painting rejected color.
- The article names broad pigment categories but does not give children instructions for handling historical, mineral, or unknown pigments.

## Ink methods and their historical boundaries

Source: Palace Museum, “破墨法”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/242650.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Broken ink can involve adding darker ink into lighter ink before it dries, or lighter ink into darker, to enrich layers and soften boundaries.
- Zhang Yanyuan’s text mentions broken-ink landscapes associated with Wang Wei and Zhang Zao; the method continued to develop afterward.

Editorial boundary:

- A textual attribution is not treated as a surviving demonstration or proof that one Tang painter invented the complete later technique.
- Broken ink is presented as one flexible method that can overlap with others.

Source: Palace Museum, “泼墨法”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/242649.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Later accounts connect splashed ink with the Tang painter called Wang Mo and describe broad ink movement, natural spreading, and forms emerging from washes.
- The method continued to develop in later periods.

Editorial boundary:

- The dramatic Tang account is a transmitted textual story, not an eyewitness film or a safe classroom procedure.
- Children are not asked to throw, splash, or spread unknown ink; the activity uses controlled drops and brushstrokes.

Source: Palace Museum, “米家山水”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/242648.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ink traditions could combine dot-like texture strokes, washes, broken ink, accumulated ink, and dry ink to suggest mist, rain, and changing atmosphere.

Editorial boundary:

- Technique names are treated as a toolbox with historical variation, not isolated recipes or a single linear sequence invented all at once.

## Handscroll viewing and spatial choices

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “A Poet’s Journey through Chinese Autumn Mountains”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/perspectives/chinese-handscroll-colophon
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Handscrolls were originally opened about one arm-span at a time rather than displayed fully at once.
- Successive sections create the experience of traveling through mountains, streams, villages, temples, and open space.

Editorial boundary:

- Moving or shifting viewpoints are explained in connection with appropriate formats, especially handscrolls, rather than declared a rule for every Chinese painting.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Ways of Seeing—Poetry and Painting”
URL: https://asia-archive.si.edu/learn/for-educators/teaching-china-with-the-smithsonian/lesson-plans/lesson-plan-ways-of-seeing-poetry-and-painting/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Handscrolls are viewed from right to left, one section at a time, in a process compared with reading chapters in a book.
- Chinese painting includes handscrolls, hanging scrolls, and album leaves, and some landscape painters used blue and green color.

Editorial boundary:

- The article avoids an East-versus-West absolute. It describes one-point and moving perspective as different choices and notes that Chinese artists used varied systems, including through cultural exchange.

## Painter communities and the literati boundary

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Painting with Words: Gentlemen Artists of the Ming Dynasty”
URL: https://asia.si.edu/whats-on/exhibitions/painting-with-words-gentlemen-artists-of-the-ming-dynasty/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ming Suzhou included both gentleman/literati artists and accomplished professional painters.
- Professional and literati artists were interdependent: professionals drew on literati subjects, while literati could contribute poetry and calligraphy; patron-client and teacher-student networks shaped careers.

Editorial boundary:

- Literati painting is treated as a powerful tradition, not the whole of Chinese painting or the only authentic category.
- Court, professional, temple, workshop, and popular/community production are not erased by a scholar-painter-centered history.

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Learning to Paint in Premodern China”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/learning-to-paint
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Painters learned through varied paths: family members, friends, manuals, treatises, and study of other works.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not present painting history as a sequence of isolated male geniuses inventing self-contained styles.

## Wang Wei, copies, and attribution

Source: National Palace Museum, *故宫学术季刊* 42.3, study of Wang Wei’s later image and *Wangchuan* transmission
URL: https://www.npm.gov.tw/NewChineseArtDownload.ashx?bid=5183
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Wang Wei’s position at the head of a southern/literati ink-painting lineage was strongly shaped by later art-historical systems, especially those associated with Dong Qichang.
- The original *Wangchuan Tu* did not survive; multiple copies and later interpretations circulated, and even the Northern Song Guo Zhongshu version survives through later transmission/rubbing evidence.

Editorial boundary:

- The article acknowledges old records of Wang Wei as a painter and his major later influence, but treats “ancestor/founder of ink painting” as later praise, not a documented one-person invention.
- No surviving work is casually presented as Wang Wei’s unquestioned original.

Source: National Palace Museum Digital Archive, “宋郭忠恕临王维辋川图 卷”
URL: https://digitalarchive.npm.gov.tw/Collection/Detail/14069?dep=P
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The museum catalogs the work explicitly as a copy after Wang Wei’s *Wangchuan Villa*, with inscriptions identifying it as a copy.
- The object demonstrates how later copying, inscriptions, and collection history form part of a painting’s transmission.

Editorial boundary:

- Copying is not automatically called forgery; it may transmit a composition, support learning, and create a new interpretation.
- Museum labels such as “copy after,” “attributed to,” and “bearing a name” are treated as evidence statements rather than nuisances to erase.

## Blank space and *Fishing Alone on a Cold River*

Source: National Institutes for Cultural Heritage, Japan, e-Museum / Tokyo National Museum, “寒江独钓图”
URL: https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100823&content_part_id=0&content_pict_id=0&langId=zh
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The painting is ink and light color on silk, dated to the Southern Song / thirteenth century, and cataloged as “attributed to Ma Yuan” (传马远笔).
- Its open area strongly contributes to the effect of a fisher in a vast cold river.
- A join in the silk above the boat suggests the work may originally have belonged to a larger composition.

Editorial boundary:

- The article retains the attribution marker and does not call the painting an unquestioned Ma Yuan original.
- Blank space is interpreted through relationships among visible marks and object history; not every unpainted area is assigned a mystical meaning.
- The app’s boat image is not this museum object and is explicitly captioned as a modern generated image.

## Modern and contemporary ink

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Ink Art: Past as Present in Contemporary China”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2013/ink-art
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese artists from the 1980s onward have transformed inherited ink traditions through painting, calligraphy, photography, woodblock printing, video, sculpture, abstraction, and work beyond the brush.
- Contemporary ink can remain connected with historical visual language while challenging or changing it.

Editorial boundary:

- Modern ink is not described as merely preserving a frozen ancient style, and “ink art” is not limited to black liquid on paper.

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Nineteenth- and Twentieth-Century Chinese Paintings from the Robert H. Ellsworth Collection”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2001/robert-ellsworth-collection
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Modern Chinese painters combined ink, mineral and water-soluble pigments, traditional formats, Western-influenced drawing, realism, abstraction, and vivid color in varied ways.

Editorial boundary:

- Modern change is presented as plural and contested rather than one simple replacement of “tradition” by “Western art.”

## Image and activity boundaries

- All three article images are retained and identified as modern generated illustrations, not historical paintings, museum photographs, copies, or details from named masterpieces.
- `2-ink-shades-brush.webp` illustrates a controlled modern exercise. Its bowls and row of tones are not the definitive five-color-ink system.
- `1-ink-landscape.webp` may help children notice tone, mist, and texture effects but cannot document a historical artist, school, dynasty, or exact method.
- `3-blank-river-boat.webp` is not Ma Yuan’s (or the attributed Ma Yuan-school) *Fishing Alone on a Cold River* and is not labeled as a Song painting.
- The activity uses washable children’s paint, ordinary disposable practice paper, an adult-protected table, and controlled drops or brushstrokes. It forbids old paper, calligraphy, art objects, museum objects, unknown ink, mineral pigments, splashing, and claims of reproducing an ancient work.

## 2026 child-facing revision

- The main article now moves from diluted ink to mountain textures, then through a handscroll and into the open water and sky made by blank paper.
- The classroom experiment, specialized technique list, painter classifications, and attribution discussion were removed from the children's narrative. The article still notes that Chinese ink painting can include color.
