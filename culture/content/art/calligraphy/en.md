# Dancing with a Brush: The Magic of Calligraphy

## One Sheet of Paper, One Brush, One Person

As Spring Festival gets close, a line forms at the old gentleman's stand on the street corner.

He spreads out a sheet of bright red paper. He lifts a brush and fills it with dark ink.

He takes a deep breath.

Then his wrist presses down, lifts, and turns.

The character *fu*, meaning good fortune, lands on the paper.

The ink is not even dry yet, and the people waiting in line are already clapping.

That character was not printed. It did not come from a machine.

It feels alive.

You can see the brush tip breathing through every part of the stroke: thick to thin, fast to slow, heavy to light.

A child nearby pulls on her mother's sleeve.

"Mom, I want to learn too."

Everyone writes words. But not every culture treats writing itself like a painting.

In China, when people look at calligraphy, they may not first ask what the words say. They may first look at whether one stroke is steady, whether one turn has strength, whether a line feels like wind passing through or a mountain standing still.

Other places have their own beautiful writing arts, such as Arabic calligraphy and Western decorative script.

But in China, calligraphy is often tied to poetry, painting, and even a person's character. Little by little, good handwriting becomes more than writing.

## Five Script Styles: Three Thousand Years of Changing Characters

Seal script is one of the oldest styles. You can see its curving lines in ancient bamboo texts and on bronze vessels used in rituals. The strokes look rounded, almost like iron wire bending. People still use seal script on seals today.

Clerical script came later and made the lines flatter and straighter. People describe some strokes as having a "silkworm head and swallow tail": the beginning is round like a silkworm's head, and the ending lifts like a swallow's tail. By this time, many characters looked less like pictures and more like the written strokes we know.

Regular script is where our usual way of writing Chinese characters comes from. The horizontal strokes are level. The vertical strokes are straight. The whole character stands upright. Most students begin calligraphy with regular script because it builds the bones first.

Running script is like regular script written faster. It is used for letters and notes. It is still easy to recognize, but it flows more smoothly.

Cursive script is the fastest and hardest to read. Sometimes you may not recognize the words at all. But you do not always need to understand them. Just watch the lines move, the speed change, and the ink go from wet to dry. It can feel like listening to music made only of lines.

## Wang Xizhi: The Words He Could Never Write Again After Sober Morning

In the year 353, Wang Xizhi invited friends to gather at the Orchid Pavilion in Shaoxing.

They drank wine beside a stream. Someone suggested that each person write a poem, and Wang Xizhi should write a preface for the collection.

Wang Xizhi picked up his brush and wrote freely on cocoon paper. The words came in one breath.

That piece, the *Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Gathering*, was later called the greatest work of running script under Heaven.

It has 324 Chinese characters. The character *zhi* appears 28 times, and each one is written differently.

The story says that the next day, after Wang Xizhi sobered up, he tried to copy the draft again. He copied it more than ten times.

None of the copies was as good as the first one.

The closest thing to perfection did not come from careful planning.

It came from one person, one cup of wine, one sheet of paper, and one afternoon when light from the stream happened to break into shining pieces beside the brush washer.

All of it met in one moment.

And that moment could not be made again.

---

## Summary

At the street corner, an old gentleman presses, lifts, and turns his brush. A *fu* character appears in black ink on red paper.

Seal, clerical, regular, running, and cursive scripts stretch across thousands of years. Wang Xizhi wrote 28 different *zhi* characters at the Orchid Pavilion, then could never make the same magic again after the wine wore off.

A fine piece of calligraphy is more than words. From the first stroke to the final lift of the brush, it feels as if everyone who ever held a Chinese brush is moving with you.
