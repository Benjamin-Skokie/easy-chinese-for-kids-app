# 一面墙是一千年前的照片吗？— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. UNESCO World Heritage Centre — “Mogao Caves”

URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/440/
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Mogao stood at a strategic point on the Silk Roads and preserves 492 decorated cave sanctuaries from roughly a thousand years of Buddhist art.
- The UNESCO description identifies scenes including a camel pulling a cart in Cave 302, agricultural workers in Cave 23, and the large Mount Wutai landscape in Cave 61 with mountains, rivers, cities, temples, roads, and caravans.

Editorial boundary:

- UNESCO's 492 count refers to decorated cave sanctuaries. The total number of surviving caves is given separately by the Dunhuang Academy.
- Everyday details in a religious mural are historical clues, not the equivalent of a photograph.

## 2. Dunhuang Academy — Mogao Caves site scale and history

Source: Interview with Su Bomin, Director of the Dunhuang Academy
URL: https://www.dha.ac.cn/info/1018/5334.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Mogao has 735 surviving caves, including 492 caves with murals and painted sculpture.
- Cave construction and mural painting continued across about a thousand years.

Editorial boundary:

- “More than 700 caves” and “nearly 500 caves with murals” deliberately distinguish the site's two different counts.

## 3. Dunhuang Academy — “Mogao Cave 23”

URL: https://www.dha.ac.cn/info/1425/3611.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- The Cave 23 farming scene illustrates the Lotus Sutra's comparison of Buddhist teaching to rain nourishing different plants.
- The scene includes an ox plowing a field and a nearby family: a father and child eating from bowls while a woman watches.

Editorial boundary:

- The scene is part of a Buddhist illustration. It can preserve recognizable details of ordinary life without functioning as a literal snapshot of one documented family.

## 4. International Dunhuang Programme / British Library — “Dunhuang’s Creative Culture”

URL: https://idp.bl.uk/discover/learning/dunhuang/dunhuang-articles/dunhuang-at-work/dunhuangs-creative-culture/
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Officials, members of the monastic community, wealthy families, rulers, and other believers commissioned or sponsored caves, sometimes including donor portraits.
- Cave making involved organized teams: laborers excavated with hammers and chisels, walls were prepared with mud and plaster, painters planned and transferred drawings, other workers prepared pigments and filled colors, and sculptors worked inside the caves.
- By the tenth century, Dunhuang had a painting academy with specialist workers.

Editorial boundary:

- Workshop organization changed across periods and caves. The article describes a collaborative process without claiming that every cave was produced in exactly the same way.

## 5. Dunhuang Academy — “Mogao Cave 257”

URL: https://www.dha.ac.cn/info/1425/3548.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Cave 257 contains the Northern Wei Deer King Jataka, the Buddhist story widely known through the image of the nine-colored deer.

Editorial boundary:

- The deer belongs to a religious story. Its appearance in a mural is not historical evidence for a magical animal.

## Editorial synthesis

- The article uses “photograph” as a child-friendly question, then explains why murals must be read as selected, mixed evidence rather than direct recordings.
- *Mural* is explained at its first English appearance as “large pictures painted directly on the walls and ceilings.” The Chinese text gives the matching explanation: “直接画在墙壁和洞顶上的大幅图画。”
- The page combines institutional counts carefully: more than 700 surviving caves in total, nearly 500 preserving murals.
