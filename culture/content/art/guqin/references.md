# Guqin — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Guqin structure, guzheng distinction, and changing strings

Source: Hong Kong Leisure and Cultural Services Department Music Office, “Music Exhibition — Guqin”
URL: https://www.lcsd.gov.hk/en/mo/activities/communityprogrammes/roving_exhibition_guqin.html/1000
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A common modern guzheng is larger, usually has twenty-one strings and movable bridges, while the guqin has seven strings, no row of bridges, and allows the left hand to slide.
- The guqin has thirteen studs marking harmonic positions.
- Silk strings were widely replaced by modern metal strings; silk remains in use and has a softer tonal character.

Editorial boundary:

- “Usually” and “common modern” are necessary because historical and regional guzheng forms vary.
- The article does not describe one string material as the only authentic choice or claim that every modern string has identical construction.

## Three basic sound types and UNESCO status

Source: UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, “Guqin and its music”
URL: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/guqin-and-its-music-00061?lang=en
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Guqin playing uses the basic categories *san* (open string), *an* (stopped string), and *fan* (harmonic).
- The page explains the hand actions that produce the three sound types.
- Guqin and its music were proclaimed a Masterpiece in 2003 and inscribed on the Representative List in 2008.
- Guqin has a strong historical association with elite and scholarly practice.

Editorial boundary:

- UNESCO's summary emphasizes the elite solo tradition, but this must not be expanded into “only male scholars played” or “the instrument was never heard publicly in any period.”
- Heritage listing supports safeguarding a living practice; it does not freeze one style as timeless.

## Formation of the seven-string, thirteen-stud form

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “The Sound of a Royal Qin”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/prince-lu-qin
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The familiar form with seven unfretted strings and thirteen *hui* emerged only in the late Han period.
- Guqin players produce slides, harmonics, ornaments, vibrato, and damped sounds.
- A surviving Prince Lu qin can be dated through its maker's seal, date, inscriptions, and historical context.

Editorial boundary:

- Earlier qin-related objects and texts do not prove that the modern standard form existed unchanged for three thousand years.
- Legends about very early players are cultural traditions, not direct archaeological dating.

Source: Palace Museum, “世间风雅君居首——故宫古琴与古琴文化”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/learing_detail/368477.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The *Book of Rites* preserves the line “士无故不彻琴瑟.”
- Seven strings were basically fixed by the Tang period.

Editorial boundary:

- The classical line expresses an ideal for the status group called *shi*. It is not evidence that all ancient people, or even all scholars, played daily.
- Fuxi, Shennong, Wen, and Wu invention or string-adding accounts remain traditional attributions.

## Reduced-character notation and reconstructed performance

Source: Palace Museum educational publication, “古琴谱与减字谱”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/pdf/5043/T00033_00.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Reduced-character notation combines shortened character components to record string number, *hui* position, and left- and right-hand techniques.
- Traditional notation primarily preserves fingering and position information; later scores sometimes added other systems and beat marks.
- Reading a score alone does not determine a complete performance.

Editorial boundary:

- The traditional attribution of the reduced system to Tang musician Cao Rou is reported as tradition, not used as a fixed invention fact.
- “Does not fully record rhythm” does not mean “contains no rhythmic clues” or that players may ignore historical style.

## Instrument making and the role of craftspeople

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Guqin,” object 2016.788a-b
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/732581
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A modern guqin object record identifies fir and zitan wood, deer-antler powder, lacquer, mother-of-pearl, and silk.
- Named contemporary makers and calligraphers show that an instrument is made material culture, not only an abstract scholarly symbol.

Editorial boundary:

- Materials vary by instrument. This object's precise recipe is not presented as universal for all periods.

## A Tang qin, later parts, repair, and inscriptions

Source: Palace Museum, “‘大圣遗音’琴”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/music/234738.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Palace Museum specialists identify *Dasheng Yiyin* as a Tang qin associated with the date 756.
- The object has internal and external inscriptions, old lacquer crack patterns, replaced string-supporting parts, and a documented modern restoration.
- The museum's account explains the evidence used in its identification and the object's later condition history.

Editorial boundary:

- A name or inscription alone cannot authenticate a Tang date.
- A “Tang qin” can include later replaced parts, inscriptions, coatings, or repairs; the article does not describe it as untouched since the Tang.

Source: Palace Museum research paper, “唐琴与两张‘飞泉’琴铭刻之辨识”
URL: https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2020/02/19/u5e4ce7af2b7f5.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Researchers distinguish old inscriptions from later additions by examining how cuts interact with lacquer crack patterns and other material evidence.
- Some inscriptions on recognized Tang qins were added in Song, Yuan, or Ming times.

Editorial boundary:

- Inscriptions are evidence that must be dated and interpreted, not labels that automatically prove the age of the wooden instrument.

## Scholar ideals and broader communities of players

Source: Central Conservatory of Music journal, Li Xiaoge, “携琴啸咏——玉山雅集琴事初考”
URL: https://xuebao.ccom.edu.cn/CN/Y2026/V0/I1/108
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A late Yuan gathering included players connected to Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist traditions and women guqin practitioners.
- Guqin activity included solo playing, accompanied song, and combinations with other instruments.

Editorial boundary:

- One gathering does not prove identical participation in every period; it is evidence that “only solitary male scholars” is too narrow.

Source: Chinese Music journal, “文人琴与艺人琴关系的历史演变”
URL: https://cmj.ccmusic.edu.cn/zh/article/42081305/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Scholarship distinguishes interacting literati and professional-performer traditions across different periods.
- Qin practice cannot be reduced to a single timeless social group or use.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses this source to broaden the frame, not to teach its full four-period scholarly model as settled fact to children.

## Boya, Ziqi, and the later growth of the story

Source: Guangming Daily republication, “伯牙子期故事流变”
URL: https://www.nydi.gov.cn/sitesources/nysjwjw/page_pc/xcjd/s/articlef5a9a7826fb14a3f953454b453cdc2d7.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Versions survive in the *Liezi* and *Lüshi Chunqiu* with different emphases.
- The *Lüshi Chunqiu* includes Ziqi's death and Boya's breaking of the strings.
- Later fiction expanded the story with more detailed places, meetings, identities, and dramatic action.

Editorial boundary:

- Boya and Ziqi are presented through a textual tradition, not as a fully documented biographical event.
- The illustration's woodcutter, clothing, landscape, facial expressions, and exact qin form are modern imagination.

## The transmission history of High Mountains and Flowing Water

Source: Counsellors' Office of the State Council, Tian Qing, “古琴——中国文人的乐器”
URL: https://www.counsellor.gov.cn/2020-11/09/c_1210880297.htm
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- *High Mountains* and *Flowing Water* survive as separate pieces in transmitted notation.
- Early surviving notation is associated with the Ming *Shenqi Mipu*.
- A version of *Flowing Water* widely performed today comes through Zhang Kongshan and the *Tianwenge Qinpu* tradition.

Editorial boundary:

- The textual phrases about mountains and water do not prove that Boya played the same notes, rhythm, or version heard today.
- The story tradition and the histories of performable scores are connected but must not be collapsed into one unbroken recording.

## 2026 child-facing revision

- The main article now distinguishes guqin from guzheng, explains open, pressed, and harmonic sounds, shows how an instrument ages, and tells the Boya–Zhong Ziqi friendship legend.
- It does not claim that Boya played the modern piece *Flowing Water* or that today's instrument remained unchanged for three thousand years.
