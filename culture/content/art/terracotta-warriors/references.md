# 地下为什么站着一支泥土军队？— References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Discovery, mausoleum context, and the limits of excavation

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/441/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The archaeological site was discovered in 1974, and thousands of figures are believed to remain underground.
- The warrior pits belong to a much larger mausoleum complex centered on the First Emperor's tomb.
- The complex includes burial pits, tombs, structures, figures, horses, chariots, and weapons rather than only one “army pit.”

Editorial boundary:

- The warriors must not be described as standing inside the emperor's main burial chamber.
- The large estimated total is not the number of complete figures already excavated or restored.

Source: Smithsonian Magazine, “Zhao Kangmin, the Archaeologist Who Pieced Together China’s Terracotta Warriors”
URL: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/zhao-kangmin-archaeologist-who-pieced-together-chinas-terracotta-warriors-has-died-81-180969153/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Farmers digging a well found fragments in 1974.
- Archaeologist Zhao Kangmin was among the first specialists to recognize their importance and piece fragments together.

Editorial boundary:

- Sources differ over individual credit for “discovering” the warriors, so the child-facing article credits the farmers' find and the cultural workers' recognition without turning one person into the sole discoverer.
- The dramatic scene in the old manuscript—an intact face staring at Yang Zhifa, a dropped hoe, exact dialogue and reactions—was removed because it was not reliably documented.

## Pits and military interpretations

Source: Smarthistory, “The Tomb of the First Emperor”
URL: https://smarthistory.org/tomb-first-emperor-qin/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The warrior pits are one part of a network of pits, tombs, and structures around the central mound.
- Only a fraction of the full complex has been excavated.
- Archaeologists use objects, spatial relationships, and non-invasive surveys to interpret the complex.

Editorial boundary:

- Pit 1 as a main formation, Pit 2 as a mixed force, and Pit 3 as a command center are archaeological interpretations based on layout and contents, not labels written by Qin builders.
- The article does not present the pits as a perfectly preserved snapshot of a known historical battle formation.

## Making figures and weapons: workshops, variation, and organization

Source: University College London and Emperor Qin Shihuang's Mausoleum Site Museum, “Imperial Logistics: The Making of the Terracotta Army”
URL: https://www.ucl.ac.uk/terracotta-army/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Research examines crafting methods, workshops, administration, material evidence, inscriptions, and spatial patterns.
- The figures and weapons were products of organized craft production rather than unexplained individual masterpieces.

Editorial boundary:

- Facial variety does not prove that every warrior is a portrait of a particular soldier.
- “Made from molds” should not be simplified into one whole-body mold repeated thousands of times; parts, repeated methods, assembly, and hand-finishing all mattered.

Source: P. S. Quinn et al., “Building the Terracotta Army: ceramic craft technology and production at Qin Shihuang’s mausoleum”
URL: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1570749/1/Quinn-P_building%20the%20Terracotta%20Army_.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Ceramic analysis connects clay preparation, forming, workshops, stamps, and inscriptions to centralized and varied production processes.
- Similarities and differences can be used to investigate production groups and organization.

Editorial boundary:

- Workshop marks support study of makers and administrative groups; they do not supply a personal military biography for each face.
- The invented names, ranks, ethnic identities, scars, and life stories in the old manuscript were removed.

## Original color and conservation

Source: Technical University of Munich, Chair of Conservation-Restoration, “Conservation of the Polychromy”
URL: https://www.arc.ed.tum.de/en/rkk/archive-emmerling/china-project-bmbf/subprojects-2007-2013/tomb-of-the-first-emporer/polychromy/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Painted surfaces were built over lacquer layers, and changes in moisture can destabilize the lacquer and overlying paint.
- Conservation treatments aim to keep the layers stable rather than merely “restore” guessed colors.

Editorial boundary:

- Color loss is not described as pigment turning into smoke or simply oxidizing away.
- The exact original color scheme of every figure is not known; the retained image is labeled a modern imagined reconstruction.

## Chromium and the bronze-weapons myth

Source: Marcos Martinón-Torres et al., Scientific Reports, “Surface chromium on Terracotta Army bronze weapons is neither an ancient anti-rust treatment nor the reason for their good preservation”
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-40613-7
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chromium was detected on only a minority of tested weapon parts and did not correlate with preservation.
- The chromium most likely transferred from chromium-rich lacquer associated with nearby objects after burial.
- Bronze composition and the burial soil's moderately alkaline pH, fine particles, low aeration, and low organic content better explain preservation.

Editorial boundary:

- The article explicitly rejects the once-popular claim that Qin makers chrome-plated the weapons to prevent rust.
- This correction does not diminish the documented skill and organization of Qin bronze production.

## Laborers, prisoners, and the cost of construction

Source: Tamsin C. O’Connell et al., Scientific Reports, “Tracing the locality of prisoners and workers at the Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang”
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4890548/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Archaeological and isotope research studied people buried in the Liyi public cemetery and Shanren prisoner mass grave associated with the mausoleum workforce.
- The workforce included skilled craftspeople as well as prisoners and other compelled laborers from different regions.
- The ancient claim of roughly 700,000 workers comes through historical texts.

Editorial boundary:

- The exact figure of 700,000 cannot be independently verified and is labeled as an ancient report, not a measured total.
- The article does not call every artisan a slave or invent an individual worker's life.
- The achievement and the human cost are presented together.

## Mercury, Sima Qian, and the unopened chamber

Source: Chang Yong and Li Tong, *Journal of Geochemical Exploration*, “Application of mercury survey technique over the mausoleum of emperor Qin Shi Huang” (1985)
URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0375674285900160
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Soil sampling over the mound found a mercury anomaly that researchers compared with Sima Qian's account.

Editorial boundary:

- A surface-soil anomaly is evidence consistent with mercury use; it is not direct observation of a liquid underground river or proof of its mechanism.
- Later atmospheric research has not found a simple, large present-day mercury plume, so the evidence should not be overstated.

Source: Sune Svanberg et al., Scientific Reports, “Mercury as a Geophysical Tracer Gas—Emissions from the Emperor Qin Tomb in Xi'an Studied by Laser Radar”
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-67305-x
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Researchers mapped elevated and localized atmospheric mercury concentrations over parts of the mound that correlated with earlier soil hot spots.
- The study treats the vapor pattern as support for mercury in the sealed complex, while also noting elevated regional pollution, wind effects, and the difficulty of the measurements.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says mercury “may have been used” and does not claim that a large amount of liquid mercury is confirmed beneath the mound.
- Sima Qian's rivers and mechanical crossbows remain a historical account, not excavated fact.

## 2026 child-facing revision

- The main article now moves from the 1974 well discovery to modular hand-finished faces, Qin Shihuang's underground army, the unopened main tomb, and the warriors' lost colors.
- The chromium debate and detailed pit labels were removed from the children's narrative. The mercury rivers remain clearly identified as an ancient written account, not an opened-tomb observation.
