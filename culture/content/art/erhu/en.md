# Erhu: Two Strings Tell a Story

## The Simplest Instrument, the One Most Like a Human Voice

The *erhu* may be one of the simplest-looking Chinese instruments.

A wooden sound box.

A bamboo neck.

Two strings.

One bow.

That is almost all.

But the sound it makes can feel like a person crying, laughing, or sighing.

The erhu has no fingerboard. The left hand presses directly on strings that hang in the air. When the fingers slide up and down, notes do not simply jump from one to the next. They glide.

That is why erhu melodies often feel like the curve of a human voice.

A piano gives you one note when you press a key.

The erhu bends slowly between notes, as if the sound is turning its head.

## Moon Reflected on the Second Spring: A Blind Musician's Last Song

The most famous erhu piece is *Moon Reflected on the Second Spring*.

The musician who made it was named Hua Yanjun. Most people knew him as Abing. Because he could not see, people also called him Blind Abing.

He was a street musician in Wuxi. He could not see, and he lived a very poor life. During the day, he walked through the streets with a cane and played erhu for money. At night, after work, he sat near an alley and played his own tunes under the moon.

No one knew how beautiful the melodies he played at night were.

Then, in 1950, two teachers from a music school came to find him with a big, heavy recorder.

Abing had not played for several years. His strings were broken. The snakeskin on the sound box was torn. People patched together an instrument for him.

Abing said, "My fingers are stiff. I do not remember."

They begged him to try.

He picked up the erhu, closed his eyes, and played three pieces.

One of them was empty and quiet, patient and deep. Later, people called it *Moon Reflected on the Second Spring*.

A few months after the recording, Abing died.

Today, the piece is one of the most famous works in Chinese music. A melody once played by a blind street musician in a dark alley has been arranged and performed again and again in concert halls in Japan, the United States, Europe, and beyond.

## The Erhu and the Horse

The erhu's older name was *huqin*.

The word *hu* was often used for people who lived north and west of China. Like the pipa, this family of instruments came into China's old farming heartland from farther away and changed over time.

People on the northern grasslands used horsehair for the bow and animal skin over the sound box.

In a way, half of a huqin was horse.

That may be why the erhu's sound often carries a lonely grassland feeling. Close your eyes and listen. You may not hear only a gentle southern tune.

You may hear wind moving through tall grass, where cattle and sheep appear as the grass bends.

---

## Summary

The erhu has only two strings and a very simple body. But the sliding sound on those strings can easily remind people of a human voice.

One piece, *Moon Reflected on the Second Spring*, helped many listeners remember the quiet, deep sadness in Chinese folk music.

From a grassland instrument to Abing's street-music voice, the erhu uses the fewest parts to tell very deep stories.
