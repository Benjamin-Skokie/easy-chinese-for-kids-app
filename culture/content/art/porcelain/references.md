# Porcelain — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Pottery, proto-porcelain, and the problem of a sharp boundary

Source: National Museum of China, “原始瓷尊”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202107/t20210728_250886.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- This early Shang vessel has a kaolin-rich body, an artificial lime glaze, a firing temperature around 1,200°C, a hard body, and low water absorption.
- Related wares improved during the Western Zhou and already possessed basic characteristics later associated with porcelain.

Editorial boundary:

- “Proto-porcelain” describes a scholarly classification in a long technical development. It does not mean its makers were culturally or technically “primitive.”
- The object does not support a single inventor, a precise invention day, or a fully mature modern porcelain recipe in the Shang.

Source: Palace Museum, “秦汉原始瓷略论”
URL: https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2021/12/21/u61c1424300fa9.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Porcelain definitions normally consider the body material, high-temperature firing, and glaze together.
- Proto-porcelain bridges earlier high-fired glazed ceramic traditions and later mature celadon.
- The terminology and exact classification of borderline objects have been debated.

Editorial boundary:

- The article preserves this uncertainty instead of presenting “pottery,” “proto-porcelain,” and “porcelain” as three perfectly sealed boxes.
- A clear ringing sound, whiteness, or translucency can be a clue but is not by itself a complete archaeological identification.

## Eastern Han and the emergence of mature porcelain

Source: National Museum of China, “青瓷四系罐”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202110/t20211028_251938.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The late Eastern Han celadon jar has a kaolin-rich body, glaze, a firing temperature around 1,300°C, tight body-glaze bonding, and low water absorption.
- The museum describes it as meeting the conditions of mature porcelain and places it within a development from Xia, Shang, and Zhou proto-porcelain and hard pottery.

Editorial boundary:

- This object supports mature porcelain in the late Eastern Han; it does not show that every Eastern Han ceramic was porcelain.
- A museum object's date and technical analysis are stronger evidence than a dramatic story about a named inventor.

Source: National Museum of China, “最早的瓷器品牌”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/yj/xscg/xslw/201812/t20181224_33107.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- A common scholarly definition considers porcelain clay, a glassy glaze, and firing at or above roughly 1,200°C.
- The source places “true porcelain” in the late Eastern Han and further technical maturity in the Three Kingdoms period.

Editorial boundary:

- This timeline is presented beside, not used to erase, the National Museum object's description of late Eastern Han mature porcelain.
- Exact labels vary with definitions and evidence; children are told about that variation instead of being given false certainty.

## Porcelain materials, firing, and the many kinds of Chinese ceramics

Source: National Museum of China, “中国古代瓷器艺术”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zl/zlhg/201812/t20181220_32218.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- High-fired Eastern Han porcelain could reach approximately 1,300°C and develop a dense, low-absorption body and well-bonded glaze.
- Chinese ceramic history includes celadon, white porcelain, black wares, underglaze and overglaze decoration, colored glazes, and many other traditions.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not equate all Chinese porcelain with blue-and-white.
- Temperatures are approximate and differ by body, glaze, kiln, place, and period.

Source: National Museum of China, “科技的力量——中国古代陶瓷科技展”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/202012kxyjs/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Proto-porcelain developed in the Shang and Zhou periods, while late Eastern Han celadon represents a major stage in porcelain development.
- Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing production involved many regional kiln traditions and changing technologies.
- Jingdezhen became a national production center in the Ming and Qing; porcelain exports expanded across multiple periods.

Editorial boundary:

- Jingdezhen is not described as China's only important kiln center in every century.
- “Kiln fire for a thousand years” is not treated literally as an uninterrupted flame.

## Jingdezhen raw materials, organization, and labor

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Jingdezhen Handicraft Porcelain Industry Sites”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1765/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The sites document porcelain production from the tenth through nineteenth centuries, including extraction, transport, kiln design, and large-scale social organization.
- The town included the Imperial Kiln, civilian kilns, guilds, and merchant associations.
- Jingdezhen developed the two-part use of kaolin and porcelain stone and became a center of technical innovation and global trade.

Editorial boundary:

- The 2026 World Heritage inscription recognizes a long industrial landscape; it does not make Jingdezhen the sole source of all Chinese porcelain.
- Modern heritage recognition is not projected backward as a title used unchanged in every dynasty.

Source: ICOMOS, “Evaluations of Nominations of Cultural and Mixed Properties,” Jingdezhen evaluation
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/document/226090
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Porcelain stone and kaolin were mined and processed around Jingdezhen; mixed together, kaolin widened the firing range and supported larger and more complex objects.
- Raw-material sites, river transport, workshops, kilns, packaging shops, guilds, and merchants formed a connected production system.
- Shaping, decorating, pigment preparation, kiln loading, and kiln monitoring became specialized tasks.
- The Ming imperial factory coexisted and interacted with civilian kilns; Jingdezhen's role changed across Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing periods.
- Persian cobalt reached Jingdezhen through trade networks in the Yuan and Ming.

Editorial boundary:

- The material pair is important to Ming-Qing Jingdezhen, but it is not a universal recipe for every porcelain body worldwide.
- The article names categories of workers without inventing a single workshop roster or pretending all periods used identical divisions of labor.

Source: National Museum of China, “窑火瓷珍转心瓶”
URL: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/yj/xscg/xslw/201812/t20181224_33277.shtml
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Qing imperial works produced for the court, while civilian kilns were central to Jingdezhen's broader prosperity.
- Official wares could be fired in civilian kiln space, and civilian production itself included several business arrangements.
- Labor was divided among clay preparation, painting, kiln firing, and even separate outlining and color-filling work.

Editorial boundary:

- One Qing-period system is not applied unchanged to the Song or Yuan.
- The article rejects unsupported claims such as “only one of every thousand pieces succeeded.” Loss rates varied and require specific evidence.

Source: Palace Museum, “匣钵”
URL: https://www.dpm.org.cn/lemmas/239526.html
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Saggars protect wares from direct contact with flame, smoke, dust, and falling kiln debris during firing.

Editorial boundary:

- A saggar reduces contamination risk; it does not guarantee that every object survives firing.
- The article does not turn professional kiln firing into a children's home experiment.

## Blue-and-white porcelain and West Asian exchange

Source: Victoria and Albert Museum, “Chinese blue-and-white ceramics”
URL: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/chinese-blue-and-white-ceramics
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Blue-and-white is generally made with cobalt pigment on a pale body beneath transparent glaze.
- Early examples exist in the Tang, while Yuan Jingdezhen refined recipes and firing for large-scale production.
- Cobalt could come from Yunnan or be imported from the Middle East; much Yuan production was intended for Middle Eastern markets.
- Chinese export workshops later adapted objects for European forms and uses.

Editorial boundary:

- Cobalt does not always look identical before firing, and its fired blue varies; the article avoids saying painters could see “nothing at all.”
- Blue-and-white is one ceramic tradition, not a synonym for Chinese porcelain.

Source: British Museum, Islamic Gallery, “Jug”
URL: https://islamicworld.britishmuseum.org/collection/RRM13115
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese blue-and-white was coveted in the Islamic world in the early 1300s and traveled by diplomacy, trade, and military campaigns.
- Craftspeople in Iran and Central Asia recreated blue-and-white effects locally on stonepaste and adapted Chinese motifs.

Editorial boundary:

- The exchange is not framed as passive “copying” by the Islamic world or as a one-way spread from China.
- Local materials, shapes, and preferences created new works rather than inferior duplicates.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, “Blue”
URL: https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/art-stories/colors/blue/
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Cobalt oxide was used beneath a clear glaze to create blue decoration.
- Much cobalt used in China in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries was imported from West Asia.
- Chinese makers combined local ceramic knowledge with imported material, and the resulting wares circulated globally.

Editorial boundary:

- Imported cobalt is one part of the story; Chinese recipes, skilled brushwork, kiln control, and local materials remain essential.
- The text does not claim that every Yuan or Ming blue-and-white object used imported cobalt.

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Plate with fish,” object 1984.296
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39638
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- This mid-fourteenth-century Yuan plate from Jingdezhen uses cobalt blue under a transparent glaze.
- Dark “heaped and piled” areas show that cobalt behavior and control were not perfectly uniform.

Editorial boundary:

- A single plate illustrates variation; it does not provide a recipe for all blue-and-white porcelain.
- Centuries-old blue is not described as chemically or visually identical to a piece fired today.

## Export wares, European competition, and local reinvention

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Ewer with the Royal Arms of Portugal,” object 202581
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/202581
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- The Jingdezhen ewer, made about 1520–40 for the Portuguese market, adapts a West Asian metalwork form.
- It bears the Portuguese royal arms painted upside down, showing the complexity and occasional misreading involved in export commissions.

Editorial boundary:

- The object is used as one vivid example, not as proof that all export painters misunderstood foreign symbols.
- Hybrid form is treated as evidence of interaction, not cultural “purity” or failure.

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “German and Austrian Porcelain of the Eighteenth Century”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/it/essays/german-and-austrian-porcelain-in-the-eighteenth-century
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Chinese and Japanese hard-paste porcelain was highly valued in seventeenth-century Europe.
- Johann Friedrich Böttger's experiments achieved white high-fired hard-paste porcelain in 1709, and the Meissen factory was established in 1710.
- Meissen then developed forms, colors, and decoration within a European court and factory setting.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not make Böttger the inventor of porcelain worldwide; it identifies an early European hard-paste breakthrough.
- European porcelain is not described as a simple endpoint of a race or as proof that Asian knowledge stopped developing.

Source: Victoria and Albert Museum, “Teapot design through time”
URL: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/teapots-through-time
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- Dutch East India Company control of Japanese porcelain trade helped maintain supply to Europe during disruption in Chinese production.
- East Asian porcelain could be modified after arrival, including the addition of European metal mounts.

Editorial boundary:

- Maritime trade is described as involving chartered companies and colonial power, not as a neutral parade of beautiful objects.
- Chinese and Japanese supply histories changed over time; “Chinese export porcelain” cannot stand for every Asian object in Europe.

Source: Victoria and Albert Museum, “The influence of East Asian lacquer on European furniture”
URL: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/east-asian-lacquer-influence
Accessed: 2026-08-21

Supports:

- English uses *china* for porcelain, just as *japan* became associated with lacquer and its imitation.
- European chartered companies competed in lucrative Asian trade and commissioned goods for home markets.

Editorial boundary:

- The vocabulary is evidence of deep association in English usage; the article does not offer a simplistic or unsupported etymology for the country name China.
- Consumer demand, commercial adaptation, colonial expansion, and unequal power are kept in the same frame.
