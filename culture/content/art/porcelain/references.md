# Porcelain — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## 1. Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art — porcelain materials and qualities

Source: “Cup,” National Museum of Asian Art
URL: https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm%3Afsg_F1914.94/
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Source: “Jiangxi province—the Jingdezhen kilns,” National Museum of Asian Art
URL: https://publications.asia.si.edu/seaceramics/place/location.php?group=7&key=102
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Porcelain can have a hard, translucent, resonant body.
- Jingdezhen used porcelain stone and later added kaolin to make a whiter, more workable body.
- Jingdezhen workshops produced for the court, domestic buyers, and overseas trade.

Editorial boundary:

- A ringing sound is a memorable physical quality, not a complete scientific test for identifying porcelain.
- Firing temperature, recipe, thickness, glaze, and kiln conditions vary; the story therefore says “above 1,000°C” rather than giving one universal temperature.

## 2. British Museum — Doucai Chicken Cup

Source: “wine-cup,” museum number PDF,A.748
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_PDF-A-748
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- This small Ming Chenghua-period cup was made at Jingdezhen between 1465 and 1487.
- Its exterior shows a rooster, hen, and chicks among lilies, peonies, and rocks.
- The cup is only 83 millimetres in diameter and 38 millimetres high.

Editorial boundary:

- The article describes the real museum object, but the accompanying illustration deliberately does not copy or reconstruct it.
- The surviving cup shows what succeeded; excavated fragments at imperial kiln sites preserve evidence of rejected and failed wares.

## 3. Jingdezhen Municipal Government — imperial-kiln remains and rejected porcelain

Source: “御窑厂遗址”
URL: https://www.jdz.gov.cn/zjcd/mljdz/tscd/t995407.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Archaeological work at the Jingdezhen imperial-kiln site uncovered large quantities of porcelain fragments and kiln remains from imperial production.
- Imperial wares that failed to meet required standards were broken and buried rather than entering ordinary circulation.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses this evidence to explain the strict selection process, without implying that every broken piece failed for the same reason.
- “Rejected” refers to wares that did not meet court standards; it does not mean that archaeologists recovered complete stacks of intentionally discarded finished cups.

## 4. Victoria and Albert Museum — Chinese blue-and-white ceramics

Source: “Chinese blue-and-white ceramics”
URL: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/chinese-blue-and-white-ceramics
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Blue-and-white ceramics use cobalt pigment on a pale body beneath a transparent glaze.
- Jingdezhen potters developed major blue-and-white traditions.
- Cobalt could be sourced in China or imported from the Middle East, while finished wares traveled outward to overseas markets.

Editorial boundary:

- Cobalt sources changed across periods and objects; the story does not claim that every blue-and-white vessel used imported cobalt.
- Blue-and-white is one important porcelain tradition, not a synonym for all Chinese porcelain.

## 5. Metropolitan Museum of Art — Chinese export porcelain

Source: “East and West: Chinese Export Porcelain”
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/east-and-west-chinese-export-porcelain
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Chinese porcelains traveled with cargoes that included tea and silk.
- Porcelain was often stored low in ships because it could act as ballast and was not harmed by water.
- Chinese workshops made forms and decorations for overseas customers.

Editorial boundary:

- The article describes a long, changing trade rather than suggesting that one bowl followed one universal route.

## 6. Victoria and Albert Museum — ceramic terms and European hard-paste porcelain

Source: “An A–Z of Ceramics”
URL: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-z-of-ceramics
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Hard-paste porcelain is fired at high temperatures and can be hard, white, translucent, resonant, and nonporous.
- European makers investigated porcelain for years; successful hard-paste porcelain in Saxony dates to 1709, followed by the Meissen factory in 1710.
- Firing transforms ceramic colors and textures, and small changes in temperature and oxygen can change the result.

Editorial boundary:

- “The fire takes over” is child-facing narrative language. Potters still prepared, loaded, fired, monitored, cooled, and unloaded the kiln with great skill.

## 7. Jingdezhen kiln form and illustration research

Source: Jingdezhen Municipal Government, “景德镇是如何一步步走向世界，成为‘瓷都’的？”
URL: https://www.jdz.gov.cn/zwzx/jrcd/t1056776.shtml
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre / ICOMOS, “Evaluations of Nominations of Cultural and Mixed Properties”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/document/226090
Accessed: 2026-09-04

Supports:

- Jingdezhen used several kiln forms over time: dragon kilns, gourd-shaped kilns, mantou kilns, and later egg-shaped kilns.
- In the Yuan and Ming periods, Jingdezhen artisans developed the gourd-shaped kiln; the egg-shaped town kiln became important in the Qing dynasty.
- Traditional kilns ranged across several forms and sizes; the loading opening was only one part of the complete kiln body.

Illustration boundary:

- The new page-two image is an original, deliberately simplified teaching illustration—not a photograph or a reconstruction of one archaeological site.
- It depicts a fifteenth-century Jingdezhen gourd-shaped kiln during firing. The fire is contained at the front firebox, while the loading opening remains closed; the image does not suggest that finished porcelain was removed while the kiln was burning.
