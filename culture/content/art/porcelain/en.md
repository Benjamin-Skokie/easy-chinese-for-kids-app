# Porcelain: Clay as Smooth as Jade

## Why Does "China" Mean China and Porcelain?

In English, "China" can mean two things:

the country China,
and porcelain.

When a person in Europe long ago lifted a porcelain cup to drink tea, the same word could carry a whole dream of a faraway country.

Somewhere on that distant land, people imagined, there was a small town where kiln fires had burned for more than a thousand years. There, clay could turn into something smooth, glowing, and almost like jade.

---

## Pottery and Porcelain: Not Just Hotter Fire

Many people think pottery and porcelain are basically the same.

Both are made from earth, right?

Not quite.

Pottery can be made from ordinary clay and fired at lower temperatures, around 700 or 800 degrees Celsius. It may feel rough, and if you scrape it, powder may come off.

Porcelain uses kaolin, a very pure, fine white clay. It needs heat above about 1,300 degrees Celsius. At that heat, the clay changes deeply. It begins to become partly glass-like.

After cooling, porcelain can have a smooth, hard surface and a clear ringing sound.

In one way, porcelain sits somewhere between clay and glass.

It is not just mud anymore.

It has become something people made, hard and shining like stone.

After firing, the body of fine porcelain can be slightly see-through. Hold a Ming dynasty sweet-white porcelain cup up to the light, and a soft milky glow may shine through the wall.

Tap it, and it rings:

*dang...*

For a long time, Chinese craftspeople were the main people who knew how to make true hard porcelain. In the early 1700s, Meissen in Germany finally made some of Europe's first true hard porcelain.

In the Meissen workshop, many failed test pieces were smashed before they found the answer.

The worried worker waiting for the kiln to cool was facing a problem Jingdezhen potters had already learned to understand.

## Blue-and-White Porcelain: A Stunning Accident of Fire

Blue-and-white porcelain is one of the most famous kinds of Chinese porcelain.

The steps are tricky.

Before the white porcelain body is fired, the painter uses a special color called cobalt to paint flowers, birds, people, fish, or vines. But cobalt does not look bright blue at first. It looks gray-black.

The painter dips a brush into this dull powdery color and paints on pale clay.

It is hard to see clearly. Everything looks gray.

So the painter works almost from memory: a peony with curling vines, a fish among lotus leaves, every petal and every leaf stroke planned without a sketch.

After painting, a clear glassy coating goes over the surface. Then the piece enters a kiln heated to about 1,300 degrees Celsius.

Inside the fire, the gray cobalt turns into pure blue.

When the kiln opens, the dull-looking clay has become a white bowl covered with blue peonies, fish, and lotus leaves.

That deep blue is not ordinary paint sitting on top.

The cobalt has entered the glaze itself. A blue from 600 years ago can still look as bright as if it came from the kiln yesterday.

Pour hot tea into an old blue-and-white bowl, and a painted goldfish at the bottom may look as if it has just taken a sip.

---

## Jingdezhen: Kiln Fires Burning for a Thousand Years

Jingdezhen in Jiangxi has made porcelain since the Song dynasty, for more than a thousand years.

An emperor gave the town part of his special emperor name, Jingde. From then on, the town was called Jingdezhen.

Around Jingdezhen were porcelain-clay hills, pine forests, streams, and water-powered mills. Clay could be shaped into the body. Pinewood could fire the kilns. Water power could crush glaze materials.

Within a few dozen kilometers, people could find almost everything needed to make porcelain.

Few towns in history have kept making one famous thing for so long.

Old Jingdezhen potters knew the right mix of kaolin clay, glaze stone, and pinewood almost better than a written recipe.

When kiln fires were lit in spring, temperatures rose step by step. During the hottest days, the whole town chopped wood, carried fuel, watched the fire, and checked the heat day and night.

The streets smelled of minerals and pine smoke, sharp and sweet at the same time.

One full kiln of good porcelain could feed more than 50 craftspeople and their families for a year.

---

## Summary

Europe spent a long time trying to make true hard porcelain. By then, Jingdezhen's kilns had already burned for many generations.

Blue-and-white cobalt looked gray on the unfired clay. But in a kiln around 1,300 degrees Celsius, it changed into pure blue.

Hold a Ming dynasty sweet-white porcelain cup toward the light, and its wall glows softly.

This is clay, but it is no longer ordinary earth. It has become something like jade and glass.

The fact that English uses "China" for both the country and porcelain shows how deeply porcelain impressed people far away.
