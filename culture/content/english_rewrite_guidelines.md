# English Rewrite Rule for Chinese Culture Articles

Last updated: 2026-09-27

The English version is for native English-speaking children, with a distinct audience contract for Easy, Medium, and Advanced. Do not apply one “children's English” voice to all three.

The English should read like good children's nonfiction, not like a translated text.

Core standard:

`Accurate idea, natural child voice, clear mental picture.`

## Write English from the brief, not from Chinese sentences

For a fully rewritten Culture article, research should first become a shared story-and-facts brief. The brief records:

- the story spine and scene order;
- factual claims that must remain;
- uncertainty that must not disappear;
- inventions and familiar retelling details that are not supported;
- the intended reading level and emotional experience.

Write the English from that brief as an original piece of children's writing. Do not keep the Chinese prose open and move through it sentence by sentence or paragraph by paragraph. Even careful nonliteral translation can inherit Chinese information order, emphasis, nouns, transitions, and explanatory habits. Chinese must also be written independently from the same brief; neither language is the other's master draft.

After the English draft is complete:

1. Read it aloud without looking at the Chinese.
2. Rewrite anything that an American parent would not naturally say to a child.
3. Check the English against the shared brief for missing facts, added certainty, or invented scenes.
4. Compare it with the Chinese only at the end, checking story beats and factual claims rather than sentence alignment.

English and Chinese may use different sentence counts, paragraph breaks, transitions, and images. They must share the same trustworthy story, not the same syntax.

## Calibrate for the selected reading level

The existing general guidance below fits the Story level. Every manuscript must first apply the standard for its own version:

- **Early Reader:** written for U.S. kindergarten listeners and first-grade supported readers. The whole article has one main idea and normally only two to four concrete supporting facts. Use familiar spoken words, short idea units, concrete actions, helpful repetition, and a warm read-aloud rhythm. Do not mistake grammatically correct adult nonfiction—or a shortened Advanced article—for a young child's text.
- **Story / Medium:** written for grades 2–4 independent readers with no assumed Chinese-history background. Use one driving question, problem, journey, decision, object, or documented sequence to create forward motion. Keep names and background under control. “Interesting enough to continue without an adult” is a required acceptance test.
- **Advanced:** written for older elementary readers, families, and teachers. Include evidence, disagreement, historical layers, and interpretation, but keep the story moving and avoid research-paper voice.

Sentence length and readability scores are only warning lights. A short sentence can still sound translated, formal, or unnatural. Native read-aloud rhythm is a separate acceptance requirement.

Use clear, simple, natural American English. Avoid hard words, formal phrasing, textbook-like sentences, and adult academic tone.

Chinese culture already asks children to understand unfamiliar ideas, so the English language should not become another obstacle.

First understand what the accepted level design asks the child to feel, notice, follow, or remember. Then write that experience in natural English for native-speaking children. Do not preserve a phrase, paragraph, section, or information order merely because it appears in the Chinese.

Translate the meaning, mood, and story energy of the Chinese source, not just the words. Keep the article accurate, natural, vivid, and fun to read.

Do not make the English stiff or overly literal in the name of precision. When needed, explain cultural ideas naturally within the sentence.

## Paragraph-Level Check

For each paragraph, ask:

- What is the main idea the child must understand?
- What picture or feeling should stay in the child's mind?
- Would a native English-speaking child at this version's stated level understand this naturally?
- Is any word technically correct but emotionally misleading, too adult, or too abstract?
- Is the sentence graceful, or just simplified?

The goal is not merely "easy English." The goal is readable, vivid, trustworthy English for the specific level.

For Easy, add these whole-article questions:

- Is there exactly one main idea?
- Could a kindergarten listener retell two concrete details with adult prompting?
- Did the draft remove extra arguments rather than merely shorten their sentences?

For Medium, add these whole-article questions:

- What makes the child want to read the next section?
- Does every section advance the same question or story engine?
- Could a child with no knowledge of Chinese dynasties follow it without a preliminary lecture?

## Practical Style Rules

Avoid em dashes in child-facing titles, prose, and figure captions. Prefer commas, full stops, or a naturally rewritten sentence that is easy to read aloud. Apply the same preference to Chinese double dashes, whose long horizontal marks can resemble the character for one. Do not replace them mechanically with shorter dashes; ordinary compound-word hyphens and numeric ranges are unaffected.

Use the familiar word first when a term may be hard for children. For example, prefer `leather or hide armor` before using `hide armor` alone. It is fine to teach the harder word in the article, but do not put an unclear word in a title if a smoother title is available.

Titles should feel polished, clear, and inviting. They do not need to explain every detail. For example, `From Leather to Iron: Armor Through the Dynasties` is better as a title than `From Animal Skin to Iron`.

Prefer natural children's nonfiction phrasing over literal translation. For example, `great heroes often carry weapons people remember right away` is smoother than a dictionary-like phrase such as `heroes have signature weapons`.

Avoid awkward literal images. If a sentence sounds strange in English, rewrite the idea. For example, use `The blade became part of how people picture Guan Yu`, not `The blade became part of Guan Yu`.

Prefer concrete actors when they help children understand the scene. For example, `Officials watched almost everything he did` is clearer than `People watched almost everything he did`.

Avoid generic historical category words when the person or title is known. `Ruler` is normal English when comparing unnamed leaders across places or periods, but it often sounds like a dictionary translation in a child-facing story. Prefer `the emperor`, `the king`, `the khan`, the person's name, or a pronoun when accurate. `Official` means a government worker and is not a synonym for `ruler`.

Avoid words that are technically correct but may create the wrong picture. For example, in a ritual context, `ritual offerings` is safer than `sacrifices`, which may sound alarming or violent to children.

Choose vivid but clear images. A phrase can be beautiful and still too abstract. For example, `a quiet connection to the exam hall` may be clearer than `a quiet shadow behind them`.

Use simple time expressions when they are easier for children. For example, `in the late 1800s` is usually clearer than `at the end of the nineteenth century`.

For articles aimed at U.S. kids, prefer familiar U.S. units in child-facing English. Use `pounds`, `feet`, `inches`, and familiar temperature wording first. If metric matters, put it second or leave it out when it does not help understanding.

Make sound words readable and light. Use forms such as `Tang-hu-lu!`, `To-fu!`, and `weng!` instead of over-marked syllables like `Tang-hu-lu-!`.

Explain cultural terms inside the sentence. For example, `These shop signs were called huangzi` is better than a dictionary-style aside.

Keep useful cultural terms when they matter, but present them gently. Italicize terms such as `*jinshi*` when helpful, then explain them in natural language nearby.

Keep sentences short enough to read aloud easily. When a sentence carries two ideas, split it unless the rhythm clearly works.

Simple words should still have polish. Do not make the English babyish, flat, or choppy just to make it easy.

## Paragraph Integrity

Short sentences do not each need their own paragraph. Group related sentences into a developed prose paragraph that carries one idea, scene, comparison, or chain of action forward.

Do not insert a blank line after every sentence. In the article source, blank lines become separate rendered paragraphs, so sentence-by-sentence breaks make the story look like notes, captions, or free verse instead of a readable article. They also create unnecessary vertical scrolling.

As a flexible default, a paragraph often contains two to five connected sentences. A single-sentence paragraph is appropriate only when it serves a deliberate purpose, such as an opening hook, a turning question, dialogue, a transition, or a moment of emphasis. If several one-sentence paragraphs appear in a row, combine them unless the changing speaker or scene truly requires separation.

English paragraph boundaries do not need to copy the Chinese exactly. Rewrite them according to natural English thought and rhythm while preserving the same meaning and factual boundaries.

## Endings: Stop When the Article Is Finished

Do not add a closing sentence merely to make the article sound like a children's story. If the final scene, fact, or explanation already completes the piece, stop there.

As a default, do not end with manufactured reader prompts such as:

- `The next time you...`
- `Notice...`
- `Ask yourself...`
- `Now you can...`
- `What will you discover?`

Also avoid an unnecessary callback to an opening joke, object, or question. A callback is useful only when it completes the article's actual story or idea; it should not be attached as decoration.

Do not repeat the introduction in summary form or add a large moral after the content has ended. Prefer finishing on the last concrete action, consequence, discovery, or useful new fact.

A reader activity is allowed only when the article is genuinely designed around that activity. It must be specific, worthwhile, safe, and realistic. For example, do not tell a child to compare two families' recipes “at the next meal” when those families would not normally be sitting together.

Final deletion test: remove the last paragraph and reread the article. If nothing important is lost—or if the ending becomes more natural—delete that paragraph.

## Background Names and Glossary Support

Dynasty names, country or people names, regions, book titles, and government titles can be hard for kids even when the sentence itself is smooth. Examples include `Han dynasty`, `Song dynasty`, `Qin`, `Qi`, `Xiongnu`, `Jiangnan`, `Central Plains`, and old job titles.

Do not add long inline explanations everywhere, because that can interrupt the story.

A tooltip must add information the sentence does not already give. Never use a tooltip merely to repeat or rephrase the surrounding sentence.

Use this decision test:

- If the child must know the explanation to understand the sentence, rewrite the explanation into the main text.
- If the term is already explained in the same sentence or paragraph, do not add a tooltip.
- Use a tooltip for optional orientation that gives the child a new anchor: where a place is on a map, roughly when a dynasty existed, what an unfamiliar title allowed someone to do, or how an ancient object was used.
- For geography, prefer a concrete map relationship over circular wording. `A coastal province in eastern China, southeast of Beijing` is useful; `the province where this event happened` is not.
- Mark the first useful occurrence, not every repetition of the term.

Use this rule:

- If the child needs the context immediately to follow the action, add very short inline help. Example: `About 1,000 years ago, in the Song dynasty, cuju became very popular.`
- If the context is useful but optional, keep the sentence flowing and mark the term for a glossary/tooltip with `[[term]]`.
- Glossary explanations should be one child-friendly sentence, not a paragraph.
- The glossary UI should be touch-friendly for iPad and also work on desktop Chrome/Safari. Avoid hover-only notes and native browser `title` tooltips.

Possible glossary examples:

- `Song dynasty`: `A time in China about 1,000 years ago.`
- `Han dynasty`: `A powerful time in China more than 2,000 years ago.`
- `Xiongnu`: `A horse-riding people from the northern grasslands.`
- `Jiangnan`: `The warm, watery region south of the Yangtze River.`
- `Central Plains`: `The old farming heartland of northern China.`

## Recent Calibration: What "Hard to Read" Looks Like

When a paragraph explains evidence, history, or cultural memory, do not make the child climb through adult academic wording before they understand the point.

Prefer a clear action chain:

`history book says stabbed -> stabbing sounds like spear or halberd -> famous curved blade is mostly for chopping -> maybe the story image came later`

This is easier than a paragraph full of terms such as `official history`, `enemy ranks`, `archaeology gives another clue`, `later periods`, or `securely proven`.

For story-to-culture explanations, make the path visible:

`novel -> painters -> opera actors -> temples -> children remember the blade`

This is easier than abstract wording such as `the image mattered more than the question of historical accuracy`.

If the English starts to sound like an encyclopedia explaining a concept, rewrite it into something a child can watch happening. Use concrete verbs such as `rode`, `stabbed`, `chopped`, `painted`, `carried`, `placed`, `saw`, and `remembered`.
