# Lord Guan — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Historical Guan Yu

Source: Chinese Text Project, Chen Shou, *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, “Book of Shu 6,” Biography of Guan Yu
URL: https://ctext.org/sanguozhi/36/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Guan Yu was an early follower of Liu Bei, served as a military commander, was captured and treated well by Cao Cao, defeated Yan Liang, and then returned to Liu Bei.
- The text describes Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei as close “like brothers” but does not record a sworn oath in a peach garden.
- Guan Yu was defeated by Sun Quan’s forces and killed around the transition from the Eastern Han to the Three Kingdoms period.

Source: Barend J. ter Haar, *Guan Yu: The Religious Afterlife of a Failed Hero*, Oxford University Press, preview of Chapter 1
URL: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192525420_A35502593/preview-9780192525420_A35502593.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The *Records of the Three Kingdoms* is the principal historical source for Guan Yu but was written decades later and contains anecdotal material.
- Historical evidence indicates a close relationship among Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei, but does not mention a sworn brotherhood.
- It is useful to distinguish Guan Yu, the historical person, from Lord Guan or Emperor Guan, the later deity.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not present the Peach Garden Oath, passing five gates, the red face, green robe, or Green Dragon Crescent Blade as verified details of Guan Yu’s life.
- It does not invent Guan Yu’s thoughts, private speeches, childhood, or final moments.
- “Around 220 CE” avoids pretending the surviving evidence supplies a precise modern date accepted without qualification.

## The Cult Predated *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*

Source: Barend J. ter Haar, *Guan Yu: The Religious Afterlife of a Failed Hero*, Oxford University Press, Chapter 1 preview and Chapter 2 abstract
URL: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192525420_A35502593/preview-9780192525420_A35502593.pdf
URL: https://academic.oup.com/book/10303/chapter-abstract/158027186
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Lord Guan’s documented divine career reaches back to the second half of the Tang dynasty.
- Worship began in the Jing region near the area where Guan Yu was executed.
- During the Tang, Yuquan Monastery drew the local cult into its Buddhist foundation and protector traditions.
- The religious cult had its own oral and local history and should not be treated as a simple result of the much later novel.

Editorial boundary:

- Accounts of a restless spirit, miraculous appearances, or supernatural protection are described as belief stories. They are evidence for what communities remembered and practiced, not proof that miracles occurred.
- The article says the later novel, theater, and storytelling influenced the cult, not that they were irrelevant. The relationship worked in more than one direction.

## Buddhist and Daoist Layers

Source: Barend J. ter Haar, *Guan Yu: The Religious Afterlife of a Failed Hero*, Oxford University Press
URL: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192525420_A35502593/preview-9780192525420_A35502593.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- A prominent Buddhist monastery adopted Lord Guan as a miraculous monastic supporter during the Tang.
- Daoist ritual specialists recruited him as an exorcistic spirit general during the Song.
- His later roles included rainmaking, protection, moral enforcement, and action against dangerous spiritual forces.

Source: Cambridge University Press, “Knowledge and Ritual: The Dual Nature of the Scripture Illustrating the Holiness of Emperor Guan”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/chinese-popular-religion-in-text-and-acts/knowledge-and-ritual-the-dual-nature-of-the-scripture-illustrating-the-holiness-of-emperor-guan-guandi-mingsheng-jing/C8963AD9DE0DA767D0A2050E4B15D355
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/9789048550364.007
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- In Chinese Buddhism, Lord Guan is honored as a *qielan* protector.
- His status rose within state and popular-religious pantheons, and his ability to fit Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian moral frameworks helped his popularity.

Source: He Liqun, “Buddhist State Monasteries in Early Medieval China,” Heidelberg University
URL: https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/17285/1/heliqun05082014.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Sanskrit term *saṃghārāma*, rendered in Chinese as *qielan*, referred to a garden or grounds for a monastic community’s residence and practice and became a common Chinese synonym for a monastery.

Editorial boundary:

- The story that Guan Yu’s spirit met the monk Zhiyi is labeled a later Buddhist sacred story. Because the two lived centuries apart, it is not presented as a historical meeting.
- The article avoids implying that Buddhism, Daoism, popular religion, and state ritual were a single organization or followed one neat sequence everywhere.
- *Qielan* is distinguished from a *qielan* guardian: the first refers to monastic grounds or a monastery, while the second is a deity who protects such a place. Guan Yu was not the only deity in this category.

## Imperial Titles and the Moral Deity

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Guan Yu,” painting ca. 1700
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/62002
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Wanli emperor elevated Guan Yu to imperial rank, after which Guandi became a major object of Qing imperial patronage.
- Qing rulers erected numerous shrines in his honor.
- A Qing religious painting identifies him with an imperial title and shows how court-supported visual culture presented the deity.

Source: Cambridge University Press, “Knowledge and Ritual: The Dual Nature of the Scripture Illustrating the Holiness of Emperor Guan”
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/chinese-popular-religion-in-text-and-acts/knowledge-and-ritual-the-dual-nature-of-the-scripture-illustrating-the-holiness-of-emperor-guan-guandi-mingsheng-jing/C8963AD9DE0DA767D0A2050E4B15D355
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- His divine titles rose from martial king and demon-quelling emperor to Saintly Imperial Lord Guan.
- During the Ming he came to be promoted as a martial saint and moral counterpart to Confucius.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article summarizes the climb from duke to king to emperor instead of giving a brittle list of every title and date.
- Imperial support is treated as one historical force among many, not the sole creator of the cult or proof that all people believed alike.

## Merchants, Guildhalls, and Trust

Source: “Shanxi Merchant Guildhalls as Religious Sites for Theatrical Performances in Late Imperial China,” *Religions* 17.4
URL: https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/17/4/404
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Many Shanxi merchant guildhalls in the Ming and Qing eras included worship of Emperor Guan and were locally known as Guan temples.
- Shanxi merchants connected the deity with shared regional origin, loyalty, righteousness, group discipline, community ritual, and theater.
- Guildhall worship combined local merchant meanings with official moral interpretations rather than simply copying one state message.

Source: British Museum, “Figure,” Guandi soapstone sculpture
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_SLMisc-1174
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Guandi was widely understood as a symbol of loyalty and trust and became a patron of merchants associated with good faith in business.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says “some merchants and trade groups,” not all merchants everywhere.
- It does not reduce Lord Guan to a magical source of money. Merchant worship is explained through trust, identity, protection, ritual, and fair dealing.
- The imagined shop scene is not assigned an exact date, location, business, or family story.

## Images and Terminology

Source: Smithsonian Institution, “Chinese Opera”
URL: https://www.si.edu/object/chinese-opera%3Ayt_DSrtArWnOb8
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: British Museum, “Shaman Painting (Guan Yu)”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1990-1113-0-6

Supports:

- In common Chinese opera color language, a red face can represent loyalty and bravery.
- Guan Yu’s familiar red face, long beard, and other recognizable visual features belong to later literary and visual traditions rather than a securely documented portrait from his lifetime.

Both retained images are modern illustrations, not records of named historical sites:

- `1-lord-guan-temple-statue.webp`: retains recognizable later iconography, but the giant statue, worshippers, attendants, architecture, clothing, offerings, and ritual arrangement are not treated as one documented temple scene.
- `2-lord-guan-shop-altar.webp`: illustrates the later association between merchants and Lord Guan, but the people, shop, lesson, abacus, account book, altar, and date are imagined.

Editorial boundaries:

- “God of War” is mentioned as a common but overly narrow English label. The article emphasizes Lord Guan’s many protective, exorcistic, moral, local, Buddhist, Daoist, and commercial roles.
- No tooltip is added for “Guan Di temples.” The existing glossary definition merely repeats the phrase and would not add useful context; the idea is explained directly in the article instead.
