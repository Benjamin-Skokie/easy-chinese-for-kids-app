# Lord Guan — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website. The article tells one clear story for ages 6–10; the sources below check the facts but do not set the voice.

## The promise, repayment, and departure

Source: Chen Shou, *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, “Book of Shu 6,” Biography of Guan Yu, Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/sanguozhi/36/zhs
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Guan Yu was captured by Cao Cao, who treated him generously and gave him a military position.
- Guan Yu said that he appreciated Cao Cao’s kindness but would not abandon Liu Bei. He intended to repay Cao Cao before leaving.
- Guan Yu defeated the opposing general Yan Liang, sealed Cao Cao’s gifts, left a farewell letter, and returned to Liu Bei.
- When Cao Cao’s men wanted to pursue Guan Yu, Cao Cao stopped them and said that each man had chosen the leader he would serve.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article leaves out Yan Liang’s name, official titles, place names, and the detailed politics of the battle because they do not help a young reader follow the central choice.
- The dialogue is a short, age-appropriate paraphrase of the historical account, not a claim to reproduce the speakers’ exact words.
- Cao Cao’s thought about trusting someone who keeps an old promise is an explanatory inference. The source records his respect for Guan Yu’s decision, but not that exact private thought.

## From Guan Yu to Lord Guan

Source: Barend J. ter Haar, *Guan Yu: The Religious Afterlife of a Failed Hero*, Oxford University Press
URL: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192525420_A35502593/preview-9780192525420_A35502593.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Guan Yu,” painting, ca. 1700
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/62002
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Guan Yu was a historical general; Lord Guan was the deity gradually created through local worship, religion, storytelling, theater, and official support after his death.
- Lord Guan’s worship developed over many centuries and spread widely.
- The familiar temple figure should not be treated as a portrait of the historical man.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not explain the full Buddhist, Daoist, local, and imperial history to children. It accurately summarizes that people gradually honored Guan Yu as a protective deity and built temples for him.
- The Peach Garden Oath and the Five Passes story are omitted. They are not needed for this article’s central story and should not be presented as facts from Guan Yu’s lifetime.

## Red face, beard, robe, and blade

Source: Smithsonian Institution, “Chinese Opera”
URL: https://www.si.edu/object/chinese-opera%3Ayt_DSrtArWnOb8
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: British Museum, “Shaman Painting (Guan Yu)”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1990-1113-0-6
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- In common Chinese opera color language, a red face can represent loyalty and bravery.
- Guan Yu’s red face, long beard, green robe, and great blade are recognizable features of his later theatrical and religious image.

## Merchants and trust

Source: British Museum, “Figure,” Guandi soapstone sculpture
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_SLMisc-1174
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: “Shanxi Merchant Guildhalls as Religious Sites for Theatrical Performances in Late Imperial China,” *Religions* 17.4
URL: https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/17/4/404
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Guandi became widely associated with loyalty, trust, and good faith in business.
- Some merchants and merchant groups worshipped him as a protector and placed shrines to him in commercial spaces and guildhalls.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says “some merchants” and “some shops,” not all Chinese businesses.
- Lord Guan is not reduced to a magical source of money. The page explains the merchant connection through promises, trust, and keeping agreements.
