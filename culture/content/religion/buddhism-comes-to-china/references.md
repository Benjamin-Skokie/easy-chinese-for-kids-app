# References for “How Did Buddhism Come to China? The White Horse Story”

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website. The child-facing article tells the White Horse story as a legend, then explains Buddhism's wider and longer history in China.

## Current child-facing version

Rewritten: 2026-09-02

- The dream, the two monks, the white horse, and the naming of White Horse Temple are clearly presented as a famous legend rather than a fully verified record.
- Exact years, names of envoys and monks, and debates over the earliest evidence remain here rather than interrupting the story.
- The article does not imply that Buddhism entered China in one trip. It explains that monks, merchants, and other travelers carried Buddhist teachings into China over a long period.
- Page 3 explains how Buddhism slowly took root and gained familiar Chinese forms; Page 4 stays in the present-day White Horse Temple and shows why different visitors still come.
- The article says Buddhism is “one of China's most widespread and influential religious traditions.” It does not give children a misleading population ranking.

## Emperor Ming, the white horse, and White Horse Temple

Source: National Museum of China, “Mirror of the Mind: An Exhibition of Buddhist Sculpture from Bo'xing, Shandong Province”
URL: https://en.chnmuseum.cn/exhibition/past_exhibitions/202010/t20201028_247936.html
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Eric Zürcher, *The Buddhist Conquest of China: The Spread and Adaptation of Buddhism in Early Medieval China*, revised edition
URL: https://brill.com/display/title/6495
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Polina Shmushko, “The Modern White Horse Temple and Online Reconfiguring of a Buddhist Heritage Space”
URL: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/178085865/jdtrea-3-1-109.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- A long-lived tradition connects Emperor Ming's dream of a golden figure with the search for Buddhist teachings.
- Later versions of the story describe two monks arriving at Luoyang with Buddhist writings and an image carried by a white horse.
- White Horse Temple is traditionally said to have been named in memory of that horse and is widely remembered as an early center of Chinese Buddhism.
- White Horse Temple remains both a Buddhist site and a powerful symbol of the religion's arrival in China.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article labels the account a legend before telling it.
- It does not present the traditional dates or every named character as settled historical fact.
- Luoyang is explained only as the Han capital because that is all a young reader needs in order to follow the scene.

## Buddhism's long growth in China

Source: Pew Research Center, “Buddhism in China”
URL: https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/08/30/buddhism/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: British Museum, “Ancient India: Living Traditions — Adaptations in China”
URL: https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/ancient-india-living-traditions/ancient-india-living-traditions-large-print-guide
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Smarthistory, “Buddhism in Chinese Art (2nd century through 907 C.E.)”
URL: https://smarthistory.org/reframing-art-history/buddhism-chinese-art/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Buddhism began in India and reached China during the Han period through monks and travelers from India and Central Asia.
- Over time, Chinese Buddhist traditions blended ideas and artistic forms from India and Central Asia with local Chinese forms.
- Temples, cliff carvings, paintings, sculpture, and stories record Buddhism's deep influence on Chinese culture.
- Dunhuang and Longmen preserve major bodies of Buddhist painting and sculpture.
- Guanyin, associated with compassion, became one of the best-known Buddhist figures in China.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing article uses a few concrete examples rather than giving a timeline of Buddhist schools or dynasties.
- *Journey to the West* and Shaolin Temple are included as familiar links to other articles in this collection, not as a full history of Chinese Buddhism.

## Buddhism in China today

Source: Pew Research Center, “6 Facts About Buddhism in China”
URL: https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/09/21/6-facts-about-buddhism-in-china/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Pew Research Center, “Measuring Religion in China”
URL: https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/08/30/measuring-religion-in-china/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Asian Art Museum, “Bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara (Chinese: Guanyin)”
URL: https://education.asianart.org/resources/bodhisattva-avalokiteshvara-chinese-guanyin-618-906/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Buddhism is the largest officially recognized religion in China, but formal identity captures only part of its reach.
- About 4% of Chinese adults formally identified as Buddhist in the cited 2018 survey, while about one-third said they believed in Buddha and/or a bodhisattva.
- Many people take part in Buddhist beliefs or practices without calling themselves Buddhist, and Chinese religious traditions often overlap.
- Guanyin became one of the most popular Buddhist figures in China.

Editorial boundary:

- Survey percentages are not placed in the child-facing story because they would interrupt the narrative and can create a false sense of precision.
- The article does not say that every temple visit, incense offering, vegetarian dish, or figure of Guanyin means the person involved formally identifies as Buddhist.
- The article uses “one of China's most widespread and influential religious traditions” because that is accurate across several different ways of measuring religious life.
