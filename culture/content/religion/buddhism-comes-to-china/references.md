# More Than One White Horse — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## A Dated First-Century Record

Source: *Book of the Later Han*, “Biography of Liu Ying, Prince of Chu,” text preserved through the Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=592401&if=en
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: “Chinese Religion: An Overview,” *Encyclopedia of Religion*
URL: https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/chinese-religion-overview
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- An order dated 65 CE says that Liu Ying, Prince of Chu, recited Huang-Lao teachings and honored Buddhist rites.
- The source mentions Buddhist lay devotees and renunciants associated with his circle.
- Scholarship commonly treats this as the earliest reliable dated notice of Buddhist practice in a Chinese source.

Editorial boundary:

- The record shows that Buddhist ideas and practitioners were present by 65 CE; it does not give a date when Buddhism first crossed into China.
- It also shows that some early practice overlapped with categories used for Huang-Lao and other local traditions.
- The *Book of the Later Han* was compiled by Fan Ye in the fifth century, though it preserves earlier records. It is not itself a first-century manuscript.

## Emperor Ming's Dream as a Received Tradition

Source: *Book of the Later Han*, “Treatise on the Western Regions,” Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/hou-han-shu/xi-yu-zhuan/zh
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Duan Qingbo, “Sino-Western Cultural Exchange as Seen through the Archaeology of the First Emperor's Necropolis,” *Journal of Chinese History*
URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-chinese-history/article/sinowestern-cultural-exchange-as-seen-through-the-archaeology-of-the-first-emperors-necropolis/3F6506262A9ED57C9DC3827EEAA6B230
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The *Book of the Later Han* introduces Emperor Ming's golden-man dream with 世傳, “tradition says.”
- Its received passage says the emperor sent people toward Tianzhu to ask about Buddhist teaching, after which images and pictures appeared in China.
- Modern scholarship does not agree on one exact date, route, or event that should be called the introduction of Buddhism to China.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not present the dream as a contemporary palace record.
- The *Book of the Later Han* passage does not itself supply the complete modern package of two named monks, a white horse carrying scriptures, and a temple founded in exactly 68 CE.
- The article may tell the dream as an influential traditional account only when the source and its late compilation are made clear.

## White Horse Temple, the Two Monks, and the Date 68

Source: Eric Zürcher, *The Buddhist Conquest of China: The Spread and Adaptation of Buddhism in Early Medieval China*, revised edition, chapter on Han Buddhism
URL: https://brill.com/display/title/6495
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Tsukamoto Zenryu, *A History of Early Chinese Buddhism*, supplementary English text in the Kyoto University Research Information Repository
URL: https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/bitstream/2433/139089/10/YunKang_v16_suppl_text.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Later Buddhist biographies and temple traditions connect She Moteng, Zhu Falan, the *Sutra in Forty-Two Sections*, and White Horse Temple to Emperor Ming's dream.
- The earliest surviving reference that securely names a White Horse Temple at Luoyang occurs in translation colophons dated 289 CE.
- A White Horse Temple was an important Luoyang Buddhist site by later centuries.

Editorial boundary:

- The traditional founding date of 68 CE is not confirmed by contemporary first-century evidence.
- Biographies of She Moteng and Zhu Falan cannot be read at face value as contemporary records.
- Do not use the current temple or its later structures as material proof of an Eastern Han foundation.
- The first illustration is a modern imagined scene; its clothing, city gate, monks, white horse, and scroll bundle are not a researched reconstruction.

## Central Asian Translators and Translation Teams

Source: Haun Saussy, “The First Age of Translation,” in *Translation as Citation: Zhuangzi Inside Out*, Oxford University Press
URL: https://academic.oup.com/book/25502/chapter-abstract/192724579
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Christoph Anderl, Bart Dessein, and Ann Heirman, “Buddhism,” *Oxford Bibliographies in Chinese Studies*
URL: https://academic.oup.com/reference/62338/reference-article-abstract/554177590
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Paul Harrison, “An Shigao,” *Encyclopedia of Buddhism*
URL: https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/shigao
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The first surviving translations of Indic Buddhist texts into Chinese were made in the second century CE.
- An Shigao, from the Parthian world, was active at Luoyang roughly from 148 to 180 and became a major early translator of meditation and doctrinal texts.
- Early transmission initially depended heavily on Central Asian Buddhists and languages.
- Translation frequently involved spoken explanation, relay, and Chinese scribal composition rather than one bilingual person silently replacing each word.

Editorial boundary:

- Many details of An Shigao's life are traditional or disputed; the article uses only the well-supported identity, approximate dates, place, and translation role.
- Do not say all early scriptures traveled directly from India in Sanskrit or that every translator was Indian.
- Do not describe translation as a simple one-way process in which a fixed text merely received Chinese wording.

## Many Routes and Continuing Exchange

Source: UNESCO, “About the Silk Roads”
URL: https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-roads/about-silk-roads?hub=196704
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Silk Roads: the Routes Network of Chang'an–Tianshan Corridor”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The Silk Roads were a changing network of multiple land and maritime routes, not one road.
- Merchants, monks, pilgrims, and envoys moved religious ideas, objects, and practices through this network.
- Buddhist sites from Central Asia into China record long-term movement and local patronage.

Editorial boundary:

- “Silk Roads” is a modern collective label and must not be drawn as one precise itinerary used by every early Buddhist traveler.
- The article may say maritime links later joined China to a broader Buddhist world. It does not claim that a specific sea voyage marks Buddhism's first entry into China.
- Avoid the image of two monks carrying a complete religion straight from India to Luoyang without intermediaries.

## Guanyin and Dunhuang: Change Without a Single “Chinese Shape”

Source: Denise Patry Leidy, “Chinese Buddhist Sculpture,” Metropolitan Museum of Art
URL: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/chinese-buddhist-sculpture
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Getty Research Institute, “Cave Temples of Dunhuang: Buddhist Art on China's Silk Road”
URL: https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/cave_temples_dunhuang/gallery.html
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Guanyin/Avalokiteshvara can manifest in multiple forms; female forms became important in China, while the tradition did not simply discard every male or non-fixed form.
- Chinese Buddhist art records repeated encounters with Indian and Central Asian practices and imagery.
- Dunhuang developed over roughly a millennium and preserves intense religious, commercial, artistic, and linguistic exchange.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not say Guanyin changed once and completely from an Indian man into a Chinese woman.
- Do not describe Dunhuang as a timeline in which “foreign” features steadily disappeared and “Chinese” features steadily replaced them.
- Chinese Buddhist traditions developed locally while remaining connected to other Buddhist regions. “Adaptation” did not end transregional exchange.

## Present-Day White Horse Temple and Existing Images

Source: Polina Shmushko, “The Modern White Horse Temple and Online Reconfiguring of a Buddhist Heritage Space,” *Journal of Daesoon Thought and the Religions of East Asia* 3.1
URL: https://www.accesson.kr/jdtrea/v.3/1/109/38840
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- White Horse Temple remains a major modern heritage and religious site whose public meaning draws heavily on the traditional first-temple narrative.
- Modern presentations of the temple continue to reinterpret and circulate that origin story.

Editorial boundary:

- The historical significance of today's site includes its long cultural memory; it is not limited to proof of a first-century building.
- 1-white-horse-arrival.webp is retained only as a modern visualization of the received legend. It is not a historical reconstruction.
- 2-white-horse-temple-today.webp is retained only as a modern concept illustration. It is not a documentary photograph, and its buildings, stone horse, visitors, trees, and incense burner must not be treated as a site survey.
