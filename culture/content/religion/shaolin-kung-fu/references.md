# References for “How Did Kung Fu Come to Shaolin Temple?”

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website. The child-facing article follows the growth of Shaolin’s martial reputation without turning the story into a timeline.

## Current child-facing version

Rewritten: 2026-09-02

- The central question is why people around the world connect Shaolin Temple with exceptional martial skill.
- Li Shimin is retained because his later importance makes the monks’ decision meaningful to young readers.
- Exact years, battle names, official documents, and lists of monks remain in this file rather than interrupting the story.
- The article separates the early military actions of Shaolin monks, the later fame of staff fighting, and the still later development of sophisticated boxing.
- It does not claim that every Shaolin monk practiced martial arts or that one person invented kung fu.

## Shaolin as a Buddhist monastery

Source: People’s Government of Henan Province, “Shaolin Temple”
URL: https://english.henan.gov.cn/2019/10-16/988665.html
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Historic Monuments of Dengfeng in ‘The Centre of Heaven and Earth’”
URL: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1305/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Shaolin Temple was founded in 495 at the foot of Mount Song.
- The temple was established for the Indian Buddhist monk Batuo, also known as Buddhabhadra, and was first a place of Buddhist teaching and monastic life.
- Shaolin remains a Buddhist monastery and forms part of the historic monuments of Dengfeng.

Editorial boundary:

- The article says “long ago” rather than requiring children to remember 495 or the Northern Wei dynasty.
- Batuo’s name is omitted because it is not needed to answer the article’s central question.

## Li Shimin and the thirteen monks

Source: Meir Shahar, *The Shaolin Monastery: History, Religion, and the Chinese Martial Arts*, University of Hawai‘i Press
URL: https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/the-shaolin-monastery-history-religion-and-the-chinese-martial-arts/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Tōnami Mamoru, *The Shaolin Monastery Stele on Mount Song*, summary and bibliographic record, Persée
URL: https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_1993_num_80_1_2226
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- The Shaolin Monastery Stele erected in 728 preserves documents connected with the monastery’s assistance to Li Shimin in 621.
- Thirteen monks are named for meritorious service.
- The monks helped Li Shimin’s side against Wang Shichong’s forces, captured Wang Renze, and recovered the Cypress Valley estate claimed by the monastery.
- Li Shimin later became Emperor Taizong of the Tang and rewarded or confirmed the monastery’s property.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not repeat the later popular claim that thirteen staff-wielding monks personally “rescued Emperor Taizong.” Li Shimin was still a prince, and the surviving record supports military assistance rather than the movie-like rescue story.
- Wang Shichong, Wang Renze, Cypress Valley, the Battle of Hulao, 621, and 728 are omitted from the child-facing text.
- The article does not claim that the stele proves the monks already practiced a complete Shaolin martial-arts system.

## Staff fighting and later boxing

Source: Meir Shahar, *The Shaolin Monastery: History, Religion, and the Chinese Martial Arts*, University of Hawai‘i Press
URL: https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/the-shaolin-monastery-history-religion-and-the-chinese-martial-arts/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Meir Shahar, “Hand Combat,” Oxford Academic / Hawai‘i Scholarship Online
URL: https://academic.oup.com/hawaii-scholarship-online/book/19612/chapter-abstract/178357584
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Source: Meir Shahar, “Epigraphy, Buddhist Historiography, and Fighting Monks: The Case of the Shaolin Monastery,” Academia Sinica
URL: https://www11.ihp.sinica.edu.tw/storage/w2_file/1508BgfeJrK.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Shaolin fighting monks are documented from the Tang period onward, but this does not mean that a complete modern kung fu system existed from the monastery’s beginning.
- By the sixteenth century, Shaolin monks had become famous for staff fighting.
- Sophisticated empty-handed Shaolin techniques developed later, especially during the seventeenth century; boxing eventually became more prominent than the staff.
- Shaolin martial practice developed over centuries through military, religious, physical-training, and cultural influences.
- Buddhist teaching forbids taking life, and monastic rules also forbade carrying weapons or joining an army, even though fighting monks sometimes broke those rules.
- A staff has no cutting edge, so it can block, push, or disarm without using a sword. This made it easier to connect staff practice with restraint, but it did not make the staff harmless.
- Historical Shaolin fighters also used bladed weapons, and a wooden or iron staff could seriously injure or kill. The popular “peaceful monk chooses a blunt staff” explanation is therefore not a complete history of why Shaolin staff fighting became famous.

Editorial boundary:

- The article uses “hundreds of years later” rather than lists of dynasties and dates.
- It does not repeat the later legend that Bodhidharma invented Shaolin kung fu after seeing weak monks. That story is unnecessary to the child-facing historical line.
- The motions and sounds used to describe staff practice are illustrative story details, not a reconstruction of one documented lesson.
- The child-facing article says the staff gave a monk more ways to protect without *trying* to kill; it does not claim that a staff cannot kill or that Shaolin monks never used swords and other weapons.

## Stories, theater, and movies

Source: Meir Shahar, *The Shaolin Monastery: History, Religion, and the Chinese Martial Arts*, University of Hawai‘i Press
URL: https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/the-shaolin-monastery-history-religion-and-the-chinese-martial-arts/
Accessed: 2026-09-02

Supports:

- Historical records, religious stories, martial legends, literature, and modern popular culture all contributed to Shaolin’s world-famous martial identity.
- The popular image should not be treated as proof that every monk was a fighter or that movie feats are documentary records of temple life.

Editorial boundary:

- The ending acknowledges movie exaggeration while preserving the genuine historical connection between the monastery and martial practice.
- The article does not turn the final page into a lecture about separating fact from fiction.
