# Yin and Yang Shared Story and Facts Brief

## Core ideas and evidence

1. Early uses of yin and yang described observable conditions such as the shady and sunny sides of a hill. Actual sunlight varies with season, weather, time, and terrain.
2. Yin and yang are relational and change with context. They are not permanent boxes, good versus evil, or scientifically measurable substances.
3. Later thinkers used the pair to discuss alternation and change. Sun and moon are symbols; Earth's rotation causes day and night, and moonlight is reflected sunlight.
4. The familiar black-and-white taiji diagram developed much later than early yin-yang thought. Modern readings of its curves and dots should be labeled as readings, not one proven original meaning.
5. Balance does not always mean equal halves, and harmony does not erase difference.
6. Go and a simple weight shift are modern examples. They do not prove the origins of Go or create permanent yin/yang labels for stones, feet, or movements.
7. Historical gender associations must not become rules about a modern person's character, abilities, or future.

## Boundaries

- Do not explain yin and yang as magic energy, scientific particles, or good and evil.
- Do not claim the familiar symbol created the philosophy.
- Keep astronomy and philosophy clearly separate.
- Do not make fixed assignments such as black always equals yin or left foot always equals yin.

## Level assignments

- Early Reader: begin with one mountain, then use day/rest and a simple weight shift to show difference, connection, and change.
- Story: add early textual history, the later diagram, careful balance, Go, Tai Chi, and the warning against labels.
- Advanced: preserve all evidence, cautions, scientific distinctions, and interpretive limits.
