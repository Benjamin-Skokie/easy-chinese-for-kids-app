# Yin and Yang — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Early Meanings: Light and Shade on a Mountain

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Yinyang (Yin-yang)”
URL: https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Early uses of *yīn* and *yáng* described observable contrasts such as shade and sunlight.
- The article cites an early *Book of Songs* passage about examining a hill’s yīn and yáng while looking at flowing springs.

Editorial boundary:

- The mountain walk is a child-facing illustration, not a reconstruction of a particular historical scene.
- The article does not turn the old geographic usage into a rigid rule for every mountain, place, or time of day.

## Relational Patterns and Change

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Metaphysics in Chinese Philosophy”
URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-metaphysics/
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- Yīn and yáng developed from concrete contrasts into relational categories used to describe interconnected and changing patterns.
- Recurring pairs such as light and dark, warmth and cold, movement and rest, and day and night can illustrate this relationship.

Editorial boundary:

- The article presents yīn and yáng as changing relationships rather than permanent labels attached to objects.
- The examples involving rocks, seasons, movement, and rest are explanations for children, not quotations from one ancient text.

## “One Yīn and One Yáng”

Source: *Book of Changes*, “Appended Statements,” Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=en&node=46918&show=parallel
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- The received text contains the line “一陰一陽之謂道.”

Editorial boundary:

- “One yīn and one yáng—this is called the Way” is a concise teaching translation, not presented as the only possible scholarly translation.

## The Later Yinyang Symbol

Source: Columbia University, Asia for Educators, “The Yinyang Symbol”
URL: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/cosmos/bgov/cosmos.htm
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- The familiar circular diagram developed much later than the early idea of yīn and yáng.
- Circular diagrams became especially important from the Song dynasty onward.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim one exact invention date for every form of the diagram.
- It distinguishes the age of the underlying concept from the later history of the familiar image.

## The Taiji Diagram as an Image of Connection

Source: Art Institute of Chicago, *Taoism and the Arts of China*, “Taiji”
URL: https://archive.artic.edu/taoism/glossary/taiji.php
Accessed: 2026-09-06

Supports:

- The familiar taiji symbol is a later image for the connected, turning relationship between yīn and yáng.
- Its two curved halves and opposite-colored dots visually suggest that the two sides remain related rather than sealed apart.

Editorial boundary:

- The explanation of the curves and dots is a child-facing reading of the image, not a claim that every visual feature has one uncontested original meaning.
