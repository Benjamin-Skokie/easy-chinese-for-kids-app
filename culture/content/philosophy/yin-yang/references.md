# Yin and Yang — References and Editorial Notes

This file is for editorial research and does not render on the website.

## Early Meanings: Sunny and Shady Sides of a Hill

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Yinyang (Yin-yang)”
URL: https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Early uses of *yin* and *yang* described observable conditions such as shade and sunlight.
- In early geographic usage, the north side of a mountain was associated with yin and the south side with yang.
- Yin and yang later developed into a philosophical vocabulary of interaction, waxing and waning, and dynamic harmonization.

Source: *Book of Songs*, “Gong Liu,” Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/book-of-poetry/gong-liu
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The ancient line “既景迺岡、相其陰陽、觀其流泉,” in which people survey a ridge’s light and shade and observe flowing springs.

Editorial boundary:

- The opening mountain walk is an explicitly imagined observation for present-day readers, not a reconstructed ancient scene.
- The article says “in many parts of China” and “often,” rather than turning the mountain-south/mountain-north pattern into a rule without exceptions. Season, latitude, weather, and terrain affect actual sunlight.
- Avoid explanations based only on the modern written shapes of 陰 and 陽. The historical development of the characters is more complex than a visual story about their present components.

## Yin and Yang as Relational Categories

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Philosophy in Han Dynasty China”
URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/han-dynasty/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Yin and yang are better described as complementary and oppositional qualities or categories than as two substances.
- Early Chinese texts use the pair to understand alternation and change; the two usually receive balanced priority.

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Gender in Chinese Philosophy”
URL: https://iep.utm.edu/gender-in-chinese-philosophy/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The earliest meanings of yin and yang were not originally fixed definitions of gender.
- Yin-yang categories indicate relationships within a context rather than permanent qualities of individual objects.
- Traditional gender associations exist, but the pairings are historically complex and not fixed in every context.

Editorial boundary:

- Do not translate yin and yang as good and evil.
- Do not present people, genders, personalities, foods, or activities as permanently “yin” or permanently “yang.”
- The lamp, daily schedule, clothing, and orchestra passages are modern explanatory examples, not claims made by one ancient text.

## The *Book of Changes* and Alternation

Source: *Book of Changes*, “Xi Ci I,” section 5, Chinese Text Project
URL: https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=en&node=46918&show=parallel
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- The received text includes the line “一陰一陽之謂道.”

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Chinese Philosophy of Change (Yijing)”
URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Later interpretation of the *Yijing* uses yin and yang to think about patterns of change and their mixtures.

Editorial boundary:

- The English rendering “One yin and one yang—this is called the Way” is a concise teaching translation, not presented as the only possible scholarly translation.
- The article does not identify yin and yang with scientifically measurable fluids, particles, or modern forms of energy.

## Day, Night, Sun, and Moon

Source: NASA / Astronomical Society of the Pacific, “Does the Moon Rotate?” activity
URL: https://nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov/docs/MoonRotate.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: NASA Science, “Top Moon Questions”
URL: https://science.nasa.gov/moon/top-moon-questions/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: NASA Science, “Moonlight”
URL: https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moonlight/
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Earth’s rotation causes the alternation of day and night on Earth.
- The Moon is visible during daytime as well as at night.
- The Moon does not produce its own visible light; moonlight is reflected sunlight.

Editorial boundary:

- Sun and moon are presented as later symbolic associations, not as the physical causes of day and night.
- The first illustration combines sun, moon, light, and shade artistically. Its caption explicitly says that it is not a literal astronomical scene.

## The Familiar Taiji Diagram Is Later than the Idea

Source: François Louis, “The Genesis of an Icon: The ‘Taiji’ Diagram’s Early History,” *Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies* 63.1 (2003), pp. 145–196
URL: https://www.biroco.com/yijing/louis_taiji_diagram.pdf
Stable record: https://www.jstor.org/stable/25066694
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- “Taiji diagram” has referred to multiple diagrams rather than one timeless design.
- Diagram traditions are much later than the early textual use of yin and yang.
- The familiar interlocking swirl developed through a later graphic history; the article’s textual evidence for combining the dynamic circle with the trigram ring points to the fourteenth century, while readily available material evidence discussed there begins later.

Editorial boundary:

- The child-facing text avoids assigning one exact invention date to the familiar icon because different related designs and uses developed over time.
- “The dots can remind us…” is labeled as a useful modern reading, not claimed as the one original meaning intended by an identifiable inventor.

## Go as a Modern Analogy

Source: British Go Association, “Rules of Play”
URL: https://www.britgo.org/files/rules/rulesofplayfull.pdf
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: British Go Association, “How to Play Go”
URL: https://www.britgo.org/howtoplay
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Go uses black and white stones; players ordinarily alternate placing stones and aim to surround territory.

Editorial boundary:

- The article does not claim that Go’s rules originated from yin-yang philosophy.
- It does not permanently assign black to yin and white to yang or reduce every move to attack/yang and defense/yin.
- Statements about a group changing from strong to threatened are explanatory observations about gameplay, not quotations from an ancient source.

## Tai Chi and Shifting Weight

Source: U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, “Tai Chi: What You Need To Know”
URL: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/tai-chi-what-you-need-to-know
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Source: Harvard Health Publishing, “Best exercise for balance: Tai chi”
URL: https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/best-exercise-for-balance-tai-chi
Accessed: 2026-08-20

Supports:

- Tai Chi practice commonly includes slow, gentle, flowing movements and controlled breathing.
- Practice involves shifting weight while maintaining balance.

Editorial boundary:

- The standing weight-shift is a simple modern experience offered to illustrate changing relationships. It is not presented as a complete Tai Chi form or ancient exercise.
- The article avoids fixed equations such as left foot = yin, right foot = yang, inhale = yin, or exhale = yang.
