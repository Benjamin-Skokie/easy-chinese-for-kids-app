[section]
page: 1
layout: two-column
heading_zh: 山路尽头的传说
heading_en: A Legend at the Mountain Pass
image: 3-1-laozi-ox-hangu-pass.webp
caption_zh: 骑青牛、伴着紫气的老子，是后来传说中很有名的形象。
caption_en: Laozi riding an ox beneath purple clouds is a famous image from later legend.
[zh]
想象一位老人沿着山路来到关口。过了这里，道路继续向西。几百年后，历史学家司马迁记录了一则传说：守关人尹喜请老人离开前写下自己的思想。老人写了五千多字，然后出关，从此不知去了哪里。

传说中的老人是老子，书后来被称为《道德经》。画家常让老子骑着青牛，身后飘着紫气。不过，这些画面主要来自后来的传说，不是可以确认的历史记录。

司马迁自己也记下了不止一种老子身份。今天，学者仍在讨论老子是不是一个可以确定的人，以及《道德经》是不是由一个人一次写成的。
[/zh]
[en]
Picture an old man arriving at a mountain pass. Beyond the gate, the road continues west. Centuries later, the historian Sima Qian recorded a traditional story about this traveler. The gatekeeper, Yin Xi, asked him to write down his ideas before leaving. The old man wrote more than 5,000 Chinese characters, passed through the gate, and disappeared from the story.

The traveler was Laozi, tradition says, and the book became known as the *Dao De Jing*. Later artists often placed Laozi on an ox with purple clouds behind him. Those details make a wonderful picture, but they come mainly from later legend, not from history we can verify.

Even Sima Qian listed more than one possible identity for Laozi. Scholars still debate whether Laozi was one identifiable person and whether one person wrote the entire book.
[/en]
[/section]

[section]
page: 2
layout: two-column reverse
heading_zh: 水为什么不争？
heading_en: Why Doesn't Water Compete?
image: 3-2-water-around-stones.webp
caption_zh: 水遇到石头时分开，绕过去以后又继续向前。
caption_en: Water divides around a rock, joins together again, and keeps moving.
[zh]
把手伸进浅浅的溪水。水从指缝间滑过。遇到石头，它分成两股，绕过去再合起来。它没有停下来和石头较劲，却一直在找路。

《道德经》第八章说：“上善若水。水善利万物而不争。”意思大致是，最好的善像水。水帮助万物，却不急着和它们争。

雨水让种子发芽，溪流让动物饮水，人也离不开水。水还总往低处流。老子借着水问人：一个人不抢最高的位置，也可以带来很大的帮助吗？
[/zh]
[en]
Put your hand in a shallow stream. Water slips between your fingers. When it meets a rock, it divides, flows around both sides, and joins again. It does not stop to fight the rock. It keeps finding a way.

Chapter 8 of the *Dao De Jing* says, “The highest good is like water. Water benefits all things and does not compete.” Rain helps seeds grow. Streams give animals a place to drink. Human life depends on water too.

Water also flows toward low places instead of struggling to stand above everything else. Laozi uses that image to ask a human question: Can a person do great good without always fighting for the highest place?
[/en]
[/section]

[section]
page: 3
layout: text-only
heading_zh: 不争不是放弃
heading_en: Not Competing Is Not Giving Up
[zh]
“不争”不是说比赛时不能努力，也不是说被欺负时只能沉默。它反对的是总想压过别人、抢走功劳、证明自己最聪明的冲动。

想象一个小组要造纸桥。一个人抢走材料，不停发号施令，最后谁也做不好。另一个人观察纸怎样弯，听大家的想法，再把不同办法接起来。第二个人没有放弃行动，只是没有用争抢控制所有人。

这是今天的例子，不是《道德经》里的课堂故事。它帮助我们看见：水绕过石头不是投降。改变方向，也可以继续向前。
[/zh]
[en]
“Not competing” does not mean that you cannot try hard in a contest. It does not mean staying silent when someone mistreats you. It warns against the constant need to push others down, grab the credit, or prove that you are the smartest person in the room.

Imagine a group building a paper bridge. One person grabs the supplies and shouts orders until no one can work. Another studies how the paper bends, listens to the group, and helps connect their best ideas. The second person has not stopped acting. They simply are not fighting to control everyone.

This is a modern example, not a classroom scene from the *Dao De Jing*. It helps us see that water has not surrendered when it goes around a rock. Changing direction can be another way to keep moving.
[/en]
[/section]

[section]
page: 4
layout: two-column
heading_zh: 柔软也有力量
heading_en: Softness Can Be Strong
image: 3-3-water-drop-stone.webp
caption_zh: 水流、沙粒和漫长的时间会慢慢磨圆石头。
caption_en: Flowing water, moving sand, and time can slowly round a stone.
[zh]
《道德经》第七十八章又写到水。水看起来柔弱，面对坚硬的东西却有自己的力量。

一滴水推不动巨石。可是河水带着沙粒年年流过，会把尖石慢慢磨圆。水也可能汇成强大的急流。柔软和有力量，并不一定互相矛盾。

能够改变方向，有时比一碰就折断更有用；退一步，有时能看见整个问题；等一等再说话，有时能听见重要的声音。不过，这不是万能规则。洪水会伤人，沉默也不总是正确。哲学给我们多一种可能，不是给所有问题同一个答案。
[/zh]
[en]
Chapter 78 of the *Dao De Jing* returns to water. Water looks soft and weak, yet it can meet hard things with a power of its own.

One drop cannot push away a boulder. A river, however, carries sand and moves year after year. Slowly, sharp stones become round. Water can also gather into a dangerous current. Softness and strength are not always opposites.

Changing direction can sometimes work better than snapping. Stepping back may help you see the whole problem. Waiting before you speak may help you hear what matters. None of these is a rule for every moment. Floods harm people, and silence is not always right. Philosophy offers another possibility to consider, not one answer to every question.
[/en]
[/section]

[section]
page: 5
layout: two-column reverse
heading_zh: “道”和“无为”
heading_en: The Dao and *Wuwei*
image: 3-4-writing-dao-de-jing.webp
caption_zh: 《道德经》常用熟悉的事物，把读者带到不容易回答的问题旁边。
caption_en: The *Dao De Jing* uses familiar things to lead readers toward difficult questions.
[zh]
“道”本来可以指道路。在《道德经》里，它也用来思考万物怎样出现、变化和联系。书一开头就提醒我们：能用一句话完全说清的“道”，还不是完整的道。

另一个词“无为”也常被误会。它不是躺着等工作自己完成，而是少用不必要的强迫和控制。

鞋带打成死结时，越用力拉可能越紧。先停一下，看清绳子怎样缠住，再轻轻松开，反而更快。这是现代例子，却能帮助我们理解：有效的行动不一定很吵，也不一定要用最大的力气。
[/zh]
[en]
The Chinese word *dao* can mean a road or a way. In the *Dao De Jing*, it also opens a much larger question: How do things begin, change, and connect? The book warns us in its first line that no short explanation can capture the whole Dao.

Another often-misunderstood idea is *wuwei*. It does not mean lying down and waiting for every job to finish itself. It points away from needless force and too much control.

Think of a shoelace pulled into a hard knot. Yanking both ends may tighten it. If you stop, study the loops, and gently loosen the right one, you may finish faster. This modern example shows how effective action can be quiet and carefully fitted to the problem.
[/en]
[/section]

[ending]
heading_zh: 水把问题留下来
heading_en: Water Leaves Us with Questions
[zh]
我们不能确定历史上的老子是谁，也不能确定《道德经》的每一句都来自同一个人。不同的早期竹简版本也不完全一样，说明这本书经历了形成和整理。

可是，两千多年来，人们一直回到这本短短的书，思考力量、欲望、领导和生活。下一次看见水绕过石头，可以问：我是在解决问题，还是只想赢？有没有一条更安静、却仍然向前的路？
[/zh]
[en]
We cannot be certain who the historical Laozi was or that every line of the *Dao De Jing* came from one person. Early bamboo manuscripts also differ from the later version, showing that the book took shape over time.

Yet for more than two thousand years, readers have returned to this short text to think about power, desire, leadership, and daily life. The next time you see water moving around a rock, ask: Am I solving the problem, or do I only want to win? Is there a quieter path that still moves forward?
[/en]
[/ending]
